# Routines that come with Prima

Prima starts with twelve routines of yours, put in once (`Prima.Core/Routines/StandardRoutines.cs`): a day for each level, a
quick warm-up, two concert warm-ups, five with one focus each, and a light day. Each is made only of exercises and drills in
the catalogue, and can be changed or taken away like any routine of yours; one taken away doesn't come back. The owner asked
for them on 24 November 2026; this is the research they were built on, read that day, building on
[exercises.md](exercises.md) and [research.md](research.md). Everything here is summarized in my own words.

## What the sources agree on

**How long, and what's in it.** No standard exists, but the figures overlap: about 15–30 minutes a day for beginners, 45–60
for intermediates and an hour or more for advanced players (a concert pianist's tutoring guide; Pianote's survey of over a
thousand players). Pieces always take the largest share: Spanswick's hour is 5 minutes of warm-up, 10 of reading, 10 of
technique and 35 of pieces; Palmer's 70 minutes in *Pianist* give 10 to warming up, 20 to scales and arpeggios, 20 to pieces
and 10 to reading. A routine can't hold a piece, so each daily routine is the part of a session that isn't pieces, and its
line says what follows it.

**By level.** Beginners: sitting and hand shape, five-finger patterns, the first chord shapes, notes and rhythm, technique
kept short; adult methods bring arpeggios in late, so the beginner day walks chord shapes instead. Intermediates: scales,
arpeggios and cadences moved through the keys over a week or a month, the hands working apart, and the left hand's figures
(DiPaolo's lesson order: a chromatic warm-up, scales, cadences, arpeggios). Advanced: technique shrinks beside the pieces and
comes from them (DiPaolo); Chang rejects the traditional half hour of scales and Hanon first; a fluent reader can give
reading's time to technique (Spanswick).

**Reading** grows with the level, to at least 5, 10 and 15 minutes a day (Fonsny), on music a grade or two easier, in time
and without stopping. Lots of easy music read in time matters most (Mishra 2014).

**One focus at a time.**

- Evenness: a slow pulse of 60, then two and four notes to the beat, and a few attentive runs played clean (Estrin, Fitch),
  with contrary motion and chromatic, as ABRSM asks.
- Hand independence: one difference at a time over a simple pattern, touch first, then rhythm, two against one before two
  against three (PianoTV, Pianote, Trinity).
- Chords and jumps: shapes walked and turned (RCM), cadences in every key (DiPaolo); for jumps, arrive early, keep what you
  land on, shrink the pause, mix the distances (Fitch, Estrin, Bangert 2014).
- Touch: one note soft to loud with the arm's weight, then a swell (*Pianist*); the pedal changed just after the new chord,
  listening for gaps and blur (Morin).
- Finger independence is disputed and [exercises.md](exercises.md) keeps it short, so it's two or three light minutes in the
  daily routines rather than a routine of its own.

**The day of a concert.** Little practice, nothing new, never faster than you'll play, then each piece once near its tempo,
once medium and once slow, and stop (Chang). Big muscles first, arm weight and moving across the keyboard, then the fingers,
slowly (Han, tonebase). Try the piano and the bench, slowly and without pedal (Estrin). Warm the body away from the piano, then
gently at it; a warm-up isn't stretching (BAPAM). The same routine every time, rehearsed beforehand, shorter when time is short
(Latała, *The Strad*).

**Light days.** Alternate heavy and light days: light ones shorter, with more breaks, on details; about a day for muscles to
recover, and every fourth week lighter. Months above the usual load with no recovery are the common road to overuse (the
Uniarts planning guide).

## The safety limits they keep

- No technical activity over five minutes, most two or three. Hanon gets three minutes, played quietly: a loud Hanon loop
  brought trained pianists to high exertion in about three and a half minutes (Goubault 2021).
- At most 12–15 minutes at the keys before a minute or two away from them (Shake out, Shoulders down, Tension check); the long
  routines end on reading drills, the lightest load.
- Every routine with technique starts away from the keys, then a slow, soft exercise; stretches come only at the end (BAPAM).
- Octaves only in the advanced day, two quiet minutes, and only for a hand that spans one without stretching; chords close,
  with rests. In one study, 74% of overuse cases tied to a technique began on octaves or chords (Sakai 2002). One finger
  holds, Thirds and Bounced double notes are left out.
- BAPAM's rest of 3–5 minutes every 20–25 doesn't fit inside a routine, so the advanced day says to rest before pieces.
- Stop if anything hurts, and add minutes gradually. No study shows that warm-ups prevent injury (McCrary 2015): these are
  precautions.

**What "advanced" means here:** the four Later exercises, the drills in more keys and faster, and more minutes. That makes it
a late-intermediate day, not concert technique: double thirds, fast octaves and wide stretches aren't in the catalogue, and the
evidence argues against adding them.

Each routine's line in the app (its purpose) carries what a player needs on the day: what follows it (Sight-read and pieces),
the concert rule for pieces, and the octave caution. An activity plays as you last left its exercise, so a drill's key moves on
only when you move it: "move each drill on a key a week" is advice, not something Prima does.

Not used, and good swaps in a copy of one: Your hand's own shape, Fan and close, Finger taps on a table, Doorknob, Smaller
turns, Hold one turn to the next, Major then minor, Broken chords through the inversions, Four chords round and round, and the
three slow trills.

**Still open:** whether Today should offer Light day after a heavy day, or for a week each month; and bringing a routine's new
exercises in one per sitting, in Wait for me, as exercises.md suggests.
