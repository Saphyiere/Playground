# What MIDI key data can measure of piano technique, published values by level, and how practice software can adapt

Notes for the report writer. Scope: key and pedal data only (press time, release time, press velocity, pedals), as Prima receives them from a Yamaha P-225 over USB MIDI on Android. Every number is given with its conditions. A threshold marked **PROPOSAL** is mine, not a published value. Where I read only an abstract, a project page or a search snippet, the bullet says so. The web-search budget for this session ran out part-way through, so several follow-ups (listed under each "Gaps") were done only by opening known links, or not at all.

## What have researchers measured from MIDI or key-sensor data, and what values did they find for experts, amateurs and beginners?

### Takeaway
Expert values exist for most measures (scale evenness about 8–12 ms SD between note onsets at 8–10.7 notes a second, velocity spread about 4–6 MIDI units, chord and hand asynchronies mostly under 20–30 ms, trills about 10–12 notes a second), and listeners start to hear unevenness at about 10 ms, or 8% of the gap. Published values for amateurs are sparse and for beginners almost absent: the usable ones are a between-hand spread of about 14 ms in amateurs against 8 ms in experts, and tapping variability at least twice the trained musician's 2% of the interval.

### Cited Findings

#### Evenness of scale playing (gaps between note onsets)
- The method Prima already follows: MIDI "scale analysis" of standardised scales, analysing key velocities and timing; in 8 professional pianists with focal dystonia against 8 matched healthy professionals, affected hands had significantly higher mean standard deviations of timing, and the SD of inter-onset intervals correlated with a clinical disability score (abstract only; the healthy group's values are not in the abstract) — [Jabusch, Vauth & Altenmüller 2004, Movement Disorders](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14978672/)
- 13 professional pianists, C major over two octaves, each hand alone, sixteenths at metronome 120 (8 notes a second, 125 ms target gap), 10–15 scales: right-hand mean SD of inter-onset intervals 8.1 ms ascending and 8.9 ms descending. Notes before a thumb-under (3-1 and 4-1, ascending) were shortened, leaving an average gap of 13.0 ms between notes that listeners do not notice (institute project page reporting Jabusch 2006) — [IMMM Hannover, "Timing precision displayed by pianists"](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/en/research/previous-research-projects/timing-precision-displayed-by-pianists/)
- 8 pianists from a conservatory's student and teacher pool, same task (8 notes a second, legato, mezzo-forte, 10–15 scales per hand): unevenness (median over runs of the SD of the gaps) 8.44 ms (SD 1.81) right hand and 9.19 ms (SD 1.67) left hand — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2013, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00134)
- Audibility: 10 piano students could not tell a recorded scale from a perfectly even one; unevenness became audible at an SD of 10.22 ms (SD 2.51), about 8.2% of the 125 ms gap, "in line with the typical 10% threshold of a single late or early note" in an even sequence — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00134)
- Each pianist's note-by-note deviations are a stable "fingerprint": traces from the same pianist 15 minutes apart differed by about 3 ms; a nearest-trace classifier recognised all 8 pianists from right-hand scales — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00134)
- 34 conservatory piano students (mean 14,140 practice hours), two-octave scales at 8 notes a second, about 30 runs each: the SD of the gaps splits into "irregularity" (the same note late or early every time) and "instability" (run-to-run spread): IOI-SD = 0.93 × irregularity + 0.64 × instability + 0.62 (adjusted R² 0.81). Instability peaks at the first note, the last note and the octave boundary, where the thumb passes. 3.9% of onsets belonged to wrongly played scales and were discarded. A simulated pianist with 9 ms random jitter per note reproduced the measured instability — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2012, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3499913/)
- Hearing a single late note: with one note delayed by 40 ms in a scale at 8 notes a second, 19 music students were 79% (SD 8.2%) correct overall, and at chance when the delayed note was at the octave boundary — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3499913/)
- Faster: 21 piano students (mean 15,600 practice hours), C major two octaves at metronome 160 with four notes a beat (10.7 notes a second): unevenness 10.4 ms (SD 2.12) right hand, 12.3 ms (SD 2.64) left hand; irregularity 6.1–6.5 ms; instability 6.25 ms (SD 0.76) right, 7.16 ms (SD 1.11) left; 1.9% of keystrokes discarded as errors — [van Vugt, Treutler, Altenmüller & Jabusch 2013, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3705811/)
- Tempo changes the pattern: at slow tempi pianists slow at the start and end of the scale (a phrase shape); at fast tempi the deviations are three delays caused by the thumb-under; a four-parameter model predicts each note's deviation across tempi (R² = 0.70) (abstract) — [van Vugt, Furuya, Vauth, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2014, Experimental Brain Research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25059908/)
- Slower, and across instruments: 7 pianists played 10 two-octave C major scales, hands together, at 4 keystrokes a second (metronome 60) on 3 different keyboards; the timing patterns were the same on all three, with "anticipatory adjustments prior to thumb strokes" (abstract) — [Lipke-Perry, Dutto & Levy 2019, Music & Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/2059204319870733)
- Speed costs evenness: 7 highly skilled pianists played C major scales with each hand alone and hands together in similar and contrary motion at 60, 90, 120 and 180 metronome beats a minute; "variability of internote intervals increased with increases in playing speed, as did the variability of velocity of keypresses" (abstract as returned by search; I could not open the chapter, so no values) — [MacKenzie & Van Eerd 1990, Attention and Performance XIII](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203772010-12)
- Wagner 1971 is cited as one of the first to use the SD of the gaps between keystrokes as an unevenness measure for scales; I could not open the paper itself — [van Vugt et al. 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3499913/)
- Practice and evenness: in 19 expert pianists measured twice about 27 months apart, change in scale evenness correlated with practice time in between; those averaging 3.75 hours a day or more improved; practice time was the only predictor (43% of variance), practice content was not (abstract) — [Jabusch, Alpers, Kopiez, Vauth & Altenmüller 2009, Human Movement Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18845349/)
- Upper reference, impaired control: 26 pianists with dystonia at a 125 ms target gap had a median unevenness of 18.9 ms (range 10.4–35.6) — [Paulig, Jabusch, Großbach, Boullet & Altenmüller 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01012/full). In pieces played at a 125 ms gap, 6 dystonic professionals averaged 20 ms (SD 3) against 11 ms (SD 1) in 6 healthy professionals (left hand; 25,000–78,110 practice hours) — [Strübing et al. 2012, Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/fileadmin/www.immm/Publikationen/Struebing_ANYAS_2012.pdf)
- Run-to-run consistency in a short figure: 7 prize-winning pianists played an eight-note right-hand melody (fingers 5-3-5-2-4-3-4-2) at a 250 ms gap; the between-trial variability of each gap averaged 7.9 ± 1.2 ms (4.5–12.3 ms across the individual strokes) — [Tominaga, Lee, Altenmüller, Miyazaki & Furuya 2016, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0161324)
- Five-finger figure at speed: 12 skilled pianists played a repeating right-hand five-finger melody at 7.0 to 16.0 tones a second (143 to 62 ms gaps); all 12 managed up to 11.7 tones a second, one managed 16.0; timing error and the coefficient of variation of the gaps rose with tempo, the variation of key velocity did not — [Goebl & Palmer 2013, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3536780/)
- 4 skilled pianists playing melodies from memory kept mean gaps within 4% (7 ms) of the prescribed ones; the gaps were relatively more variable at faster tempi — [Dalla Bella & Palmer 2011, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3121738/)
- Experts against amateurs on technique exercises: 34 experts and 34 amateurs (amateurs: 12.5 years of playing on average, 3,970 lifetime practice hours) played Hanon no. 1 and a C major scale hands together at 4 notes a second on a Disklavier; a classifier on MIDI timing and velocity features told the groups apart with an F1 of 93.6% once differences between the hands were added; the variability of the gap ratio differed significantly between groups (raw group means of the gap SD are not printed) — [Kim, Park, Rhyu, Nam & Lee 2021, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)
- Novices to experts learning a new piece (ages 9–26, 3–15 years of training, 11 practice trials): tempo and pitch accuracy improved with skill and practice following a power function, with a trade-off between relative timing and pitch accuracy and many simultaneous pitch-and-time errors (abstract) — [Drake & Palmer 2000, Cognition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10594308/)

#### Keystroke velocity and its variability
- Within a scale run (10.7 notes a second, mezzo-forte, 21 piano students): the SD of MIDI velocity was 5.8 units (SD 1.5) in the right hand and 6.2 (SD 1.3) in the left — [van Vugt, Treutler, Altenmüller & Jabusch 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3705811/)
- Between trials (7 prize-winning pianists, target velocity 75, 250 ms gaps): the variability of each stroke's velocity averaged 4.1 ± 1.2 MIDI units (1.2–7.5 across strokes) — [Tominaga et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0161324)
- Balance between the hands in unison exercises: experts played the right hand 11.1 units (SD 3.5) louder than the left, amateurs 7.5 (SD 2.6); note-to-note velocity changes were more erratic in amateurs, while experts changed dynamics gradually following the line's contour — [Kim et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)
- In a trill, the two notes' dynamic levels differed by less than 2 MIDI units and their durations by less than 1 ms (4 pianists, Disklavier; Moore 1992 as summarised by a later paper, original not opened) — [Han & Bresin 2019, Nordic SMC](https://zenodo.org/records/3743459)
- Wrong notes are played softer than right ones, and the gaps around an error lengthen by about 150 ms (12 professional pianists) — [Strübing et al. 2012](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/fileadmin/www.immm/Publikationen/Struebing_ANYAS_2012.pdf)
- Fatigue shows in velocity: in 50 pianists, exhausting repetitions of a right-hand 16-note figure and of a chord sequence (played loud and fast; the tempo I could confirm for one excerpt was quarter note = 112) lowered key velocity in both, and the number of notes sounded in the chord sequence — [Goubault et al. 2021, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87403-8)
- With force sensors rather than MIDI: for the same tempo and loudness, amateurs pressed harder and for longer than experts (abstract) — [Parlitz, Peschel & Altenmüller 1998, Journal of Biomechanics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880064/)

#### Legato overlap and staccato gaps
- Definitions: key overlap time is the next key's press minus the previous key's release (positive = overlap); key overlap ratio is that divided by the gap between onsets; key detached time and ratio are the same for staccato — [Bresin 2000, "Virtual Virtuosity" (KTH thesis, summary of Bresin & Battel 2000)](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf)
- Legato, Mozart K. 545 Andante, 2,237 notes: the overlap ratio depended on the gap between onsets (a longer gap gave a lower ratio but a longer overlap in milliseconds); overlap was larger in "passionate" and smaller in "flat" renderings; descending sixteenth-note figures were played more legato than ascending — [Bresin 2000 thesis](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf)
- Staccato, 548 notes: the detached share was independent of the gap between onsets, about 60% of it in "natural" performances (so the key is down for about 40%); it moved towards staccatissimo in "bright" and "light" renderings and mezzo-staccato in "heavy" ones. C. P. E. Bach's rule, quoted there: a staccato note sounds for less than half its written length — [Bresin 2000 thesis](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf)
- Repeated notes: detached for about 40% of the gap (key down about 60%), and unlike staccato the share varied with the gap: the detached time in milliseconds stayed almost constant, because the finger must lift to strike the same key again — [Bresin 2000 thesis](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf); the journal abstract gives the same 40% and 60% durations (abstract via search) — [Bresin & Battel 2000, Journal of New Music Research](https://doi.org/10.1076/jnmr.29.3.211.3092)
- Skilled pianists' overlaps in legato scales and arpeggios grew with tone duration and as tempo fell, did not adapt to the instrument's decay, and differed greatly between individuals; on a digital piano the tone keeps sounding for a while after the key is released (abstracts; millisecond values not in them) — [Repp 1995, JASA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7790662/); [Repp 1997, JASA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9301065/)
- Professionals leave small gaps, not overlaps, before a thumb-under: 13.0 ms on average at 8 notes a second — [IMMM Hannover project page](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/en/research/previous-research-projects/timing-precision-displayed-by-pianists/)
- At scale (2026): across six MIDI corpora of professional performances (over 600 hours, 14 million notes) the overlap ratio follows the score's legato and staccato marks and the register; notes whose keys are released while the pedal is down are treated as sustained until the pedal lifts — [Hu, Zhang, Peter, Cancino-Chacón, Dixon & Widmer 2026, TISMIR 9(1)](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2026/HuEtAl-TISMIR2026.pdf)
- An "articulation" feature (key-down time divided by the gap to the next onset) and its difference between the hands separated experts from amateurs — [Kim et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)

#### Spread between the notes of a chord, and melody lead
- 22 skilled pianists (professionals, graduate students, professors) on a Bösendorfer recording grand: the melody note sounded 20–30 ms before the other notes of a chord when measured at the hammer, but the lead "decreases almost to zero" at the moment the fingers meet the keys: the louder note's key and hammer simply travel faster — [Goebl 2001, JASA](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- Why loudness shifts timing: a key takes about 25 ms to travel from rest to the bottom at forte and about 160 ms at piano (Askenfelt & Jansson, quoted there) — [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- Deliberate asynchronies are larger: usually over 30 ms; a bass note played early is typically about 50 ms ahead and up to 180 ms; one pianist led with the melody by 40–70 ms as a habit — [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- 10 graduate-student pianists on a Disklavier: the highest note tends to lead, inner notes of a chord within one hand lag behind the outer notes, and the lead goes with the difference in MIDI velocity; some pianists lead with the left hand regardless of velocity (abstract) — [Repp 1996, JASA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8969489/)
- In the 2026 multi-corpus study of professional recordings, the distributions of per-chord asynchrony (root mean square) in the two corpora compared peak below 20 ms (chords over 200 ms were excluded as likely transcription errors), melody lead appears in every corpus, and the leads on beats "may primarily reflect dynamic accentuation" — [Hu et al. 2026](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2026/HuEtAl-TISMIR2026.pdf)

#### Asynchrony between the hands
- Hands in unison at 4 notes a second (Hanon no. 1 and a C major scale): the SD of the right-hand-minus-left-hand onset difference was 0.008 (SD 0.002) in 34 experts and 0.014 (SD 0.006) in 34 amateurs; experts' right hand was ahead by 0.004 on average, while amateurs' mean difference was near zero (about 0.001 the other way; one table cell for the amateurs looks misprinted). The paper does not print the unit; the values fit seconds, so a spread of about 8 ms against 14 ms — [Kim et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)
- In one concert pianist's complete Chopin (63,344 between-hand asynchronies), a band of ±30 ms is used as the range "within which asynchronies are not likely to be perceived as such"; a bass anticipation is defined as the lowest note more than 50 ms early and occurs in under 1% to almost 2% of simultaneous events by genre; faster pieces have smaller and less variable asynchronies — [Goebl, Flossmann & Widmer 2010, Computer Music Journal](https://continuum-hypothesis.com/music/Goebl-InvestigationsBetweenHandSynchronization-2010.pdf)
- For comparison, players in ensembles are 30–50 ms apart (Rasch, quoted in both) — [Repp 2005](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf); [Goebl et al. 2010](https://continuum-hypothesis.com/music/Goebl-InvestigationsBetweenHandSynchronization-2010.pdf)

#### Tapping and timing against a metronome
- People tap ahead of the click: some by as much as 100 ms, others hardly at all — [Repp 2005, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf). At an 800 ms click interval drummers were about 20 ms early and professional pianists, amateur pianists, singers and non-musicians about 50 ms early; musicians playing their own instrument were only 2 ms early against 13 ms when tapping — [Repp & Su 2013, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2.pdf)
- Spread: a trained, practised musician can reach an SD of asynchronies of 2% of the interval; novices are typically at least twice that; percussionists' tap-to-tap SD can be 0.5% — [Repp 2005](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf). Drummers' tap intervals varied by about 2.5% of 800 ms, and professional pianists varied less than amateur pianists, singers and non-musicians — [Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2.pdf); [Krause, Pollok & Schnitzler 2010, Acta Psychologica](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751937/)
- Musical amateurs and people with no training did not differ in asynchrony spread at a 500 ms interval, but 9 highly trained musicians had smaller asynchronies and lower variability than 31 college students — [Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2.pdf); [Repp 2010, Human Movement Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20074825/)
- Rate limits: trained musicians can stay with a click down to gaps of 100–120 ms; tapping between the clicks gets hard below 350 ms (some manage 300 ms). Between about 250 and 2,000 ms variability grows in proportion to the interval; below 250 ms it stops shrinking. Filling a beat with subdivisions lowers variability only while each subdivision is at least 200–250 ms long — [Repp 2005](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf); [Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2.pdf)
- Pianists synchronising melodies with a metronome were less accurate on the beat when something heard or played fell between beats, most of all when it was heard (abstract) — [Loehr & Palmer 2009, Music Perception](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2009.26.5.415)

#### Fastest repeated notes, octave-type strikes and trills
- One-hand tapping is usually taken to top out at 5–7 a second (150–200 ms between taps); a record-holding drummer reached 10 a second with a stick (abstract) — [Fujii, Kudo, Ohtsuki & Oda 2009, Neuroscience Letters](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19409958/)
- 24 pianists from competition winners to amateurs struck two keys a minor sixth apart (E3 and C4) together with thumb and little finger as fast and accurately as possible on an upright: 6.0 to 7.8 strikes a second; the best predictors were single-finger tapping rate and elbow-extensor strength, not practice hours — [Furuya, Oku, Miyazaki & Kinoshita 2015, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep15750)
- Tremolo of a sixth (alternating E and C): 5 professionals and 5 amateurs both kept to the metronome up to the fastest condition, 115.5 ms between alternate strokes (about 8.7 strokes a second) — [Furuya, Goda, Katayose, Miwa & Nagata 2011, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3107480/)
- Ring and little fingers tap more slowly than index and middle in everyone, much less so in expert pianists, who are also steadier with index and middle; finger strength did not differ (abstract) — [Aoki, Furuya & Kinoshita 2005, Motor Control](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15784948/)
- Trills: 3 pianists on a Disklavier averaged 10.1 notes a second (SD 1.5): 11.1 with the right hand (93 ms between notes), 9.1 with the left (109 ms). They report Moore's 1992 study of 4 pianists as an average trill rate of 12 Hz — [Han & Bresin 2019](https://zenodo.org/records/3743459)

#### Leaps
- 36 music students practised a left-hand leap between two octave grips a tenth apart (15 semitones), all white keys, with a goal of 187.5 ms (a sixteenth at 80 beats a minute), 160 metronome-guided trials in 30 minutes. Errors were counted as missed notes, slips (extra notes next to a right one) and wrong notes within 500 ms either side. Error counts did not fall significantly after training or the next day, and leap times stayed well above the 187.5 ms goal. The study also logs leap time and the loudness difference between landing and take-off as measures (values only in figures) — [Bangert, Wiedemann & Jabusch 2014, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4128393/)

### Inferences
- Expert unevenness is about 7% of the gap at 8 notes a second (8.4 ms of 125 ms) and 11–13% at 10.7 notes a second (10.4–12.3 ms of 94 ms), which is also about where listeners begin to hear it (8%). For a learner playing at 2–4 notes a second, a relative threshold is the sensible form, because between 250 and 2,000 ms variability scales with the interval.
- "Even" for a learner cannot be read off a published norm; the nearest anchors are the 10 ms / 8–10% audibility figures, the novice tapping floor of about 4% of the interval, and the 14 ms hand-to-hand spread of long-term amateurs.
- Run-to-run spread ("instability") is the part that reflects control; the repeatable part ("irregularity") is partly anatomy and phrasing. Reporting them separately, as van Vugt 2012 does, tells the learner whether a late note is a habit or noise.
- Thumb crossings and the octave boundary should be expected to be the least steady places even in experts, and a gap of up to about 13 ms before the thumb is normal, not a legato fault.
- Because a louder key arrives sooner, any velocity difference inside a chord or between the hands shows up as an apparent timing lead of up to tens of milliseconds. Timing rules for chords and hands need to allow for it.
- The fastest rates give ceilings for exercise design: about 5–7 a second for one finger on one key, 6–8 a second for octave-type strikes, 10–12 notes a second for a trill, 8–11 notes a second for scales and five-finger figures by trained pianists. A grade 1–5 learner will work far below them.

### Gaps
- No published SD-of-gaps norms for beginners or grade 1–5 learners in scales were found. Kim 2021 has 34 amateurs but prints only test statistics for the gap SD.
- Jabusch 2004's own healthy-pianist values, MacKenzie & Van Eerd's numbers and Wagner 1971 are behind paywalls or unavailable; I report them only through abstracts and later papers.
- Repp's legato overlap values in milliseconds, and Bresin & Battel's fitted overlap formula, were not accessible (JASA and the journal are paywalled; the Haskins reprint links are dead).
- Moore 1992 on trills was not opened; "12 Hz" is Han & Bresin's summary, and whether it means notes or cycles per second is not stated there.
- For leaps I found one study; nothing with accuracy by distance, direction or key colour. The search budget ran out before a second pass.
- Kim 2021's between-hand unit is inferred (seconds), not stated.

## What does the literature on automatic assessment of piano performance offer for exercises?

### Takeaway
For beginners, wrong and repeated notes dominate a teacher's grade and are easy to compute; for technique exercises, simple MIDI statistics (spread of onsets, velocities and durations, and differences between the hands) separate experts from amateurs well, but they predict individual teachers' ratings poorly because the teachers themselves disagree. No published system scores the specific exercise families beyond scales; the literature supplies measures, not thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- Review of the field: assessment tools "typically" judge pitch and timing accuracy or tone quality; beginners gain from feedback on pitch and rhythm accuracy, advanced players from articulation and expression; features aligned to the score beat score-independent ones and tempo-variation features were "particularly critical"; learned features outperform hand-made ones but cannot be explained; the judged qualities are "ill-defined, leading to large differences in subjective opinion among music educators"; commercial tutors (SmartMusic, Yousician, Music Prodigy, SingStar are named) are "not reliable enough to be used in educational settings"; datasets are small and skewed — [Lerch, Arthur, Pati & Gururani 2020, TISMIR](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.53)
- Technique exercises rated by teachers (2024): 104 recordings by 39 advanced students of scales, arpeggios, dyads, block chords and octaves, rated by 45 professional pianists on a 6-point scale. The questions show what teachers attend to: uniform note values, solid sound, clean attack, correct rhythm, constant tempo, connected lines, sense of tonality, natural change of dynamics, even legato, balance between the hands, onsets aligned between the hands. For most questions about 65% of doubly rated items agreed within 2 points; dynamics and legato only about 50%. Models using onset, velocity and duration differences (mean and SD) predicted ratings with an error of 1.04–1.38 points against 1.05–1.57 for always guessing the average — [Zhang, Cheung, Nishioka, Dixon & Furuya 2024, "How does the teacher rate? Observations from the NeuroPiano dataset"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03139)
- Experts against amateurs from MIDI alone: timing features (key-down ratio, onset-gap ratio, articulation and their note-to-note changes) and velocity features, per hand and as differences between hands, on Hanon no. 1 and a C major scale: F1 of 93.6% — [Kim et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)
- Beginners: in students playing Beyer op. 101 pieces, a weighted rate of wrong pitches and extra notes correlated 0.86 (Spearman) with the teacher's evaluation (the teacher graded wrong touch and re-playing, rhythm, and dynamics, each from A to C); steadiness was measured as the root-mean-square error of local tempo against the median tempo — [Kato, Nakamura, Mine, Doeda & Yamada 2023, EC-TEL](https://eita-nakamura.github.io/articles/Kato_PianoPerformanceAutomaticEvaluation_%20ECTEL2023.pdf)
- The clinical scale analysis is the best-validated MIDI score of technique: the SD of the gaps tracks a clinician's disability rating and treatment effects — [Jabusch et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14978672/)
- A thesis scoring one-octave scales on a MIDI piano used five parameters (onset time, velocity, duration, legato, tempo) and compared its scores with four expert pianists' by correlation (search snippet only; the repository page did not load) — [NYCU thesis record](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/126351)
- Audio and video systems, which mark what key data lacks: a video-and-audio dataset for judging a player's skill level — [Parmar, Reddy & Morris 2021, "Piano Skills Assessment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.04884); audio encoders reach 93.6% on ranking expertise but 33.7% on difficulty and 46.7% on detecting techniques — [Zhang, Liang & Dixon 2024, ISMIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04518); a language-model "coach" answering questions about a recording — [Zhang et al. 2024, LLaQo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08795)
- Score difficulty is hard to predict: 9 levels from Henle's grading, best model 39.5% balanced accuracy and a median squared error of 1.13, from fingering and expressiveness features — [Ramoneda et al. 2023, "Combining piano performance dimensions for score difficulty classification"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08480)
- Hand motion needs cameras: 10 hours of 3D hand motion from 15 elite pianists, reconstructed from multi-view video and refined with Disklavier key data — [Wang et al. 2024, FürElise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05791); 116 hours of overhead video with 10 million hand poses — [Gan et al. 2025, PianoMotion10M](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09326)
- Large MIDI corpora now exist for norms of professional playing (six corpora, over 600 hours) — [Hu et al. 2026](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2026/HuEtAl-TISMIR2026.pdf)
- Early tutoring systems existed for beginners (title-level only; paper not opened) — [Dannenberg et al. 1990, "A computer-based multi-media tutor for beginning piano students"](https://doi.org/10.1080/09298219008570563); and a 2020 overview of assessment technology for instrument learning (title-level only) — [Eremenko, Morsi, Narang & Serra 2020, CSEDU](https://doi.org/10.5220/0009817006290640)

### Inferences
- The teachers' eleven questions in NeuroPiano map almost one-to-one onto what MIDI gives: uniform note values and tempo (gap SD, drift), clean attack and solid sound (velocity level and spread, only partly), connected lines and even legato (overlap), balance and alignment of the hands (velocity difference, onset difference). "Sense of tonality" and "natural dynamics" are the least measurable and also the ones teachers agree on least.
- Since teachers agree within 2 points on a 6-point scale only about two-thirds of the time, an app's score should be presented as a measurement (milliseconds, velocity units, share of the beat) with a reference band, not as a grade.
- For a grade 1–5 learner the order of importance is right notes first, then steadiness, then evenness, touch and legato, which matches both the Kato result and the review's point about beginners.

### Gaps
- I could not find a study relating MIDI measures to teacher ratings for the specific families asked about (thirds, sixths, broken chords, cadences, leaps, trills, pedalling). NeuroPiano covers dyads, chords and octaves but reports no per-exercise feature weights.
- The Akinaga and Miura work on automatic scale evaluation named in earlier searches did not surface; the overview by Kim et al. (CSEDU 2022) and the 2023–24 mistake-detection papers by Morsi et al. were not opened.
- Nothing from 2025–26 on commercial apps' scoring was checked; the search budget ran out.

## What are the limits of MIDI from a digital piano (P-225 over USB to Android), and can fingering or hand position be inferred?

### Takeaway
Timing over USB MIDI is good to a millisecond or two on average with a few milliseconds of jitter, and Android stamps each message when it arrives, not when the key moved, so thresholds of 10 ms and more are safe and differences of 2–3 ms are not. Velocity is a 1–127 number whose meaning is the instrument's own; release velocity and continuous pedal values cannot be assumed on the P-225 and must be checked on the instrument; and nothing in the data says which finger played, with the best models matching a pianist's fingering on only about 60–64% of notes.

### Cited Findings

#### Velocity
- MIDI 1.0 note-on and note-off each carry a key number and a velocity of 0–127 (7 bits) — [MIDI Association, Summary of MIDI 1.0 Messages](https://midi.org/summary-of-midi-1-0-messages)
- A higher-resolution velocity exists as controller 88, "High Resolution Velocity Prefix" — [MIDI Association, Control Change Messages](https://midi.org/midi-1-0-control-change-messages); Android can use MIDI 2.0 over USB from Android 13 — [Android Open Source Project, MIDI](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/midi)
- The MIDI standard "does not specify exactly how velocity should be interpreted"; measured synthesizers map velocity to level roughly by a square law but their dynamic ranges differ by more than 60 dB (from 11 dB to 89 dB in the instruments tested) — [Dannenberg 2006, "The Interpretation of MIDI Velocity", ICMC](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rbd/papers/velocity-icmc2006.pdf)
- Even research pianos differ: a Disklavier captured dynamics well "only in a wide middle range", and neither it nor a Bösendorfer SE could capture type of touch — [Goebl & Bresin 2003, JASA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14587624/)
- A piano set to Fixed touch sends a single velocity, which Prima already handles by not judging loudness — [Prima concept, in this repository](../../Music/docs/concept.md)
- Users report that Yamaha P-series pianos used as MIDI keyboards do not reach the lowest and highest velocities, and that the touch-curve setting changes what is reachable (forum reports, not measurements; none first-hand for the P-225) — [KVR Audio forum thread on the P-225](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8919892)

#### Timing resolution, jitter and Android timestamps
- A MIDI cable carries 31,250 bits a second, about 1 ms per three-byte message (measured 1.04 ms, SD 0.02) — [Schultz 2019, Behavior Research Methods](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-018-1042-7)
- USB MIDI sends 32-bit event packets over bulk endpoints, and the class specification's revision history says "Removed all references to time stamps": a USB MIDI 1.0 event carries no time of its own — [USB Device Class Definition for MIDI Devices 1.0](https://www.usb.org/sites/default/files/midi10.pdf)
- Bulk transfers have "no guarantee of a minimum or consistent latency". Three USB MIDI interfaces measured on a PC had mean latencies of 0.96–1.22 ms with SDs of 0.99–1.27 ms and a range of 0–4 ms; card-slot interfaces 0.96 ms, SD 0.8, range 0–2 ms — [Schultz 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-018-1042-7)
- The instrument adds its own delay: three MIDI percussion pads all took more than 1 ms from strike to MIDI message, the best averaging 3.35 ms — [Schultz 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-018-1042-7)
- Android timestamps are "based on the monotonic nanosecond system timer", the same clock as `System.nanoTime()` — [Android developers, android.media.midi](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/media/midi/package-summary)
- For a USB device, Android's system service stamps the data after the USB read returns ("Record time of event after receiving response. `long timestamp = System.nanoTime();`"), once per read, and passes that one timestamp on with all the bytes read — [AOSP, UsbDirectMidiDevice.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/master/services/usb/java/com/android/server/usb/UsbDirectMidiDevice.java)
- Android's MIDI documentation and its MIDI test procedure contain no latency, jitter or timestamp-accuracy figures; the tests are functional — [AOSP MIDI](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/midi); [AOSP MIDI test procedure](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/midi_test)
- For scale, a Disklavier's own onset capture is accurate to about ±10 ms and a Bösendorfer SE's to −10 to +3 ms — [Goebl & Bresin 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14587624/)

#### When the note-on happens, relative to the finger
- On an acoustic grand the key takes 25 ms (forte) to 160 ms (piano) from rest to bottom, and the hammer strikes 12 ms before key bottom at piano but 3 ms after it at forte — [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf); travel times also depend on whether the key is pressed from the surface or struck from above — [Goebl, Bresin & Galembo 2005, JASA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16158669/)
- So onset times taken late in the key's travel shift with loudness: asynchronies between notes of different velocity are "largely a consequence of dynamic differentiation" — [Repp 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8969489/); [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)

#### Release velocity
- Yamaha models differ and the manuals are not reliable on this: forum users measured a fixed release velocity of 64 on the P-525 and CLP-785 although their MIDI references said 1–127, and report that the older P-515, CLP-6xx and CSP-170 do send varying values. The P-225 is not mentioned (user reports) — [Modartt (Pianoteq) forum thread](https://forum.modartt.com/viewtopic.php?pid=995116)

#### Pedals
- In MIDI 1.0 the damper (64), sostenuto (66) and soft (67) pedals are defined as switches: "≤63 off, ≥64 on" — [MIDI Association, Control Change Messages](https://midi.org/midi-1-0-control-change-messages)
- Research on professional MIDI recordings treats controller 64 as a continuous signal with a threshold, and finds pedal releases as large drops in its value — [Hu et al. 2026](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2026/HuEtAl-TISMIR2026.pdf)

#### Which finger, which hand, how the hand moved
- Fingering cannot be read from the notes even by people: comparing two pianists' fingerings of the same piece, "on average, 60% to 80% of the notes are played with the same fingers" (150 pieces, 309 fingerings, about 100,000 notes, by pianists with music-college training or 20 years of playing) — [Nakamura, Saito & Yoshii 2020, "Statistical learning and estimation of piano fingering"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10237)
- The best automatic methods match a pianist's fingering on about 60–64% of notes (first- and second-order hidden Markov models), a neural network on about 60%, a constraint-based search on 57%; a human matches another human on about 70% by the same measure — [Nakamura et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10237)
- A rule-based ergonomic model ranks fingerings of short single-line fragments by difficulty (spans, weak fingers, thumb passes); pianists' own fingerings were mostly among its easiest, but it also predicted many that pianists do not use (abstract) — [Parncutt, Sloboda, Clarke, Raekallio & Desain 1997, Music Perception](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285730)
- Timing does betray where the thumb passes: more run-to-run spread at the octave boundary, delays at thumb-unders at fast tempi, shortened notes before the thumb — [van Vugt et al. 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3499913/); [van Vugt et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25059908/); [IMMM Hannover project page](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/en/research/previous-research-projects/timing-precision-displayed-by-pianists/)
- Finger and joint motion, wrist rotation and key force have been measured only with motion capture, cameras or force sensors — [Goebl & Palmer 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3536780/); [Wang et al. 2024, FürElise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05791); [Parlitz et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880064/)

### Inferences
- Measurement noise on a gap between two notes is roughly 1.5 ms from USB alone (two arrivals, each about 1 ms SD) plus Android scheduling and the piano's key scanning, both unmeasured. Treat differences under about 3 ms as noise; an unevenness of 10 ms is comfortably measurable.
- Notes of a chord that arrive in the same USB read get the same Android timestamp, so chord spread is quantised to the USB transfer rhythm (about 1 ms); that is fine against thresholds of 15–30 ms.
- Because the timestamp is the arrival time, a constant delay (the piano's scan, USB) cancels out of every within-performance measure (gaps, overlap, spread, hand sync). It matters only against the metronome, where the tablet's click also has an output delay; Prima's timing offset setting is the right fix, and sounding the click on the piano over MIDI would remove the tablet's audio delay from the comparison.
- Velocity rules should be written against the player's own measured range on this piano (Prima's Keyboard check already records it), never as absolute "forte = 100".
- If the P-225 sends a fixed release velocity, how fast a key is let go (a staccato quality teachers hear) is not judgeable; release *time* still is.
- If the standard foot switch is plugged in, pedal data is on/off; half-pedalling can only be judged if a continuous pedal is attached and the piano passes the values on.
- Fingering can be assumed (the drill prescribes it) but not verified. A per-finger report is honest only as "the notes this finger should have played". A statistical check is possible: a thumb pass leaves a timing signature, so a missing signature where one is expected, or one where none is, hints at a different fingering, but cannot prove it.
- Which hand played is knowable in drills where the hands have separate registers or the app prescribes hands; it is ambiguous when hands cross or share notes.

### Gaps
- Yamaha's P-225 MIDI reference could not be opened (Yamaha's sites refused the request), so these are **unverified for this instrument**: whether note-off carries a varying velocity; the pedal values sent with the standard foot switch, an FC3A or the three-pedal unit; whether the touch setting (other than Fixed) changes the velocities sent; the number of key sensors and the point in the key's travel where note-on is sent; the fastest repetition the action registers. All can be read off the instrument with Prima's Keyboard check.
- That note-on with velocity 0 counts as note-off is standard MIDI practice but is not on the summary page I could open; the full specification needs a login.
- No published measurement of Android USB MIDI input jitter was found. The Android audio latency page I opened has no table, so the tablet click's delay is unquantified here.
- Hand-separation methods for MIDI (for example Hadjakos et al. 2019) could not be opened.

## How should exercises adapt?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a few plain rules: keep most attempts correct (errors during practice predict worse retention), raise tempo in small ordered steps only after several clean runs and drop it after a failure, judge mastery on a later day, not within the session, and mix items once each is playable, warning the learner that mixed practice feels worse than it works. No study shows that one tempo ladder beats another, and for leaps and other generated exercises Fitts' law gives a usable difficulty index that nobody has validated on the piano.

### Cited Findings

#### Accuracy targets and tempo
- In 17 advanced piano students practising a hard passage, next-day performance rank was unrelated to practice time or number of repetitions but was related to the share of practice trials played correctly (r = −0.51 for all trials, −0.71 for complete trials; lower rank number is better) and to the number of incorrect trials (r = 0.48) — [Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009, Journal of Research in Music Education](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- A theoretical optimum for learning by gradual error correction is about 85% success (15.87% errors); derived for two-choice classification by learning algorithms, not for motor skill — [Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4). Prima already aims for about 85% — [Prima concept](../../Music/docs/concept.md)
- The challenge-point framework holds that the best task difficulty depends on the learner's skill, because learning depends on how much usable information the performance gives (a framework, not a threshold) — [Guadagnoli & Lee 2004, Journal of Motor Behavior](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15130871/)
- Tempo patterns compared directly: 60 high-school wind players practised an étude six times with a steady increase of tempo, at performance tempo, or alternating slower and faster; "Tempo patterns had no significant effect"; hearing a model helped rhythm and tempo gains — [Henley 2001, Journal of Research in Music Education](https://doi.org/10.2307/3345868)
- Complete novices practising a piano sequence at several tempi: practice conditions (a large or small set of tempi, in random or non-random order) "had little effect on learning and transfer performance of timing skill", but for movement consistency "lower temporal variability during practice" and a "non-randomized learning schedule" transferred better to new tempi and new sequences — [Caramiaux, Bevilacqua, Wanderley & Palmer 2018, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0193580)
- Speed costs accuracy and evenness even in experts — [Goebl & Palmer 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3536780/); [MacKenzie & Van Eerd 1990 (abstract)](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203772010-12); and gains in tempo and accuracy over practice trials follow a power function, steep at first and then flat — [Drake & Palmer 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10594308/)
- Up-down rules from psychophysics give a principled way to hold a success rate: move harder after n successes in a row and easier after one failure — [Levitt 1971, "Transformed up-down methods in psychoacoustics", JASA](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1912375)
- Signs that a set should stop: with fatigue, key velocity falls and notes drop out — [Goubault et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87403-8); errors are preceded and followed by slowing of about 150 ms — [Strübing et al. 2012](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/fileadmin/www.immm/Publikationen/Struebing_ANYAS_2012.pdf)

#### Scheduling over days: spacing, sleep, interleaving
- 29 musicians (not pianists) learned a 9-note keyboard sequence in three sessions 5 minutes, 6 hours or 24 hours apart: accuracy improved in the second session only with 24 hours between; speed kept improving in the third session only with 6 or 24 hours between — [Simmons 2012, Journal of Research in Music Education](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429411424798)
- Within one sitting, spacing did nothing: untrained adults learning a 17-note piano sequence with 0, 1, 5, 10 or 15 minutes between learning episodes showed no spacing effect on note accuracy, evenness of key pressure or timing after 5 minutes — [Wiseheart, D'Souza & Chae 2017, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0182986)
- For facts, the best gap between study sessions grows with how long the memory must last: about 20–40% of a one-week delay, falling to 5–10% of a one-year delay (over 1,350 people) — [Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x); for motor skills, the title result is that spacing sessions across days helps (abstract not available to me) — [Shea, Lai, Black & Park 2000, Human Movement Science](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-9457%2800%2900021-X)
- Mixed order, piano: 25 trained pianists practised melodies in fixed order (A, A, B, B) and random order; they were faster on fixed-order melodies at first but faster on random-order ones two days later, while believing fixed order had worked better — [Abushanab & Bishara 2013, Memory & Cognition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23508339/)
- Mixed order, beginners: 41 beginning clarinettists practising three short exercises in blocked or random order did not differ at the end of practice; at retention the random group was faster and the blocked group had slowed — [Stambaugh 2011, Journal of Research in Music Education](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429410385945)
- Mixed order, advanced: 10 clarinettists practised pieces blocked (12 minutes each) or interleaved (3 minutes at a time); next-day ratings by three professionals favoured interleaving wherever they differed, though raters varied, and players reported better focus and goal-setting — [Carter & Grahn 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Long-run maintenance of evenness in experts depended on how much they practised, not what — [Jabusch et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18845349/)

#### Difficulty models for generated exercises
- Fitts' law: movement time = a + b × ID, with the index of difficulty ID = log2(D/W + 1) in the common "Shannon" form (D the distance to the target, W its width); a and b are fitted per person and task; with a 4% miss rate the nominal width equals the effective width (4.133 × the SD of landing points) — [Wikipedia, "Fitts's law", summarising Fitts 1954 and MacKenzie 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitts%27s_law)
- Keyboard geometry: an octave spans 164–165 mm, white keys are about 23.5 mm wide at the base, black keys average 13.7 mm (the article cites no source for these) — [Wikipedia, "Musical keyboard"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_keyboard)
- A leap of a tenth to an octave grip in 187.5 ms was beyond music students even after 160 trials, so that combination of distance and time is above the useful range for learners — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4128393/)
- Fingering-based difficulty: rule costs for spans, weak-finger use and thumb passes — [Parncutt et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.2307/40285730); statistical fingering models that give a likelihood for any note sequence — [Nakamura et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10237)
- Whole-score difficulty is predicted poorly by current models (39.5% balanced accuracy over 9 levels; 33.7% from audio) — [Ramoneda et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08480); [Zhang, Liang & Dixon 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04518)
- Rate ceilings that bound any tempo ladder: synchronising with a click fails below 100–120 ms gaps; subdividing helps only above 200–250 ms per subdivision; playing between clicks is hard below 350 ms — [Repp 2005](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Tempo rule (PROPOSAL).** Raise the tempo one small step after n clean runs in a row and lower it one step after a failed run. By the up-down logic, with equal steps up and down, this settles where the chance of n clean runs in a row is one half, so the chance of a clean run is 0.5^(1/n): 71% for two in a row, 79% for three, 84% for four. "Four clean runs in a row to go up, one failed run to come down" therefore lands on Prima's 85% target with no extra machinery. Steps should be small and ordered (the novice result on tempo order), for example 4–6% of the tempo.
- **What counts as a clean run (PROPOSAL).** No wrong, missed or extra note, and each of the drill's measures inside its band. Requiring both keeps the Duke result in force: the learner mostly rehearses correct trials.
- **Mastery (PROPOSAL).** Count an item (a key, a pattern, a tempo) as held only when the first run on a later day is clean at that tempo. Same-session success overstates learning (Abushanab & Bishara; Stambaugh), and accuracy gains needed a night's sleep in Simmons's study.
- **Scheduling (PROPOSAL).** One short daily visit per item beats repeats within a sitting (Simmons against Wiseheart). While an item is new, keep it in a short block until it is playable; once it is, rotate keys and patterns between runs and say plainly that it will feel harder and work better. Stretch the interval between visits as an item stays clean, to roughly 10–30% of how long it should last (the fact-learning figures are 20–40% for a week and 5–10% for a year; carried over to technique, so a starting point only).
- **Stop rule (PROPOSAL).** End a set when velocity sags or notes start dropping across successive runs, or after two consecutive step-downs; the fatigue study suggests these are the MIDI-visible signs.
- **Leap difficulty (PROPOSAL).** Use ID = log2(D/W + 1) with D in millimetres from the octave span (13.75 mm per semitone on average; exact key centres are better) and W = 23.5 mm for a white landing key, 13.7 mm for a black one. An octave to a white key is 3.0 bits, to a black key 3.7; two octaves 3.9 and 4.6; a tenth to a white key 3.3. Divide by the time allowed to get a required rate in bits per second, fit the learner's own a and b from logged leap times, and generate leaps whose predicted time fits the gap with a margin. A chord landing, a landing by the weak side of the hand, or a leap away from the body's midline should be treated as harder than the index says; that part is judgement.
- **Other generated difficulty (PROPOSAL).** Order by: hands separate before together; white keys before mixed; stepwise before skips; fewer thumb passes before more; then tempo. This follows what the data show is hard (thumb passes, the left hand, speed) rather than a validated model.
- The 85% figure is a reasonable design target but it is borrowed: it comes from a theory of classification learning. The piano-specific support is Duke's correlation, which says "more correct trials", not "exactly 85%".

### Gaps
- No controlled study of an automatic tempo-raising rule on the piano was found; Henley's wind study is the nearest and found no effect of tempo pattern.
- The convergence percentages for the up-down rule (71%, 79%, 84%) are my arithmetic from the rule's logic; I had only the abstract of Levitt 1971, not its tables.
- Mastery-criterion research from tutoring systems (n correct in a row against model-based criteria: Kelly et al. 2015; Pelánek & Řihák 2017, 2018) could be identified only by title — [Pelánek & Řihák 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/13614568.2018.1476596). My recollection that simple criteria do about as well as model-based ones is unverified.
- The brain-sensing adaptive piano tutor — [Yuksel et al. 2016, "Learn Piano with BACh", CHI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858388) — could not be opened, so its results are not reported.
- No Fitts' law constants for piano leaps were found, and no data on leap accuracy by direction or key colour. ABRSM's minimum scale tempi by grade, which would anchor the tempo ladder, were not checked.
- Spacing evidence for motor skills over weeks (as opposed to facts) rests here on one title and one three-session study.

## For each exercise family: what MIDI can judge, a scoring rule with numbers, and what needs a teacher's eye or a camera

### Takeaway
Every family can be scored on right notes, timing against the grid, evenness, loudness pattern and key overlap, with bands anchored on the published values above; families that add something new are chords and double notes (spread and balance), leaps (landing accuracy and leap time), two-against-three (position in the cycle) and pedalling (order of pedal and key events). What stays out of reach in every family is which finger was used, how the hand, wrist and arm moved, tension, and tone beyond velocity.

### Cited Findings
The anchors used below, each with its source (details and conditions are in the first section):
- Audible unevenness from an SD of about 10 ms, or 8% of a 125 ms gap; a single late note from about 10% — [van Vugt et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00134)
- Expert scale unevenness 8–9 ms at 8 notes a second and 10–12 ms at 10.7 — [van Vugt et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00134); [van Vugt, Treutler et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3705811/)
- Timing spread of 2% of the interval for trained musicians, at least 4% for novices; early by 20–50 ms against a click — [Repp 2005](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03206433.pdf); [Repp & Su 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2.pdf)
- Velocity spread within a run 5.8–6.2 units in piano students; 4.1 between trials in prize-winners — [van Vugt, Treutler et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3705811/); [Tominaga et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0161324)
- Hands: spread of the onset difference about 8 ms in experts, 14 ms in amateurs; right hand louder by 11 units in experts, 7.5 in amateurs — [Kim et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250299)
- Asynchronies within ±30 ms are unlikely to be heard as such; deliberate ones exceed 30 ms; an early bass is over 50 ms; a louder note arrives 20–30 ms early from key travel alone — [Goebl et al. 2010](https://continuum-hypothesis.com/music/Goebl-InvestigationsBetweenHandSynchronization-2010.pdf); [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- Professional chord asynchronies peak below 20 ms — [Hu et al. 2026](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2026/HuEtAl-TISMIR2026.pdf)
- Staccato: key down about 40% of the gap, whatever the gap; repeated notes about 60%; legato overlap grows with the gap in milliseconds and shrinks as a share; professionals leave about 13 ms gaps before the thumb — [Bresin 2000 thesis](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf); [IMMM Hannover project page](https://www.immm.hmtm-hannover.de/en/research/previous-research-projects/timing-precision-displayed-by-pianists/)
- Rates: one finger 5–7 a second; sixth or octave-type strikes 6.0–7.8 a second; trills 9–12 notes a second; five-finger figures to 11.7 tones a second — [Fujii et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19409958/); [Furuya et al. 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep15750); [Han & Bresin 2019](https://zenodo.org/records/3743459); [Goebl & Palmer 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3536780/)
- Polyrhythms: accuracy is "substantially lower than in simple rhythm contexts" and falls as the ratio gets more complex (3 musically trained people; three against two at a 1,200 ms cycle is 400 ms against 600 ms on a 200 ms grid), and performers learn the combined pattern first — [Deutsch 1983, Perception & Psychophysics](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03203045.pdf); skilled and unskilled players both organise the slow hand under the fast hand — [Summers, Rosenbaum, Burns & Ford 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8473848/); professional pianists time the two hands as one pattern at slow tempi and as two parallel streams at fast tempi (three against four) — [Krampe, Kliegl, Mayr, Engbert & Vorberg 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10696614/)
- Pedal timing in two pianists' performances of one piece showed no simple rule across tempi, was consistent within each pianist, and differed strikingly between the two, who differed in skill (abstract; no values) — [Repp 1996, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735696242011); the pedal is a switch at 64 in MIDI 1.0 — [MIDI Association, Control Change Messages](https://midi.org/midi-1-0-control-change-messages)
- Leaps: errors as missed, slipped and wrong notes; leap time; landing-against-take-off loudness — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4128393/)
- Teachers agree least on dynamics and legato (about 50% within 2 points of 6) — [Zhang et al. 2024, NeuroPiano](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03139)
- Prima's present expression rules use 8 velocity units for an accent and 6 for a hairpin — [Prima concept](../../Music/docs/concept.md)

### Inferences
All bands below are **PROPOSALS** built on the anchors above; "gap" means the time between successive note onsets at the drill's tempo. Common to every family: a run with a wrong, missed or extra note does not count for the timing and touch measures (as in the clinical scale analysis and in Prima today); bands are relative to the gap where the literature says variability scales with it; and three bands are suggested throughout: **clean**, **nearly**, **work on it**.

Shared rules, used by several families:
- **Evenness of timing.** Fit a straight line to onset time against note number for the run and take the SD of the gaps (or of the deviations from the line). Clean: SD ≤ the larger of 10 ms and 5% of the gap. Nearly: up to 10% of the gap. Work on it: above 10%. Anchors: audible at 8% and about 10 ms; experts and conservatory students at 7–13% at 8–10.7 notes a second; novice tapping at 4% or more of much longer intervals. Report the repeatable part (the same note late every run) separately from the run-to-run part once there are five or more runs.
- **Evenness of touch.** SD of velocity over notes meant to be equal. Clean: ≤ 6 units. Nearly: ≤ 10. Anchors: 5.8–6.2 in piano students at speed, 4.1 in prize-winners. Scale the bands to the player's calibrated range if it is much narrower than 25–105.
- **Legato.** Per transition, overlap = next press − previous release. Clean: from −15 ms (a gap too short to hear; professionals leave 13 ms at thumb passes) up to 30% of the gap. Detached: below −15 ms. Heavy: 30–50% of the gap. Smeared: above 50% of the gap, and always when the overlap exceeds the whole gap, since then three keys of a stepwise line are down together. The upper bands are judgement: published overlaps vary widely between pianists.
- **Staccato.** Key-down time as a share of the gap. Clean: ≤ 50% (C. P. E. Bach's rule; measured natural staccato is about 40%). Also report the SD of the key-down times, since an even staccato is the point.
- **Together (chords and hands).** Spread = last press − first press among notes meant to sound together. Clean: ≤ 20 ms. Nearly: ≤ 30 ms. Apart: > 30 ms; rolled: > 50 ms. If one note is meant to be louder, allow it to lead by up to 30 ms without penalty, because the louder key arrives sooner.

By family:

- **Five-finger patterns.** *MIDI can judge:* right notes; evenness of timing and touch; each position's average lateness and loudness (reported per finger on the assumption that the printed fingering was used); legato overlap; in held-note variants, whether the held keys stay down (their release must come after the pattern ends). *Rule:* the shared evenness and legato bands; flag a position that is late or early by more than 5% of the gap in most runs, or more than 6 units louder or softer than the mean, as a weak or heavy finger. *Out of reach:* whether fingers 4 and 5 really played their notes, finger height and curvature, a collapsing knuckle, wrist tension.

- **Scales in thirds and sixths (double notes).** *MIDI can judge:* both notes of each pair present; spread within each pair; evenness of the pairs' onsets (use the earlier note or the mean); balance of upper against lower voice; legato in the upper voice and, separately, the lower. *Rule:* pair spread by the "together" bands (≤ 20 ms clean, > 30 ms apart); pair-to-pair timing by the evenness bands; upper voice at least as loud as the lower on average, and ≥ 8 units louder when the top is to be voiced; legato judged on the upper voice only, since joining both voices is often physically impossible. *Out of reach:* the fingering of each pair, which is most of the difficulty; whether the hand shifts or stretches; sliding fingers. No published norm for double-note scales was found; the bands are carried over from chords.

- **Broken chords and arpeggio figures.** *MIDI can judge:* as for scales, with a close look at the notes either side of each change of hand position; the accent pattern of the groups (first of each group louder, if asked). *Rule:* the shared evenness bands overall; report the largest single deviation separately and expect it at the position change; legato tolerance widened to −30 ms at a position change in wide figures (the 13 ms professional gap at a scale's thumb pass is the only published figure, so this is a guess scaled up). *Out of reach:* whether the thumb passed under or the hand moved over, wrist rotation, a jerking elbow.

- **Solid chords with inversions and cadences.** *MIDI can judge:* right notes, missing and extra notes; spread on the way down and, separately, on the way up (releases together); balance between top note and the rest and between the hands; whether common notes between two chords were held or re-struck when the exercise asks; the gap or overlap between successive chords; time taken to find each new chord when untimed. *Rule:* spread by the "together" bands; top voiced when it is ≥ 8 units above the mean of the other notes (11 units is the expert right-over-left difference); chord-to-chord connection by the legato band on the top voice; release spread ≤ 30 ms. *Out of reach:* fingering and finger substitution, hand shape, whether the arm or the fingers made the sound, tone quality beyond velocity.

- **Leaps.** *MIDI can judge:* whether the landing key was right, one key off, or right with a neighbour brushed (a second key within about 50 ms of the landing); how late the landing was against the beat; leap time (take-off release to landing press); the landing's loudness against the take-off's; hesitation before take-off (a shortened take-off note). *Rule:* a leap is clean when the landing key alone sounds, within the drill's usual timing band, and its velocity is within 10 units of the take-off's unless a dynamic is marked; advance distance or tempo when the clean share over the last 10–12 leaps is at least 85%; order leaps by the Fitts index in the adaptation section (octave to white key 3.0 bits, to black key 3.7, two octaves 3.9 and 4.6) divided by the time allowed. *Out of reach:* whether the player looked, the path and height of the hand, whether the arm prepared over the landing key, tension on landing. Only one study measured leaps this way, and it found a tenth in 187.5 ms out of reach for music students.

- **Octaves.** *MIDI can judge:* both notes present and no neighbour caught (a seventh or ninth sounding instead); spread between thumb and fifth finger; balance of top against bottom; evenness of timing and loudness over repeated or moving octaves; the trend of velocity and dropped notes across a set, as a fatigue sign. *Rule:* spread ≤ 20 ms clean; the shared evenness bands; stop the set when mean velocity has fallen by more than 6 units from the first runs or notes start dropping. Rate ceiling for design: trained pianists manage 6.0–7.8 strikes a second on a sixth; a learner's ladder should stop far below that. *Out of reach:* whether the hand can span an octave comfortably, wrist against arm motion, stiffness and pain, which matter more here than in any other family.

- **Repeated notes.** *MIDI can judge:* evenness of timing and loudness; key-down share of the gap; the fastest rate held cleanly; missed re-strikes (a note that failed to sound shows as a doubled gap). *Rule:* the shared evenness bands; expect key-down of about 60% of the gap and a roughly constant lift time; raise the rate by the tempo rule, with the published one-finger ceiling of 5–7 a second as the top of any ladder. *Out of reach:* whether fingers changed on the key (3-2-1) or one finger repeated, which is the technique being taught; how far the key was allowed to rise. The P-225's own repetition limit is unknown and should be measured before the drill's top rate is set.

- **Trills.** *MIDI can judge:* rate in notes a second; evenness of the gaps (coefficient of variation); balance between the two notes in loudness and in key-down time; overlap between the two keys; whether the rate holds or sags through the trill. *Rule:* clean when the coefficient of variation of the gaps is ≤ 10% and the two notes' mean velocities differ by ≤ 6 units (experts: under 2); report the rate without a pass mark, with 10–12 notes a second as the expert reference and each hand tracked separately because the left is slower even in pianists. *Out of reach:* which finger pair was used (the drill names it, the data cannot confirm it), forearm rotation against finger action, tension.

- **Coordination between the hands, including two against three.** *MIDI can judge:* for unison or parallel figures, the onset difference between the hands note by note (mean and SD), which hand leads, and the loudness balance; for two against three, each onset's position within the cycle and each hand's own evenness. *Rule, hands together:* clean when the median absolute difference is ≤ 15 ms and the SD ≤ 14 ms (amateur level; 8 ms is expert), nearly up to 30 ms, apart beyond; ignore a lead of the louder hand up to 30 ms. *Rule, two against three:* with cycle length T, the three-hand's notes belong at 0, T/3 and 2T/3 and the two-hand's at 0 and T/2; clean when every onset is within 3% of T of its place (36 ms at a 1.2 s cycle; flattening the triplet onto an even grid of four moves its second note by 8% of T, so the band must stay under half of that) and each hand's own gaps vary by ≤ 10%; start at a cycle of 1.2–1.5 s so that the smallest unit (T/6) is at least 200–250 ms, where a counted combined pattern is steadiest, and shorten the cycle from there. *Out of reach:* whether the player is counting the combined pattern or feeling two streams, and any visible tension or mirrored movement between the hands.

- **Dynamic control: a crescendo over a scale.** *MIDI can judge:* whether velocity rises (or falls) steadily; the total change; the largest reversal; whether timing stayed even while the loudness changed. *Rule:* clean when the correlation between note number and velocity is ≥ 0.8, the first-to-last change (averaging the first and last three notes) is ≥ 20 units or a third of the player's calibrated range, and no note falls back by more than 6 units against the trend; timing must still meet its evenness band, since getting faster while getting louder is the usual fault. Under a square-law instrument a doubling of velocity is about 12 dB, but the P-225's own curve is not published, so say "velocity units" and not decibels. *Out of reach:* tone colour, and whether the result sounds "natural": teachers agree on dynamics only about half the time.

- **Dynamic control: voicing the top note of a chord.** *MIDI can judge:* the top note's velocity against the mean of the others, chord by chord; how consistent that difference is; the chord's spread. *Rule:* voiced when the top is ≥ 8 units above the mean of the rest in at least 85% of chords; strongly voiced from about 15 units; do not penalise the top note arriving up to 30 ms early. *Out of reach:* how it was done (weight to the fifth finger, finger angle), and the actual balance in the room, which depends on the piano's voicing and register.

- **Staccato against legato (one in each hand, or alternating).** *MIDI can judge:* each hand's key-down share and overlap separately; whether the hands stay together in time while differing in length; whether the staccato hand's shortness leaks into the legato hand at the same moments. *Rule:* legato hand inside the legato band on at least 85% of transitions; staccato hand key-down ≤ 50% of the gap on at least 85% of notes; hands-together timing by the bands above. *Out of reach:* finger, wrist or arm staccato; the quality of the release (and its speed, if the piano sends a fixed release velocity).

- **Legato pedalling (changing the pedal on each new chord).** *MIDI can judge:* the order and timing of four events at every change: the new chord's press, the pedal's release, the pedal's next press, and the old chord's key releases. *Rule (logical conditions for a clean change, not published thresholds):* (1) the pedal comes up no earlier than 30 ms before the new chord sounds, otherwise there is a hole unless the fingers hold; (2) the old chord's keys are released before the pedal goes down again, otherwise the old harmony is caught and blurs; (3) the pedal goes down again while the new chord's keys are still held, otherwise the new chord is lost; (4) the pedal stays up for at least about 50 ms so the dampers can act. Report which condition failed, in those words. Depth of pedal can be judged only with a continuous pedal. *Out of reach:* how clean the change sounds (the piano's damping and resonance model), heel position and ankle motion, half-pedal depth with a foot switch. Published pedal timing shows large personal differences and no simple rule, so the bands should be loose and the feedback about order, not milliseconds.

### Gaps
- None of the proposed bands has been tested against teacher judgement or against learners at grades 1–5; the anchors are experts, conservatory students, long-term amateurs and tapping studies. They should be treated as starting values and tuned on the owner's own runs.
- No published values exist, that I could find, for: evenness or spread in double-note scales; chord release spread; trill evenness as a coefficient of variation; leap accuracy by distance; clean pedal-change timings in milliseconds; a 50 ms neighbour-key window for slips (Bangert's study used a wider window and gives no value for it).
- The legato upper bound, the widened tolerance at arpeggio position changes, the 10-unit landing-loudness band and the 50 ms minimum pedal-up time are judgement calls with no source.
- Whether the P-225 sends what the release-speed and half-pedal judgements would need is unverified (see the limits section).
