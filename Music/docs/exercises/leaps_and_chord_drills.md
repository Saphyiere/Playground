# Leaps, jumps and chord changes at the piano: how they are taught, what research says, and drills a program can generate and score

## 1. How do leading pedagogues teach leaps: single notes, octaves and chords, stride and waltz bass, and shifts from chord to chord?

### Takeaway
Almost every source teaches the same core routine: split the leap into sideways travel and the keystroke, make the travel far faster than the music needs so the hand arrives early and (in practice) rests on the target keys before playing, judge the landing without correcting it, then shrink the pause until the leap is in time. Around that core they add a low curved path with a vertical landing, a guide inside the hand (thumb, octave span, same finger), practice without looking, and "watch one hand, feel the other" when both hands leap; they disagree on whether a "kick-off" from the first note is real and on which part of the arm drives the leap.

### Cited Findings

**Prepare the next position fast, stop on it, check, then play**
- Graham Fitch's "Quick Cover": hold the first note or chord; move the arm quickly and loosely to the surface of the next chord's keys without playing; check you are centred, with no finger in the cracks between keys or hanging over the edge of a black key; only if the move was fast and accurate, play; if not, do not play, diagnose the error (his example: the thumb-to-second-finger span was too narrow) and retry. — [Fitch, Leaps of Faith: On Practising Waltzes (2012)](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/); [Fitch, Precision Measurement in Jumps (2019)](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- Fitch's stated aim is "a millimeter-accurate measurement of the distance involved across the keyboard and within the hand", and "our only concern is to form the reflexes involved in making the jumps very fast and very accurate"; rhythm is not the goal of these exercises. — [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- Robert Estrin's version: "practice the jump without playing the next notes"; move, stop directly over the notes, check the hand, try again; expect to overshoot or undershoot at first; play only once the hand is set ("Security comes first"); then shorten the pause bit by bit until it is gone; work on one jump at a time. His warning: "If you miss the jump repeatedly, you end up reinforcing the mistake." — [Estrin in Pianist magazine, 12 Aug 2025](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/how-to-play-big-leaps-successfully-on-the-piano); [Estrin, Living Pianos, July 2025](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/effortless-big-piano-leaps-the-one-trick-you-need/)
- Chuan Chang breaks a jump into two principal motions, "A horizontal translation of the hand to the correct position" and "The actual downward motion to play", plus two optional ones, feeling the keys and the take-off. Practise slowly by moving quickly to the target, stopping, feeling the keys and waiting before playing; shorten the wait as the tempo rises. — [Chang, Fundamentals of Piano Practice, 1.III.7.f "Practicing Jumps"](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps); [mirror of the same section](https://mail.pianofundamentals.com/book/en/1.III.7.6)
- Seymour Fink's "Lateral Extension – Preparation Shifts" (as summarised in a 2018 doctoral study of his book) is for leaping passages of single notes, intervals including octaves, or chords: after playing a central key the player prepares the hand for the next note but does not strike, and should "freeze on the aimed key"; the summary says this training makes long-distance shifts very fast. — [Kim, Seymour Fink's Mastering Piano Technique: Understanding Basic Technical Movements, DMA, Univ. of Kansas 2018](https://exercisesforfree.com/pdf/163.pdf) ([KU record](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/a2d01f85-f3fa-44d3-8c2d-7ab5b7998255))
- Alan Belkin (an essay that takes György Sándor's book as its reference): "Touch, then play" — lightly touch the distant note before sounding it, with a tiny pause; "Ideally the arm should be in position before the note in the new region is played"; practise shifts by "blocking", moving into a whole position rather than to a single note. — [Belkin, Technique](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)
- Alan Dorn (LRSM): play the note or chord before the leap, then leap as quickly as possible to the target without sounding it; practise on the key surface without pressing, which builds "prepared" playing. — [Dorn, Piano Athletics: Leaps](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- A Japanese teaching commentary on Burgmüller's "La Styrienne" gives the same advice for its right-hand leaps: practise each leap in segments, placing the finger silently on the target key before pressing it. — [PTNA Piano Encyclopedia, Styrienne (commentary by K. Ooi)](https://enc.piano.or.jp/en/musics/23989)

**One motion, then freeze on whatever you hit**
- Fitch's "Springboarding" is the complement of Quick Cover: rest on the keys, play and propel the hand to the next position in one motion ("do not prepare the position"), then freeze: "The golden rule is to hold on to whatever you land on", right or wrong. If it is wrong, resist correcting; work out why, and repeat from the previous position. — [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/); [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/)
- Dorn's loop for very short sections (as little as half a bar) is "plan, play, judge", re-planning before each repeat, and noting whether a miss was an overshoot or an undershoot. — [Dorn](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)

**Land on part of the chord first; separate the leap from the change of shape**
- Fitch's "Selective Landing": when leaping to a chord, land first on chosen notes (for example the middle note, then the outer two) and fill in the rest afterwards; useful for awkward chords and new hand positions; it need not be rhythmic. His example is the last two bars of Schumann's "Fürchtenmachen" (Kinderszenen). — [Fitch, Managing Leaps: Selective Landing (2013)](https://practisingthepiano.com/managing-leaps-selective-landing/); [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- Dorn: work separately on the leap and on any change of hand shape (his example: triad to octave), then combine them. — [Dorn](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- Before any jump drills, Fitch has the player find the harmonic skeleton: identify the bass notes (little finger, main beats) and the chords (off beats), and play the left-hand chords as a plain progression. — [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)

**Move faster than the music needs**
- Chang: "The first skill to practice is to make the horizontal motion as fast as possible" "so as to reserve enough time to locate the keys after the hand reaches its destination"; he also gives an away-from-the-piano drill (elbow down, forearm forward, flick the hand sideways and relax) and says gains come over days, not in one session. — [Chang 1.III.7.f](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps)
- Fitch: in Quick Cover the tempo is very slow but the movements are very fast, "fast but free and loose". — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/); [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- Belkin: practise leaps slowly, but make the shifts themselves at full speed. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)

**The path the hand takes**
- Chang: "The combined motion should look more like an inverted "U" than an inverted "V""; the U "has short legs and a flat top"; untrained players use a V, which makes accurate landing hard. — [Chang 1.III.7.f](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps)
- Belkin: an upward leap is arced, and "the descent into a note must be fairly vertical, so as not to arrive on the side of the key"; in alternating leaps the arcs flatten and shorten as speed rises. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)
- Heinrich Neuhaus (chapter "On Technique") is reported as saying that the shortest path between two points on the keyboard is a curve, calling the keyboard the realm of "non-Euclidian geometry", and warning that fast leaps of chords are where banging and thumping easily happen. — [Neuhaus, The Art of Piano Playing, ch. 4](http://baes.ua.pt/bitstream/10849/296/12/Chapter%204_On%20Technique.pdf) (excerpt only; translations differ)
- Edna Golandsky (Taubman approach) warns against exaggerating the upward movement: moving directly across saves time and effort. — [Golandsky, How to Leap with Freedom in Chopin's Etude Op. 25 No. 3](https://pianomarvel.com/article/how-to-leap-with-freedom-in-chopins-etude-op-25-no-3)
- Murray McLachlan is reported as saying leaps should feel like single shaped gestures, but that a "wave" approach may not work best where the firmest, most percussive articulation is wanted. — [McLachlan, The nuts and bolts of piano technique: Leaps, International Piano, 2025](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps) (excerpt only)
- Otto Ortmann's 1929 recordings agree with the low, curved, vertical-landing picture; see section 3. — [Ortmann, The Physiological Mechanics of Piano Technique](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)

**What starts the leap, and which part of the arm does it (sources disagree)**
- Golandsky: the common fault is to "barely play the first note and to stretch over to the second note". In a staccato leap the first note sends the hand ("the feeling is one of being automatically sent"); in a legato leap it is "a rotational motion combined with lateral hand and forearm motion". The larger the leap, the shorter the staccato on the first note. — [Golandsky](https://pianomarvel.com/article/how-to-leap-with-freedom-in-chopins-etude-op-25-no-3)
- The Golandsky Institute says the approach avoids stretching and "teaches the correct strategies to make leaps reliable"; "Leaps" is its own volume (1 h 43 min) in the ten-part Taubman Techniques video series. — [Golandsky Institute, The Taubman Approach](https://www.golandskyinstitute.org/resources/); [Univ. of Wisconsin library record of the videos](https://search.library.wisc.edu/catalog/999805800702121) (video listing: excerpt only)
- Chang: "Start the take-off with a small downward and sideways kick of the wrist". — [Chang 1.III.7.f](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps)
- Against this, Ortmann measured the force under the hand as it left for a leap and found no downward "kick-off" at any loudness; he calls the spring-release idea "erroneous" and explains the sensation by the abrupt arrival on the key just before. — [Ortmann 1929, ch. "Lateral Arm-Movement"](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- Fink (via Kim): the forearm's sideways movement "stems from the motion of the upper arm"; wide leaps are hard when the upper arm and elbow stay close to the body, so moving the elbow away from the body (arm abduction) is "the basis for moving sideways at the keyboard". — [Kim 2018](https://exercisesforfree.com/pdf/163.pdf)
- Dorn: "Many leaps are best handled by a shift of the whole arm", finishing the distance with the arm, not the hand; link repeated leaps into one flowing circular motion. A commenter on the same page (Neil Stannard) disagrees: in quick leaps the forearm, hinged at the elbow, is the motor and the upper arm is heavy and slow. — [Dorn, with comments](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- Alan Fraser's site says "Rotation helps you leap with alacrity" and advises keeping the wrist and elbow in when leaping outward. — [Alan Fraser Institute, The Craft of Piano Playing](https://www.alanfraserinstitute.com/craft-of-piano-playing.php)
- Belkin: when moving to a new register, lean toward it during the last notes of the preceding phrase; decide consciously between which two notes each shift happens. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)

**A guide inside the hand: thumb, octave span, same finger**
- Fitch, for waltz basses: add the upper octave to the bass note with the thumb; it "acts as a gauge for the hand and makes the distances feel much smaller". The hand should not stay in octave position in the finished playing, but the practice has a lasting effect. Two more variants: play the bass note an octave lower than written, and omit the bass note, playing only the thumb's upper note and the chords. "These tools apply, of course, to any jump." — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/)
- Fink's "doubly extending lateral" pattern, applied to a leap from a low chord to an octave: play only the bass note with the fifth finger, then aim the thumb at the following octave; said to improve accuracy of large leaps. — [Kim 2018](https://exercisesforfree.com/pdf/163.pdf)
- McLachlan is reported as saying single-note leaps generally work best with the same finger on both notes. — [McLachlan 2025](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps) (excerpt only)

**Not looking: feel, black-key landmarks, eyes closed**
- Chang: "Locate the keys by feeling them before the actual playing"; "Practice feeling the keys whenever possible so that it becomes second nature". — [Chang 1.III.7.f](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps)
- Fitch: practising in the dark or with eyes closed is "a very good practice tool"; it sharpens hearing and touch, and jumps are easier to judge once the eyes are open again. Switch the lights off, because "you will doubtless cheat if you simply close your eyes!" The eye is involved "in so much of what we do, often unnecessarily and distractingly so". — [Fitch, An Energy Saving Tip (2012)](https://practisingthepiano.com/an-energy-saving-tip/)
- McLachlan's reported eyes-closed sequence: first repeat the leap with eyes closed keeping the first note silent and sounding only the second; when that is comfortable, play both. For octaves: work three at a time, stopping on the third, eyes closed, so the hand learns each octave by touch. — [McLachlan, Leaps](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps); [McLachlan, Octaves](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-part-1-octaves) (both excerpt only)
- Dorn: practise with eyes closed to build a feel for the size of the leap and to find notes by touch. — [Dorn](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- Belkin: "An experienced pianist develops an inner, sensory, map of the keyboard"; practise "blind"; keep the fingers close to the keys and the hands lightly in contact with them while reading. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)
- Josh Wright: "try to only look at your music and orient yourself by the three and two black keys on the piano"; this builds a "mental topography of the keyboard". He says he hardly ever looks down when reading "unless there's a big jump or something that I'm a little nervous about". — [Josh Wright interviewed by Musical U, 4 Dec 2018](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/practice-performance-and-powerful-levers-with-josh-wright/)
- Estrin: the ragtime left hand is "all but impossible to play without looking at your hands", so he practises such leaps while keeping his eyes on the score until they can be managed by feel. — [Estrin, Why is it So Hard to Sight-Read Ragtime Music? (2020)](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/why-is-it-so-hard-to-sight-read-ragtime-music/)

**Where the eyes go, and what to do when both hands leap**
- Belkin: look ahead to the goal note; if both hands leap, watch the hand that travels farther; make shifts in both hands together when possible. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)
- Dorn: when both hands leap outward you cannot see both landings, so decide which hand to look at and make sure the other can find its target by feel (his example is the last chord of Chopin's second Scherzo). — [Dorn](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- McLachlan is reported as saying that when the right hand leaps while the left has the melody, focusing on the left hand rather than on the leaps "will (ironically) often make the jumps more successful". — [McLachlan 2025](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps) (excerpt only)

**Lightness, accents and rhythm**
- Ortmann, from recordings: accenting one end of a repeated leap made the movement measurably less accurate, so "if accuracy be the aim in learning a passage that involves leaps and speed, the lightest possible practice will be advisable". — [Ortmann 1929](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- McLachlan is reported as saying leaps should be hidden from the listener and the second note not accented. — [McLachlan 2025](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps) (excerpt only)
- A general-audience exercise set adds two rhythmic variants: jump from beat four to beat one with the upbeat light and the downbeat accented, then the reverse, accent on the upbeat with a light, springy downbeat. — [Piano & Keyboard All-in-One For Dummies, jump exercises](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/piano/jump-exercises-for-the-piano-or-keyboard-203706)
- The same source's image: the hand should look the same before and after each jump, like "a frog jumping from lily pad to lily pad"; before moving, picture the relaxed hand shape, the arc and the finger landing. — [Dummies](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/piano/jump-exercises-for-the-piano-or-keyboard-203706)

**Waltz bass, stride and ragtime left hands**
- Fitch on waltzes: looseness comes first ("A pre-requisite for mobility across the keyboard is physical ease and looseness"); then Quick Cover and Springboarding on the left hand alone, an "arriving early" exercise, selective landing, and the three bass-note variants above. — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/)
- Stride: "The left hand characteristically plays a four-beat pulse with a single bass note (or an octave, major seventh, minor seventh or major tenth interval) on the first and third beats and a chord on the second and fourth beats", and stride left hands "travel greater distances on the keyboard" than ragtime ones. — [Wikipedia, Stride (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stride_%28music%29)
- Jerry Kovarsky's beginner pattern: root, a simple triad voicing above it, the fifth, the triad again; "take it slow and get used to the distances your hand has to go between the bass notes and the chords"; the same patterns work in 3/4. — [Kovarsky, The Basics of Stride Piano, Yamaha, 2022](https://hub.yamaha.com/keyboards/k-how-to/the-basics-of-stride-piano)
- Jonny May simplifies the landing: jump from the root to only the 3rd and 7th of the chord, because two notes are easier to land on than three and the chords stay near each other. — [Piano With Jonny, How to Play Stride Piano](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/learn-how-to-play-stride-piano/)
- PianoGroove: "It can be challenging to move between the chords whilst keeping a steady pulse"; "Focus on accuracy and play as slowly as needed". — [PianoGroove, Beginner Stride Piano Lesson](https://www.pianogroove.com/?p=13849)
- Estrin describes the ragtime left hand as "octaves alternating with chords" and adds that once memorised, ragtime is "not particularly difficult to play". — [Estrin 2020](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/why-is-it-so-hard-to-sight-read-ragtime-music/)

### Inferences
- The methods form a natural sequence that a program can follow: (1) know the two positions as shapes and harmony; (2) fast travel, stop on the keys, check, play (Quick Cover and its relatives); (3) one motion and freeze on the result (Springboarding); (4) shrink the pause until the leap is in time; (5) take the eyes away. Fitch himself names stages 2 and 3 as separate tools, and Estrin describes stage 4.
- "Prepare and stop" and "one curved motion" are not rival schools. Ortmann's recordings (section 3) show players doing the first at slow tempos and the second at fast ones, so the stop is a practice device that disappears with speed.
- The consistent rule "do not correct a miss, hold it and diagnose it" (Fitch, Dorn, Estrin's warning about reinforcing mistakes) argues for scoring only the first landing and showing by how far it missed, not for letting the player fish for the note.
- The guide ideas (thumb octave, same finger, aim the thumb) all turn a leap of unknown size into a known hand span plus a smaller move; that suggests ordering drills from "leap measured by the hand" (octave-based) to "leap measured by the arm alone".
- On the kick-off, the only measured evidence found (Ortmann) says there is no downward push; Chang's "kick" and Golandsky's "being sent" are best read as sensations, and a program should not try to teach or score a push-off.

## 2. Which exercises, studies and pieces are standard for leaps and chord work at late-beginner and intermediate level, and what does each demand?

### Takeaway
The classic exercise books train fingers, scales and octaves far more than leaps; leap and chord-change practice at this level comes mostly from pieces (waltz and dance left hands, character pieces with a hand that jumps) and from exam technical lists of triads and inversions. I could confirm what a number of items demand, but not a number-by-number list for Czerny, Burgmüller or Duvernoy.

### Cited Findings
- Roskell's graded technique series starts leaps very early: "Essential Piano Technique Primer A: Hop, skip and jump" and "Level 1: Leaping Ahead", the latter covering from hands-together playing to Grade 1. — [Forsyths listing, Level 1](https://www.forsyths.co.uk/music/sheet-music/instruments/keyboard-instruments/piano/167566-essential-piano-technique-level-1-leaping-ahead-9790577023465.html); [Forsyths listing, Primer A](https://www.forsyths.co.uk/music/sheet-music/instruments/keyboard-instruments/piano/167564-essential-piano-technique-primer-a-hop-skip-and-jump-9790577023441.html) (titles and level description: excerpt only; the books' contents were not seen)
- J. S. Bach, Musette from the Anna Magdalena Notebook: Estrin's example of a student piece with tricky jumps across the keyboard, a step up from the Minuet in G, which stays in one position. — [Estrin in Pianist magazine](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/how-to-play-big-leaps-successfully-on-the-piano)
- Burgmüller, "La Styrienne", Op. 100 No. 14: marked crotchet = 176; the hard places are right-hand leaps in bars 29, 31 and 33; the left hand is to be light and detached. One database rates it late intermediate. — [PTNA Piano Encyclopedia](https://enc.piano.or.jp/en/musics/23989); [Piano Music Database](https://pianomusicdatabase.netlify.app/work/Austrian%20Dance%20Op.%20100%2C%20No.%2014-Friedrich%20Burgm%C3%BCller?id=528) (rating: excerpt only)
- Max Bruch, Moderato, Op. 12 No. 4 (ABRSM Grade 6): left-hand jumps from bass notes on the main beats to chords on the off beats; Fitch's worked example for his three jump tools. — [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- Chopin, Waltz in A flat: Fitch's example for waltz-bass practice (bass note, then chords). Schumann, "Fürchtenmachen" (Kinderszenen), last two bars: his example of a leap to an awkward chord. — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/); [Fitch 2013](https://practisingthepiano.com/managing-leaps-selective-landing/)
- Scott Joplin, *School of Ragtime* (first published 1908): six exercises, free on IMSLP. Ragtime left hands are "octaves alternating with chords". — [IMSLP, School of Ragtime](https://www.imslp.org/wiki/School_of_Ragtime_%28Joplin,_Scott%29) (excerpt only); [Estrin 2020](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/why-is-it-so-hard-to-sight-read-ragtime-music/)
- Stride left hand: bass note, octave or tenth on beats one and three, chord on two and four; a beginner form is root, triad, fifth, triad. — [Wikipedia, Stride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stride_%28music%29); [Kovarsky 2022](https://hub.yamaha.com/keyboards/k-how-to/the-basics-of-stride-piano)
- A general exercise set orders jump practice as note-to-note, note-to-chord, chord-to-chord, then accented versions (beat four to beat one). — [Piano & Keyboard All-in-One For Dummies](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/piano/jump-exercises-for-the-piano-or-keyboard-203706)
- Fink's own leap exercises are the "Lateral Forearm Skip" ("in essence a leap exercise"), "Lateral Extension – Preparation Shifts", a lateral figure-eight pattern and a "doubly extending lateral" pattern. — [Kim 2018](https://exercisesforfree.com/pdf/163.pdf)
- Hanon, *The Virtuoso Pianist*: Part 1 (Nos. 1–20) is finger patterns, Part 2 (21–43) scales and arpeggios, Part 3 (44–60) repeated notes, scales in thirds and octaves, and tremolos; the article lists no exercise for leaps or chord changes, and records criticism of such exercises (Taubman is cited as saying they do more harm than good). — [Wikipedia, The Virtuoso Pianist in 60 Exercises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Virtuoso_Pianist_in_60_Exercises)
- Duvernoy, *École primaire*, Op. 176: 25 beginner studies whose publisher's topic list includes chords and broken chords, Alberti bass, alternating hands and repeated notes; no leaps are listed. — [Schott, Elementary Studies Op. 176](https://www.schott-music.com/en/elementary-studies-noc303221.html) (excerpt only)
- Cortot, *Rational Principles of Pianoforte Technique*: retailer descriptions say it ends with the technique of extensions, the technique of the wrist and the execution of chords. — [Presto Music listing](https://prestomusic.com/sheet-music/products/7252770--cortot-rational-principles-of-piano-technique) (excerpt only)
- Malwine Brée's *The Groundwork of the Leschetizky Method* (1902, public domain) has chapters on preparation for broken triads, chords, and the upper part in chord playing. — [IMSLP record](https://imslp.org/wiki/The_Groundwork_of_the_Leschetizky_Method_%28Br%C3%A9e,_Malwine%29) (chapter titles: excerpt only)
- Czerny: a book chapter says he set out to develop the player's ability to execute leaps; nothing I could open named which numbers of Op. 599, 139 or 849 train them. — [Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/e/9781317113560/chapters/10.4324%2F9781315589596-18) (excerpt only)
- Exam technical lists are the main source of standard chord exercises: RCM requires broken and solid triads with inversions from its first levels (details in section 4). — [RCM Piano Syllabus, 2022 Edition](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)
- Where the ladder leads (advanced, named only as markers): Mozart Sonata K. 331 variation 4 and Beethoven "Pathétique" first movement for hand-crossing jumps, Chopin Ballade No. 1 coda — [Chang](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps); Chopin Scherzo No. 2 — [Estrin](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/how-to-play-big-leaps-successfully-on-the-piano); Chopin Étude Op. 25 No. 3 — [Golandsky](https://pianomarvel.com/article/how-to-leap-with-freedom-in-chopins-etude-op-25-no-3); Liszt "La Campanella" and the end of Schumann's Fantasie — [Ortmann](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583); Liszt Hungarian Rhapsody No. 6 — [Estrin 2020](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/why-is-it-so-hard-to-sight-read-ragtime-music/).

### Inferences
- For a late beginner the realistic sources of leap practice are (a) left-hand bass-then-chord accompaniments (waltz, march, ragtime in slow motion), (b) pieces where one hand changes register (the Musette type), and (c) exam triad-and-inversion lists. A generator should produce exactly these three textures.
- Because the finger-exercise books do not cover leaps, there is no public-domain "Hanon for leaps" to transcribe; generated drills fill a real hole.
- "La Styrienne" at crotchet = 176 shows how quickly a waltz tempo turns a modest leap into a fast one: a beat lasts about a third of a second.

### Gaps
- I could not verify which individual studies in Czerny Op. 599/139/849, Burgmüller Op. 100 (other than No. 14) or Duvernoy Op. 176 use left-hand bass-and-chord leaps; the one site with per-study notes carried an AI-generated disclaimer and I did not use it.
- Dohnányi's *Essential Finger Exercises* could not be checked (search allowance exhausted). From memory, and unverified: it is about finger independence, not leaps; Czerny's Op. 740 contains a study whose title mentions skips. A search excerpt whose source I could not identify described Czerny's Op. 399 as a left-hand school with long leaps and octave passages.
- I did not find which graded exam pieces at ABRSM Grades 1–5 are chosen for leaps; the grade given above for the Bruch piece is Fitch's statement.

## 3. What does research say about speed and accuracy in keyboard leaps, the roles of vision, touch and landmarks, playing without looking, and looking at the keyboard in sight-reading?

### Takeaway
There is very little direct research on piano leaps: one controlled training study (2014), Ortmann's 1929 recordings, and an exploratory blindfold study (2024); no study was found that tests Fitts' law at the keyboard or shows that practising without looking improves leap accuracy. What exists says: one short session of 160 repetitions does not reduce errors, mixing distances costs nothing, loud or accented leaps are less accurate, removing vision raises note errors even in easy material, and better readers look down less and know the keyboard by touch.

### Cited Findings

**The one training study of a piano leap**
- Bangert, Wiedemann and Jabusch (2014) trained 36 music students (piano as second study) on a left-hand leap between two octave grips (thumb and little finger), white keys only, 15 semitones apart, to be made in 187.5 ms (a semiquaver at 80 BPM); a 19-semitone leap was the untrained transfer test. Before training the leaps actually took about 280 ms. — [Bangert et al., Front. Hum. Neurosci. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)
- Errors were counted from MIDI: any required note missed, any slip note beside a correct one, and any extra wrong note from 500 ms before to 500 ms after the leap, all weighted equally, summed over 15 repetitions. Leap execution time, loudness (key velocity) and the loudness difference between the two ends of the leap were secondary measures; players were told to keep the two ends equally loud. — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393); [full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598/full)
- Three groups practised for 30 minutes (160 trials): the target only, massed; the target only, in spaced one-minute blocks; or four distances (8, 12, 15 and 22 semitones, 40 trials each). No group improved significantly from before to after training or a day later, and the groups did not differ. The median before training was 21 errors per 15 leaps (range 2 to 57); the best post-training rate was about 0.26 errors per note, which the authors think may be a floor. — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)
- The mixed-distance group matched the others on the target leap with only a quarter of its practice spent on it; the predicted next-day advantage of varied practice did not appear. The authors call the support for variable practice "partial" and the study a pilot (one short session, 12 per group, no feedback other than the piano itself). — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)
- Slower execution did not buy accuracy in their data: "a slower execution of the movement does not seem to facilitate hit rate". — [Bangert et al., full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598/full)
- The paper does not discuss Fitts' law; it treats the leap as a time-constrained reaching movement modelled on a 1977 reaching experiment (McCracken and Stelmach), and does not say whether players could see their hands. — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)

**Recordings of the arm's path (Ortmann 1929)**
- Ortmann traced the hand with a pantograph and photographs. A co-ordinated leap is a low curve with the change of direction shifted to one side so the hand meets the key vertically; stopping and poising over the key is "wasted work" at speed. The outward and return paths differ: an octave leap back and forth "is not a single arc of a circle". — [Ortmann 1929, ch. "Lateral Arm-Movement", pp. 160–163](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- Speed changes the form. With a twelfth taken at two seconds per shift, players moved the hand horizontally close to the keys, then lifted and dropped (a "double movement", the method of preparation); as speed rose to about a third of a second per stroke this merged into the single curve. — [Ortmann 1929, pp. 168–169](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- Loudness raises the curve without making it more angular (octave leap pp versus ff). Two players were sure they had not lifted higher in a crescendo; the photographs showed they had, which Ortmann takes as proof that pianists' reports of their own movements are unreliable. — [Ortmann 1929, p. 167](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- Accenting one end of rapidly repeated octave leaps made the lines of motion diverge, "an inaccuracy in the movement", absent when the two ends were equally loud; hence his advice to practise leaps as lightly as possible. — [Ortmann 1929, p. 172](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)
- Arm length matters: measured adult arms varied by about six inches, roughly an octave of keyboard, enough to affect accuracy in rapid crossings. — [Ortmann 1929](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583)

**Speed–accuracy laws, and how far they apply**
- Fitts' law predicts movement time from an index of difficulty, ID = log2(2D/W), with MT = a + b·ID; it describes rapid aiming at a target of width W at distance D, and its predictions weaken when distance and width vary widely. — [Wikipedia, Fitts's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitts%27s_law)
- Piano keys as targets: octave span 164–165 mm, white keys about 23.5 mm wide at the front, black keys averaging 13.7 mm. A fingering model takes 13.7 mm as the average distance per semitone. — [Wikipedia, Musical keyboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_keyboard); [Parncutt et al. 1997](https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/_Persoenliche_Webseite/parncutt_richard/Pdfs/PaSlClRaDe97_FingeringModel.pdf)
- For movements that must arrive at a set time, as a leap in rhythm must, Schmidt and colleagues (1979) found endpoint variability rises with distance and falls with movement time, a linear trade-off that fits timed movements better than Fitts' logarithmic one. — [Schmidt et al. 1979, summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/motor-output-variability-a-theory-for-the-accuracy-of-rapid-motor-acts/812079394007810049-7281); ["A mathematical description of the speed/accuracy trade-off of aimed movement"](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02300015) (both excerpt only; the excerpt did not show which page the linear-versus-logarithmic sentence came from)
- A textbook adds a wrinkle from the same group: for movements that are already rapid, going faster can make timing and endpoint more consistent. — [Fairbrother, Fundamentals of Motor Behavior, excerpt](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/the-speed-accuracy-trade-off)
- Trained musicians may not show the trade-off. Nine cellists shifting between notes kept endpoint variability roughly constant as distance and elbow speed increased; notes with a physical landmark (the thumb meeting the join of neck and body) were landed more consistently and allowed faster movement; the more accurate players also had better pitch perception. — [Chen, Woollacott & Pologe, Exp. Brain Res. 2006](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00221-006-0483-x) (abstract via excerpt)
- The same group later had nine cellists shift back and forth between two notes once a second for two minutes. With sound, landings were precise and stable; with eyes closed and the string muffled, positions drifted, often outside the pitch window, behaving like a random walk. "Somatic cues alone were inadequate"; the shift with no body landmark was the most dependent on hearing. — [Chen et al., Front. Hum. Neurosci. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3728976)

**Vision in aiming**
- A review of a century of aiming research keeps Woodworth's two parts (a planned initial impulse, then correction using feedback) and reports that when vision is reliably available or absent, having it improves accuracy even for movements as short as 100 ms; earlier estimates of the visual loop were 190–260 ms. — [Elliott, Helsen & Chua 2001](https://typeset.io/pdf/a-century-later-woodworth-s-1899-two-component-model-of-goal-lebbms9vvm.pdf) (excerpt only)
- Without vision, the felt position of the hand is less precise sideways (in azimuth) than towards or away from the shoulder, and less precise farther from the shoulder. — [van Beers, Sittig & Denier van der Gon 1998](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007%2Fs002210050525) (excerpt only; I could not get the sizes in centimetres)

**Playing without seeing the hands**
- Seventeen experienced pianists (aged 33–54) played Hanon No. 1 from memory normally, blindfolded, on the closed fallboard, and both. Blindfolded playing produced significantly more note errors than normal playing, in an exercise with no leaps at all. The authors call the study exploratory. — [Nunes-Silva et al. 2024, DOI 10.1177/1321103X241241192](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/effects-of-altered-sensory-feedback-on-piano-performance-errors-an-exploratory-study) (journal not named on the page; the ISSN is that of Research Studies in Music Education)
- Sight-reading is disrupted when sight of the keyboard is prevented, more so for inexperienced readers (Banton 1995; Lehmann & Ericsson 1996, as reported by later authors). — [Ronkainen & Kuusi 2009](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/20949) (their summary of the earlier work: excerpt only)
- Banton is also cited for the observation that sight-readers look down at the keyboard to check the position of their fingers. — [Qi & Adachi 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9360971)
- In interviews after sight-reading, five pianists said they used sight of the keyboard and fingers for discrete movements, mainly when they could not grasp the passage from the score. — [Ronkainen & Kuusi 2009](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/20949)

**Looking at the keyboard while reading**
- Twenty-two pianists sight-read a short Bartók piece four times with two minutes' practice between. More proficient players made far fewer glances at the keyboard (r = −.77). Glances averaged about 0.49 s each, fell with practice (5.27, 4.62, 4.27, 4.39 across the four trials) and were most frequent in the two developmental sections, which contain the jumps (7.24 and 5.85 against 2.98–3.59 elsewhere). — [Cara, J. Eye Movement Research 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10696908)
- The same paper's review: glances are needed where hand displacements, register changes and hand crossings are frequent, and can disrupt flow or encoding. — [Cara 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10696908)
- When readers are unsure of a note they "habitually look down at the keyboard", which forces pauses. — [Qi & Adachi, Front. Psychol. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9360971)
- Upcoming skips pull the eyes ahead: in simple melodies, notes after a larger interval were looked at earlier and with longer incoming eye movements. — [Huovinen, Ylitalo & Puurtinen 2018](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/4094) (excerpt only)
- Older teaching research, as summarised by a pedagogy review: the fewer times a student looks down, the better the sight-reading (Fuszek 1977); "keyboard psychomotor skill is the most important factor in sight-reading achievement", ahead of note reading (Eaton 1978); locating notes relative to the black keys and feeling intervals builds a map of the keyboard (Hilley 1977); transposing and playing with eyes closed are ways to develop feel for the keyboard (Dumn 1984). — [Hardy, Teaching Sight-Reading at the Piano, Piano Pedagogy Forum 1998](http://sightreadingmastery.com/dianne-hardy-teaching-sight-reading-at-the-piano/research.htm)
- A literature review says expert sight-readers have a stronger tactile command of keyboard geography. — [Wristen 2005](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1005&context=musicfacpub) (excerpt only)

**How to schedule repetitions**
- Ten clarinettists practised some material in one block and some interleaved in short turns; where raters heard a difference, the interleaved material was rated better, and players reported better goal-setting and mistake-spotting. Small sample, uneven rater agreement. — [Carter & Grahn, Front. Psychol. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027) (abstract via excerpt)

**What can be measured from key presses**
- Notes written as a chord are not struck together: the emphasised voice sounds about 30 ms early at the hammers, but in 22 skilled pianists this lead shrank almost to zero at the moment the fingers met the keys; it comes from the louder note's faster hammer. Deliberate bass-before-melody spreading between the hands is larger, over 50 ms. — [Goebl, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- Articulation has been measured from MIDI as key overlap time between consecutive notes (positive for legato, negative for detached); staccato notes lasted about 40% of the time to the next note in one study. — [Bresin & Battel 2000](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1076/jnmr.29.3.211.3092) (excerpt only)
- Scale-analysis software has used MIDI to report velocity, note duration, note overlap and inter-onset timing back to pianists, who turned out to hold wrong ideas about their own playing. — [Riley, J. Technology in Music Learning 2007](https://www.wpta.info/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/JTML-2007.pdf)
- A fingering model scores changes of hand position by the distance the hand travels in semitones ("the distance in semitones traveled by the hand's center of gravity ... is a reasonable measure of the difficulty of the change") and gives comfortable and maximum spans per finger pair; for thumb to fifth finger, relaxed up to 10 semitones, comfortable up to 13, practical maximum 15. — [Parncutt, Sloboda, Clarke, Raekallio & Desain, Music Perception 1997](https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/_Persoenliche_Webseite/parncutt_richard/Pdfs/PaSlClRaDe97_FingeringModel.pdf)
- A 2026 pilot of a browser tool giving visual feedback on piano practice reports gains in accuracy measures but little in timing measures. — [SciTePress 2026](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2026/150643/pdf/index.html) (excerpt only)

### Inferences
- A leap in rhythm is a timed movement, so the better guide to its difficulty is Schmidt's relation, not Fitts': expected scatter grows with distance divided by the time allowed. A useful single number for a generated leap is therefore "average hand speed required" = distance ÷ travel time. Illustrations (my arithmetic, using 13.7 mm per semitone): Bangert's leap is about 206 mm in 187.5 ms, about 1.1 m/s (0.73 m/s at the 280 ms the students actually took), and their error rates ran from about 0.26 per note at best to about 0.35 per note at the pre-training median (21 errors over 60 notes, my division); an octave in half a second is 0.33 m/s; successive triad inversions (41–69 mm apart) with half a second of rest are 0.08–0.14 m/s.
- Fitts' index still orders targets by size: for an octave leap, log2(2×165/23.5) ≈ 3.8 bits to a white key and log2(2×165/13.7) ≈ 4.6 bits to a black key; two octaves to a white key ≈ 4.8 bits. So "landing on a black key" is worth roughly as much added difficulty as doubling the distance. This is a calculation, not a tested result.
- Because experienced pianists made more wrong notes blindfolded even in a five-finger exercise, a learner's accuracy will drop when not looking; a drill should score looking and not-looking separately and expect the second to lag.
- The cello result (accurate shifts need hearing, and landmarks help) and the pedagogy (feel the black keys) point the same way: without sight, accuracy is held by re-anchoring to something felt or heard on every landing. Blind there-and-back drills should watch for drift over repetitions, not only for single misses.
- One 30-minute, 160-repetition session changed nothing measurable in music students, and Chang says gains appear over days; progress should be judged across days, with short daily doses.
- Mixed distances were no worse than one distance for the trained leap, and interleaving looked at least as good in the clarinet study, so a generator can vary distance and key freely instead of drilling one leap in a block.
- Ortmann's finding that players misreport their own lift, and Riley's that they misjudge their own scales, support showing measured feedback (which key, how early, how loud) over asking the player how it felt.
- Chord notes pressed by a skilled hand are nearly simultaneous at the keys, with tens of milliseconds appearing only through loudness differences; so a spread of a few tens of milliseconds is normal and a spread beyond about 50 ms is heard as a deliberate roll.

### Gaps
- No study was found applying Fitts' law to piano leaps, and none measuring pianists' gaze during leaps specifically (Cara's sections contain jumps, but glances were not analysed by leap).
- No controlled study was found showing that practising leaps without looking improves accuracy, with or without sight; the support is pedagogical opinion plus the correlational sight-reading work.
- I could not open Banton (1995), Lehmann & Ericsson (1996), Lannert & Ullman (1945) or Wristen (2005); their findings are reported second-hand.
- Nunes-Silva et al. (2024) was read from an abstract page; no numbers were available.
- I did not obtain figures for proprioceptive precision, for the general finding that gaze reaches a target before the hand, or for Goebl and Palmer's work on touch and timing; these would strengthen the "look first, then leap" and "touch then play" advice.
- Nothing was found on MIDI timing error on Android, which bounds how finely chord spread can be measured.

## 4. How are chords taught as technique, in what order for adults, how is success judged, and is there a measured chord-change drill for piano?

### Takeaway
Adult methods introduce chords as left-hand accompaniment in the order tonic, dominant seventh, subdominant in C, then the same in G and other keys, then blocked versus broken; exam technique (RCM) drills every triad broken and solid through its inversions from the first levels at stated metronome marks, adding cadences at Level 5 and four-note chords after that; college class-piano tests the cadence I–IV–I–V7–I in all keys. Success is judged by right notes from memory at a steady minimum tempo with good tone and fingering. I found no named, timed chord-change drill for piano like the guitar's One Minute Changes; RCM's metronome-rated solid triads are the nearest thing.

### Cited Findings

**Order of introduction in adult methods**
- Alfred's Basic Adult Piano Course, Lesson Book 1, in order: two chords for the left hand, C major and G7; the F major chord for the left hand; C and F chords for the right hand; G position, with G major and D7 chords for the left hand; block chords and broken chords; later the primary chords in D minor. A review of the All-in-One edition says it centres on I, IV and V7 in the commoner keys. — [Sheet Music Plus contents listing](https://www.sheetmusicplus.com/en/product/alfred-s-basic-adult-piano-course-lesson-book-book-1-3659208.html) (excerpt only)
- Faber's Adult Piano Adventures All-in-One Book 1 runs through pentascales, intervals and the C major scale (Unit 12) to the G7 chord (Unit 13), primary chords in C major (I, IV, V7), the G major scale and primary chords in G major. The publisher describes the book as "the fundamentals of music notation, chord playing, and musical form". — [Faber, Unit 13: The G7 Chord](https://archive.pianoadventures.com/blog/videos/unit-13-the-g7-chord-adult-piano-adventures-book-1/); [BYU-Idaho video, Unit 12: The C Major Scale](https://video.byui.edu/media/t/1_12yd9djf/10300902) (title: excerpt only); [Calgary Public Library contents record](https://link.calgarylibrary.ca/resource/LqBElQItnpc) (contents: excerpt only); [publisher's description](https://checkout.pianoadventures.com/products/adult-piano-adventures-all-in-one-course-book-1)

**Exam syllabuses**
- RCM (2022 syllabus), chords by level, from a teacher's chart checked against the syllabus:
  - Preparatory A: a triad sequence, broken and solid, hands separately, one octave, stepping up the white keys (broken in triplets at crotchet = 60, solid at 72).
  - Preparatory B: tonic triads broken, root position and inversions, hands separately, one octave (triplets at 50).
  - Level 1: tonic triads broken (50) and solid (100), root position then inversions, hands separately, one octave; C, G, F major and A, E, D minor.
  - Level 2: the same at 60 and 112, in more keys.
  - Level 3: hands separately over two octaves (69 and 120).
  - Level 4: hands together over two octaves (60 and 120).
  - Level 5: tonic triads with inversions, hands together, two octaves, ending with a I–V–I cadence; dominant sevenths with inversions, broken and solid, hands separately.
  - Level 6: adds leading-note diminished sevenths.
  - Level 7: four-note tonic chords broken, hands together, ending I–IV–V–I; sevenths hands together.
  - Levels 8–10: four-note chords with longer cadences, broken, solid and in a broken alternate-note pattern.
  — [Joy Morin's RCM 2022 technical requirements charts](https://colorinmypiano.com/wp-content/files/RCM_2022_Technical_Requirements_Charts_Prep-10.pdf); [RCM Piano Syllabus, 2022 Edition](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)
- The syllabus itself distinguishes "Solid/blocked triads with rests (Levels 1–4)" from "Solid/blocked triads without rests (Levels 5–6)", and lists the cadences as I–V–I (Levels 5–6), I–IV–V–I (Level 7), a version with a cadential six-four (Level 8) and I–vi–IV–V–I with six-four and seventh (Levels 9–10). — [RCM Piano Syllabus 2022, pp. 120–123](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)
- Small-hand alternatives are built in: at Level 9 a broken alternate-note pattern may replace solid four-note chords. — [RCM chart](https://colorinmypiano.com/wp-content/files/RCM_2022_Technical_Requirements_Charts_Prep-10.pdf)
- ABRSM: a review of the 2021 syllabus says broken chords were dropped entirely at Grade 1, with one-octave arpeggios hands separately in their place. — [David Barton, ABRSM Piano Syllabus 2021/22: Publication Review](https://www.davidbartonmusic.co.uk/?p=7237) (excerpt only)
- Trinity's technical work is scales, arpeggios and exercises; the exercises "focus on tone, balance, voicing, co-ordination, finger & wrist strength and flexibility". Chords and cadences are not mentioned. — [Trinity, Introduction to technical work](https://www.trinitycollege.com/qualifications/music/grade-exams/piano/resources/piano-technical-work/introduction-technical-work)

**College class piano (adult beginners who are musicians)**
- The standard test piece is the cadence I–IV–I–V7–I in all major keys (and i–iv–i–V7–i in minor), with harmonisation of a melody. One college stages it as the progression in all major keys, then with all inversions in C, G, D, A, then E, B, F. Another has the right hand play blocked chords while the left plays roots only. Harder versions add vi and ii. — [Longwood University](https://www.longwood.edu/music/student-resources/assessments/keyboard-proficiency-requirements/); [Snow College](https://snow.edu/offices/advisement/downloads/pianoproficiencyrequirements.pdf); [Oklahoma Baptist University](https://www.okbu.edu/fine-arts/music/documents/obu-piano-proficiency-2022.pdf) (all excerpt only)
- Stated standards include "I-vi-IV-ii-V7-I, all keys, hands separately, mm.=60, one chord per beat", and a minimum of crotchet = 108 elsewhere. — [University of Utah](https://www.music.utah.edu/documents/undergraduate-forms/PianoProficiencyRequirement2017-2018.pdf); [Pittsburg State University](https://www.pittstate.edu/humanities/_files/documents/music/departmental/piano-proficiency-examination-requirements1.pdf) (both excerpt only)

**How chords and changes are practised**
- Inversions change the fingering: in the right hand 1-2-5 is used for a first-inversion triad, and in the left hand 1-2-5 is kept for second inversion. Traditional inversion exercises take one chord through root position, first and second inversion, slowly. — [Piano With Jonny, Top Piano Chord Inversion Exercise](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/top-piano-chord-inversion-exercise/)
- The "21 inversion exercise": start on the tonic triad in root position; raise the top note to the next scale note, then the middle note, then the bottom note, and repeat until the tonic triad returns an octave higher. This passes through all three positions of all seven triads of the key and can be done in any major key as a daily warm-up. — [Piano With Jonny](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/top-piano-chord-inversion-exercise/)
- Smooth movement means keeping chords close: "all of the chords are near each other" is the stated goal for a musical left hand. — [Piano With Jonny, stride lesson](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/learn-how-to-play-stride-piano/)
- A chord-inversion guide gives the method as: find the notes the previous chord shares with the next, keep them, and move the others the shortest distance; practise without pedal first so untidy changes are not hidden. — [MusicRadar, How to play chord inversions](https://musicradar.com/how-to/how-to-play-chord-inversions-on-a-piano-or-midi-keyboard) (excerpt only; the excerpt did not make clear which of two guides each sentence came from)
- Roskell's book gives chords four sections (fundamentals, cantabile, detached, advanced); the fundamentals cover "Preparing and practising chords", "Playing well co-ordinated chords" including "Synchronising chords", and widely spaced chords and hand size. — [Pianodao review of The Complete Pianist](https://pianodao.com/2020/05/07/penelope-roskells-complete-pianist/)
- Belkin: align the arm with the average centre of the fingers in a chord; "Chords which are unvoiced – all the notes are played equally – sound brittle and harsh". — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)

**How success is judged**
- RCM: technical tests "must be played from memory"; "Metronome speeds are intended as a guideline for the minimum tempo of each requirement"; all patterns are to be played "ascending and descending, with good tone and logical fingering, at a steady tempo". — [RCM Piano Syllabus 2022, pp. 7 and 120](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)
- Class piano: a set tempo with one chord per beat (above). — [University of Utah](https://www.music.utah.edu/documents/undergraduate-forms/PianoProficiencyRequirement2017-2018.pdf) (excerpt only)
- Notes sounding together: skilled pianists' fingers meet the keys of a chord almost simultaneously. — [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)

**A measured chord-change drill?**
- The guitar drill: choose two chords, set a one-minute timer, and count the changes; the target is 30 complete back-and-forth changes, or 60 counting each arrival, one a second; log the number and try to beat it; pair each new chord with every chord already known. — [JustinGuitar, One Minute Changes](https://justinguitar.com/guitar-lessons/one-minute-changes-exercise-b1-110) (excerpt only; the page was blocked)
- Two searches for a piano equivalent returned only guitar material. — no piano source found.

### Inferences
- The common order across sources is: tonic triad in root position; a compact dominant seventh and subdominant reached with little movement; the same in G and F; blocked versus broken; triads and inversions in every key as technique; cadences in every key; four-note chords (dominant and diminished sevenths, then tonic four-note chords). A chord ladder for Prima can follow it directly.
- The two traditions train different things. Adult methods and class-piano cadences train near changes (common notes held, small moves); RCM's inversions and a root-position progression train shifts of the whole hand with a change of shape. The owner's "chord jumps" are the second kind, but the first is what makes real accompaniments smooth, so both belong.
- RCM's solid triads "with rests" are in effect a metronome-rated chord-change drill: if each chord is a crotchet followed by a crotchet rest (the chart's note-value symbols suggest this, but they were garbled in extraction), chords arrive every two beats, 50 a minute at crotchet = 100 (Level 1) and 60 a minute at 120 (Levels 3–4). Sixty landings a minute is also the guitar drill's target, which gives a defensible first benchmark for a piano version.
- "Good tone, steady tempo, logical fingering" can be approximated from key data as: all notes correct on first landing, onsets close together, loudness even or deliberately voiced, and chord-to-chord timing steady. Fingering cannot be seen from MIDI.

### Gaps
- No named or published one-minute chord-change drill for piano was found; absence of evidence only (two searches before the allowance ran out).
- I could not open ABRSM's own syllabus or marking criteria, so the statement that ABRSM practical grades test scales and arpeggios but not chord progressions rests on a reviewer's excerpt and is otherwise unverified.
- The exact voicings the adult methods use for their first G7 and F chords were not verified (from memory: a three-note G7 with B at the bottom and F with C at the bottom, both a small move from C–E–G).
- Full unit lists for the Faber and Alfred books were not retrieved; the orders above come from catalogue excerpts.
- RCM note values for solid chords at Levels 5–10 were unreadable in the extracted chart, so only the tempo figures for Levels 1–4 are used.

## 5. Which drill designs suit late beginners and intermediates, what makes each easier or harder, what can be measured from key presses, and in what order should they come?

### Takeaway
The sources supply a small set of well-described drills (cover-then-play, springboard-and-freeze, selective landing, octave-gauge waltz basses, eyes-closed leaps, solid triads through inversions at a metronome mark, cadence loops, one-minute changes). A program can generate all of them from a few parameters (hand, start, target, distance, shape change, time allowed, look condition) and score first-landing accuracy, by how far a miss missed, how tightly a chord's notes arrive, travel time, and loudness evenness; the proposed order runs from finding keys by feel, through same-shape hops and inversions, to bass-and-chord left hands, wide leaps and both hands.

### Cited Findings

**Drills taken from named sources (each described in words; the adaptation to an app is discussed under Inferences)**
- S1 Quick Cover: play position A and hold; move fast to the surface of position B without sounding it; check; play only if centred; otherwise go back and repeat. Any hand, any pair of positions, no rhythm. — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/); [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- S2 Springboarding: play A and leap to B in one motion, sound it, and hold whatever was hit; if wrong, do not correct, return to A. — [Fitch 2019](https://practisingthepiano.com/precision-measurement-in-jumps/)
- S3 Selective Landing: leap to a chord but sound only chosen notes first (outer notes, or the middle note), then add the rest. — [Fitch 2013](https://practisingthepiano.com/managing-leaps-selective-landing/)
- S4 Waltz-bass variants, left hand, bass note on beat one and chords on beats two and three: (a) add the octave above the bass with the thumb; (b) play the bass an octave lower than written; (c) leave the bass out and play the thumb's upper note and the chords. — [Fitch 2012](https://practisingthepiano.com/leaps-of-faith-on-practising-waltzes/)
- S5 Jump without playing, then shrink the pause: one jump at a time; land over the notes silently; then play after the pause; shorten the pause step by step until it is in rhythm. — [Estrin 2025](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/how-to-play-big-leaps-successfully-on-the-piano)
- S6 Feel-then-play jumps, with fast horizontal travel and a wait that shrinks as tempo rises; plus the sideways hand flick away from the piano. — [Chang](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html#f-practicing-jumps)
- S7 Preparation shifts: after a central key, place the hand on the aimed key and freeze without striking; for wide left-hand leaps, play the bass with the fifth finger and aim the thumb at the next octave. — [Kim 2018 on Fink](https://exercisesforfree.com/pdf/163.pdf)
- S8 Eyes-closed leap in two stages: first note silent and second sounded; then both sounded. Octaves three at a time, stopping on the third, eyes closed. — [McLachlan, Leaps](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-leaps); [McLachlan, Octaves](https://gramophone.co.uk/international-piano/articles/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-piano-technique-part-1-octaves) (excerpt only)
- S9 Practising in the dark. — [Fitch, An Energy Saving Tip](https://practisingthepiano.com/an-energy-saving-tip/)
- S10 Note-to-note, note-to-chord and chord-to-chord jumps, then the same with the accent on the downbeat (leap from beat four to beat one) and on the upbeat. — [Dummies](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/piano/jump-exercises-for-the-piano-or-keyboard-203706)
- S11 Leap and shape change practised apart, then together; "plan, play, judge" on half-bar units; when both hands leap outward, watch one. — [Dorn](https://pianosage.blogspot.com/2012/08/piano-athletics-leaps-jumps-on-piano.html)
- S12 Touch-then-play; shifts at full speed inside slow practice; when both hands leap, watch the one that travels farther. — [Belkin](https://alanbelkinmusic.com/technique/)
- S13 Solid triads through root position and inversions, up and down, with rests, at crotchet = 100 to 120, hands separately then together, one then two octaves; the white-key triad sequence before that. — [RCM chart](https://colorinmypiano.com/wp-content/files/RCM_2022_Technical_Requirements_Charts_Prep-10.pdf); [RCM syllabus](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)
- S14 The 21-inversion walk through a key. — [Piano With Jonny](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/top-piano-chord-inversion-exercise/)
- S15 Cadence I–IV–I–V7–I in every key, one chord per beat at a set tempo. — [University of Utah](https://www.music.utah.edu/documents/undergraduate-forms/PianoProficiencyRequirement2017-2018.pdf); [Longwood University](https://www.longwood.edu/music/student-resources/assessments/keyboard-proficiency-requirements/) (excerpt only)
- S16 One Minute Changes between two chords, counted and logged. — [JustinGuitar](https://justinguitar.com/guitar-lessons/one-minute-changes-exercise-b1-110) (excerpt only)
- S17 Stride skeleton: root, triad above, fifth, triad again; or root to the 3rd and 7th only. — [Kovarsky 2022](https://hub.yamaha.com/keyboards/k-how-to/the-basics-of-stride-piano); [Piano With Jonny](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/learn-how-to-play-stride-piano/)
- S18 Repeated octave leaps there and back, and the same with one end accented (Ortmann's test movement); a timed leap between two octave grips a tenth apart with errors counted from MIDI (Bangert's task). — [Ortmann 1929](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.89583); [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)

**Measures already used in the literature**
- Wrong, missing and extra notes in a window around the leap; leap execution time; key velocity; velocity difference between the two ends of the leap. — [Bangert et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4128393)
- Onset spread within a chord. — [Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)
- Note duration, overlap between notes, inter-onset timing. — [Riley 2007](https://www.wpta.info/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/JTML-2007.pdf)
- Distance travelled by the hand in semitones as the difficulty of a position change. — [Parncutt et al. 1997](https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/_Persoenliche_Webseite/parncutt_richard/Pdfs/PaSlClRaDe97_FingeringModel.pdf)

### Inferences

Everything below is my own proposal, built on the findings above. Thresholds are starting values to be tuned on the owner's own piano and data.

**What key presses can and cannot show**
- A silent arrival on the keys produces no MIDI, so Quick Cover cannot be checked directly. Two indirect ways: (i) a shrinking pause, where the landing must sound exactly on a beat that comes sooner each stage (Estrin's method made strict); (ii) a cue at an unpredictable moment after the departure note, where a correct chord very soon after the cue shows the hand was already there.
- The eyes cannot be seen either. The look condition is either declared (lights off, a cloth over the hands, eyes closed) or forced by the task: if each next target appears on the screen only shortly before it is due, looking down costs the player the next target.
- Fingering cannot be seen; the drill text has to state it.

**Scores (definitions)**
- First-landing accuracy: the first key or keys pressed after leaving position A, compared with the target set. Later corrections do not count (Fitch's and Dorn's rule). Report as a percentage over the set.
- Miss size and direction: for a wrong key, target minus played, in keys, signed (short or long). Two neighbouring keys struck together by one finger is a "crack" landing. For chords, list missing and extra notes separately.
- Chord spread: last onset minus first onset among the chord's notes. Proposed bands: up to 30 ms together; 30–60 ms loose; over 60 ms rolled (from Goebl's figures).
- Travel time: release of the last key of A to first onset of B. Departure hold: how long A was held as a share of its written length (a clipped first note is Golandsky's "barely play the first note" fault when the drill is in rhythm, but is the point of an "arrive early" drill).
- Beat error: landing onset minus the click, signed.
- Loudness: landing velocity minus departure velocity (a thump on landing is the fault Ortmann and McLachlan describe); range of velocities within a chord; consistency over repetitions.
- Rate: clean landings per minute, where clean = all notes right on first landing and spread within the band; and "fastest clean tempo", the quickest setting at which 9 landings in 10 are clean.
- Drift: in there-and-back drills, the trend of signed miss size over repetitions.
- Looking cost: accuracy with the hands in view minus accuracy without.

**Difficulty settings shared by all drills**
- Hand: right, left, both in parallel, both outward or inward.
- Distance: keys or semitones the hand's centre moves. Under an octave the hand's span helps; an octave is the natural unit; beyond it the arm alone measures.
- Direction: outward from the body's middle or back toward it, scored separately (Ortmann: the two are different movements).
- Target: one note; octave; triad; four-note chord. White keys only; black-key landings; mixed.
- Shape change: none (same shape moved); inversion change; change of key colour pattern (C major to D major puts a black key under the middle finger); change in number of notes (single note to chord, triad to octave).
- Time allowed: the tempo and the written length of the note before the leap; summarised as required hand speed (distance ÷ travel time).
- Look condition: free; look at the target first, then leap; eyes on the screen; eyes closed or lights off.
- Loudness: soft and even first (Ortmann); accents later.
- Order: fixed pair repeated; ladder of distances; shuffled.

**Proposed drills**

P1 Find it by feel (own; built on Wright, Hilley, Chang, Belkin).
- Hands start in the lap. The screen shows one note on the staff. Eyes stay on the screen. The player finds the key by touching the groups of two and three black keys and presses it with finger 2 or 3.
- Easier or harder: range (one octave around middle C, widening to four octaves); white keys only, then black; left or right hand; time limit.
- Measured: right first press, miss size, time from display to press.

P2 Cover and cue (own adaptation of Fitch's Quick Cover, Estrin, Fink's preparation shifts).
- One hand. Notation shows position A and position B (start with single notes a fifth to an octave apart, same finger; later octaves and triads). Play A on the click; move at once and rest on B silently; a second click comes after a random wait of one to three seconds; play B on it. Then B becomes the new A.
- Look condition: look at B before leaving A, later eyes on the screen.
- Measured: first-landing accuracy, miss size, delay from cue to sound (short means the hand was ready), chord spread. A wrong landing is shown as "one key short", "two keys long", and the pair repeats.

P3 Shrinking pause (own adaptation of Estrin and Chang).
- Same material as P2 with a steady click. A sounds on beat 1 and is held one beat; B must sound on beat 4; when 9 of 10 are clean, on beat 3; then beat 2 (A and B now a beat apart). Then halve A's length so the travel time shrinks again.
- Tempo fixed at crotchet = 60 while the pause shrinks, then raised.
- Measured: first-landing accuracy, beat error, travel time, departure hold, landing-minus-departure loudness.

P4 Springboard and freeze (own adaptation of Fitch's Springboarding and the For Dummies accent pair).
- In rhythm at once: A as a quaver upbeat, B on the downbeat, repeated with a bar's rest between. Whatever is hit is held for the rest of the bar; no second try inside the bar.
- Variants: light upbeat with firm downbeat; then the reverse.
- Measured: as P3, plus whether any extra key was pressed inside the bar (a correction).

P5 There and back (own; built on Ortmann's repeated octave leaps, Bangert's task, Chen's alternating shifts).
- One hand alternates between two positions in steady crotchets, 8 to 16 times: first a single key with one finger an octave apart (for example left-hand fifth finger on the C two octaves below middle C and the C above it); then an octave grip moved an octave; then a tenth and a twelfth.
- Play softly and evenly. Later versions: eyes on the screen; eyes closed (stage one: touch the first position silently and sound only the second, per McLachlan; stage two: sound both).
- Measured: accuracy by direction, drift, evenness of timing and loudness.

P6 Distance ladder and shuffle (own; built on Bangert's mixed-distance group).
- From one home note, leap to the third, fifth, octave, tenth, twelfth and two octaves above and return home between each; first in that order, then shuffled, with the target shown one beat before it is due.
- Measured: accuracy and miss size per distance, giving a personal curve of error against distance that the app can use to choose what to practise.

P7 Bass then chord (own grading of Fitch's waltz tools and Kovarsky's stride skeleton).
- Left hand, 3/4: a low root with the fifth finger on beat 1, a close-position triad of the same chord about an octave higher on beats 2 and 3. Start in C, G and F with the tonic chord only, then alternate tonic and dominant bar by bar.
- Steps: (a) chords alone as a progression, no bass; (b) bass as an octave with the thumb on top, then the chord; (c) thumb's note and chords without the low bass; (d) as written; (e) bass an octave lower than written; (f) 4/4 with bass on 1 and 3 and chords on 2 and 4, bass alternating root and fifth.
- Easier or harder: tempo (start at crotchet = 60; a real waltz is two to three times that); distance from bass to chord; two-note landings (third and seventh) before three-note ones; right hand added later, played by the user or by the app through the piano.
- Measured: bass accuracy, chord accuracy and spread, travel time in each direction, beat error, bass-versus-chord loudness.

P8 Same shape, new place (own; built on RCM's white-key triad sequence and on separating leap from shape change).
- One hand plays a root-position triad, lifts, and plays the same shape elsewhere: first stepping up the white keys (C, D minor, E minor, F, G, A minor), then by thirds, fourths, fifths and the octave, then an octave and a fifth. Crotchet chord, crotchet rest.
- Then triads whose middle note is black (D, A, E major), then black–white–black shapes (E flat, A flat, D flat major).
- Measured: first-landing accuracy per chord tone (which finger missed), spread, travel time, loudness range.

P9 Inversion ladder with rests (RCM's pattern, scored).
- One hand: root position, first inversion, second inversion, root position an octave up, and back down; crotchet chord, crotchet rest; right hand fingered 1-3-5, 1-2-5, 1-3-5 and left hand 5-3-1, 5-3-1, 5-2-1.
- Keys in RCM's order (C, G, F, A minor, E minor, D minor, then outward). One octave, then two; hands separately, then together. Click from 80 up to RCM's marks (100, 112, 120).
- Measured: as P8, plus the fastest clean tempo, reported against the RCM mark for the level.

P10 One-minute chord changes for piano (own; modelled on the guitar drill and RCM's metronome marks).
- Choose two chords with stated voicings and one hand. Alternate for 60 seconds with no click, as fast as stays clean.
- Pair types, in order: near pairs sharing a note (C major root position and F major with C at the bottom); pairs a step apart; pairs a fourth or fifth apart in root position (a true jump); pairs with a colour change (C and D major); a root-position chord and an inversion of a far chord.
- Score: clean landings in the minute; share of landings that were clean; median travel time. Logged per pair so the app can show the slowest pairs. A first benchmark of 60 clean landings a minute matches RCM's rate for Levels 3–4.

P11 Cadence loop, near and far (own; built on the class-piano cadence).
- Right hand: I–IV–I–V7–I in close position keeping common notes, starting from each of the three positions of the tonic chord, one chord per beat at crotchet = 60; left hand plays roots, which leap by fourths and fifths. Then the same progression with every right-hand chord in root position, so the hand jumps.
- Keys: C, G, F, then D, A, E, then flat keys, then minor.
- Measured: accuracy, spread, steadiness of the beat, and the difference in clean rate between the near and the jumping version.

P12 Leap with shape change (own; built on Dorn).
- Three stages for each pair: the leap with the old shape (stay in a triad shape, wrong notes allowed in the app's "shape only" mode); the shape change in place (no leap); then both. Pairs: single note to triad, triad to octave, root position to an inversion a sixth or more away, triad to four-note dominant seventh.
- Measured: which stage carries the errors.

P13 Both hands (own; built on Belkin, Dorn, McLachlan).
- (a) Both hands leap the same way by the same distance, octave grips or triads. (b) Both leap outward to the ends of a chord; the instruction names the hand to watch (the one travelling farther) and the other must land by feel. (c) One hand holds a simple stepwise tune while the other does P7 or P5; the instruction is to watch the tune's hand.
- Measured: accuracy per hand, spread between the hands, and whether the unwatched hand is the one that misses.

P14 Chord flash (own).
- From a neutral position, a chord appears as notation or as a symbol; play it at once, one hand. Shuffled keys and inversions.
- Measured: time to sound, accuracy, spread. This tests knowing the shape, apart from travelling to it.

**Proposed order, easy to hard**
1. P1 with white keys near the middle; octave span by feel (thumb and fifth finger).
2. P2 then P3 with single notes, same finger, up to an octave, white to white, looking allowed.
3. P5 with one key, then with an octave grip, an octave apart; begin the eyes-on-screen version at this distance only.
4. P8 stepping on white keys, then by larger moves; P14 with white-key triads.
5. P9 one octave hands separately at 100, then 112, then two octaves at 120, then hands together.
6. P10 and P11 near pairs in C, G and F; then all keys.
7. P7 steps (a) to (d) at a slow click; tonic and dominant.
8. P4 and P6 with distances beyond the octave; black-key landings; P5 eyes closed.
9. P8 and P10 with colour changes; P12; dominant sevenths and four-note chords.
10. P13; P7 step (f) at rising tempo; shuffled leaps at tempo with eyes on the screen.

- Rough placing (my estimate, loosely anchored to the sources): steps 1–4 before and around Grades 1–2 (Roskell introduces leaps before Grade 1; RCM has solid triads at Level 1); steps 5–7 around Grades 2–4 (RCM Levels 1–4 inversions; first waltz basses); steps 8–10 around Grades 4–6 (Fitch's Grade 6 Bruch example; "La Styrienne" rated late intermediate). Beyond that lie Chopin's waltzes, Joplin's rags and stride.
- Move up a step when the clean rate is 9 in 10 on two different days, not within one sitting. Keep sessions short and mix distances and keys. After a miss, show which key was hit and how far off, and replay the target through the piano.

### Gaps
- None of the proposed drills, thresholds or the order has been tested; the 30 and 60 ms spread bands, the 60-a-minute benchmark and the 9-in-10 rule are reasoned starting points.
- I found no evidence on how quickly late beginners improve at leaps with any of these methods, or on how much not-looking practice is useful.
- How a digital piano reports timing and velocity for notes in one chord, and the tablet's MIDI timing error, were not researched; they decide how fine the spread bands can be.
- No source was found that grades leaps by size for learners (for example which interval is expected at which grade); the distance steps above follow the hand's span and the fingering model, not a syllabus.
