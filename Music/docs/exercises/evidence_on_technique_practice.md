# Evidence on how piano technique should be practised (for Prima's technique exercises)

*Researcher's notes, 10 November 2026. Scope: scheduling, pacing, variation, ending rules and feedback for technique exercises, for one adult learner (about ABRSM grades 1 to 5, about 20 minutes a day, digital piano).*

*How to read the evidence labels. Each finding says what kind of evidence it is: **controlled** (an experiment with a comparison condition), **meta-analysis / systematic review**, **observational** (correlations, surveys, case series), or **expert opinion** (a pedagogue's or clinician's advice). It also says what was studied (piano/keyboard, another instrument, or general motor skills) and the sample size where the source gives one. Numbers are quoted from the abstract or full text of the source linked beside them.*

*How these notes were compiled, and one limit. The session's web-search allowance (shared across the whole session) ran out after my first 18 searches. Up to that point I had also looked studies up by title in Europe PMC, Crossref and OpenAlex to read their abstracts. From then on I did not search: I fetched sources I already knew directly (journal and PubMed Central pages, records by DOI, the pedagogues' own sites and handouts). Most studies named in the brief could still be checked against their abstracts or full text. What could not be checked is listed under each **Gaps** heading, and nothing unverified is presented as a finding.*

## 1. What does motor-learning research say about practice schedules for piano or closely related skills?

### Takeaway

The best-supported ideas are that how a passage is practised matters more than how long (errors corrected at once, tempo adjusted to accuracy, repeating only until stable), that practice spread over days with sleep in between improves accuracy, and that mixing tasks slows progress during practice but tends to help next-day performance, most clearly in adults and in laboratory tasks. Almost every piano-specific study is small (10 to 60 people), single and unreplicated, and for slow practice, rhythm variations and "how many correct repetitions" there are no controlled comparisons at the piano at all.

### Cited Findings

#### Blocked against interleaved or random practice

- **Controlled, piano, n = 25, single study.** Pianists with formal training practised melodies in fixed (blocked) and random order, within subjects. They were faster on fixed-order melodies during practice, but two days later they were faster on the random-order melodies. They still believed fixed-order practice would give the better retention (an "illusion of competence"). — [Abushanab & Bishara 2013, Memory & Cognition](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0311-z)
- **Controlled, beginner clarinet, N = 41.** Three practice sessions on three seven-pitch exercises, blocked or random. No difference at the end of practice. At retention the random group played significantly faster, F(1, 38) = 24.95, p < .001, and the blocked group had slowed down; no difference on transfer tasks or attitude. The abstract reports η² = .92, which does not match that F value (it works out at about .40 by my calculation), so the size of the effect is uncertain. — [Stambaugh 2011, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429410385945)
- **Controlled, clarinet and saxophone, N = 19, one 18-minute session.** Blocked, hybrid and serial schedules gave no differences in accuracy, attitude or musicality, either immediately or 24 hours later. — [Stambaugh & Demorest 2010, Update](https://doi.org/10.1177/8755123310361768)
- **Controlled, university wind players, N = 46.** At 24 hours, woodwind players who practised in random order played faster (η² = .15) and more evenly (η² = .16) than those who practised blocked; for brass players blocked practice gave better accuracy (η² = .15) and speed (η² = .18). The effect depended on the instrument. — [Stambaugh 2013, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735612449505)
- **Controlled, advanced clarinet, n = 10.** Twelve minutes per piece, either in one block or in alternating 3-minute segments. Interleaved pieces were rated higher on average in every comparison, but most comparisons were not significant and raters disagreed (inter-rater r from .02 to .59). Six of ten players found interleaving more useful, six of ten still preferred blocked practice, and all ten said they normally practise blocked. The authors call the effect modest. — [Carter & Grahn 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- **Observational, 14 musicians.** Players rated repetition as the effective part of their own practice sessions; two independent raters judged that varied strategies had brought more progress than habitual repetition. — [Mornell, Osborne & McPherson 2020, Musicae Scientiae](https://doi.org/10.1177/1029864918771731)
- **Meta-analysis, general motor skills (not music).** 139 effect sizes from 61 studies: average effect of random over blocked practice .38; larger in basic research (.57) than applied settings (.19), and larger for adults (.50) than younger learners (.10). — [Brady 2004, Perceptual and Motor Skills](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.99.1.116-126)
- **Meta-analysis, general motor skills, 54 studies.** Random practice has a medium benefit for retention overall; effective in laboratory settings, "almost negligible" in applied settings; large for older adults, medium for adults, negligible for young participants. — [Czyż et al. 2024, Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65753-3)
- **Meta-analysis, sports practice.** A 2023 review is titled "The myth of contextual interference learning benefit in sports practice". I verified only its title and citation, not its numbers. — [Ammar et al. 2023, Educational Research Review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2023.100537)
- **Controlled, general motor (key-press sequences), young and older adults.** Three 4-element sequences practised over two days, tested three days later: random practice gave better retention in both age groups, although older adults paid a larger switching cost during practice. — [Lin et al. 2010, Psychology and Aging](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020196)
- **Review, general.** Conditions that make practice look best in the session can be worse for long-term retention and transfer, and learners misread ease during practice as learning. — [Schmidt & Bjork 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00029.x); [Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)
- **Expert opinion (a 2024 Oxford University Press book that applies this research to practising).** Start blocked, move to serial (ABC ABC), then to random order. Practical forms: switch what you practise every 2 to 5 minutes; "serial practice" through a list of at least 3 spots (5 is called optimal, more than 10 too many), aiming for five correct performances of each, with a miss wiping that spot's tally; draw key, pattern and tempo at random for scales and arpeggios. The handout says a spot should already be playable five times in a row before it goes on the list. — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf); [Gebrian, interleaved practice handout](https://mollygebrian.squarespace.com/s/interleaved-practice-handout.pdf)

#### Variable practice

- **Controlled pilot, piano, 36 music students.** A wide left-hand leap practised for 30 minutes as one fixed interval, four intervals, or the fixed interval with rests. Errors were the same after training and after 24 hours. On a new interval, the fixed group made more errors than on the trained one; that difference disappeared after 24 hours and then appeared in the variable group. The authors call it only partial support for variable practice. — [Bangert, Wiedemann & Jabusch 2014, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598)
- **Controlled, piano keyboard, 48 complete novices (12 per group).** An eight-note right-hand sequence, 144 practice trials, at either two tempi (350 and 450 ms between notes) or six (250 to 550 ms), in a fixed or reshuffled order. Timing skill was unaffected. Movement was smoother (less variable) on new tempi and new sequences after the small tempo set and after the fixed order. Tests were immediate; retention was not tested, and the authors suggest more skilled players might benefit more from variety. — [Caramiaux et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5832267/)

#### Spacing, rests and sleep

- **Controlled, keyboard, 29 non-pianist musicians.** A 9-note sequence in three sessions separated by 5 minutes, 6 hours or 24 hours. Accuracy improved in session 2 only when sessions were 24 hours apart; speed improved in all groups, with further speed gains in session 3 only for the 6-hour and 24-hour groups. — [Simmons 2011, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429411424798)
- **Controlled, piano.** A 12-note melody recalled after 12 or 24 hours with or without sleep: accuracy improved only after intervals containing sleep; evenness also improved 24 hours after training without further practice. Sample size not given in the abstract. — [Simmons & Duke 2006, JRME](https://doi.org/10.2307/4151346)
- **Controlled, keyboard.** Performance of short key-press sequences kept improving over the first and second nights after training. When a second, similar sequence was learned straight after recalling the first, the first showed no further overnight gain. — [Duke & Davis 2006, JRME](https://doi.org/10.2307/4101434)
- **Controlled, piano, N = 60 musicians.** A 13-note melody learned in the evening improved in speed and accuracy overnight, but practising a second, similar melody afterwards suppressed those gains. — [Allen 2013, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735612463947)
- **Controlled, keyboard, 36 non-musicians.** Twelve 30-second practice blocks alternating with 30-second pauses. A 5-minute rest after block 3 produced a jump in performance, continued improvement during the session, and the largest overnight gains; a rest after block 9 produced the jump but not the continued improvement. — [Cash 2009, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429409343470)
- **Controlled, keyboard, replication.** The same early-rest advantage appeared in non-pianist musicians learning a 13-note melody. — [Duke, Allen, Cash & Simmons 2009, Annals NYAS](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04795.x)
- **Controlled, keyboard, N = 118 (59 music majors, 59 non-musicians).** In a 12-minute session with a 5-minute break after 3 minutes, filling the break with a different key-press sequence reduced the gain across the break; talking or memorising word pairs did not. Non-musicians made larger percentage gains across the break and overnight than musicians. — [Simmons, Allen, Cash & Duke 2019, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735617735373)
- **Controlled, keyboard, n = 32.** When practice of the target melody was strictly limited in time and repetitions, performance got worse overnight instead of better, to varying degrees across conditions; the authors speculate that tightly rationed practice may interfere with consolidation. — [Allen & Duke 2013, Update](https://doi.org/10.1177/8755123313502350)
- **Controlled, piano, 100 students, mostly without piano experience.** Gaps of 0, 1, 5, 10 or 15 minutes between two learning episodes of a 17-note left-hand sequence adapted from Hanon, tested 5 minutes later: no spacing effect on accuracy (p = .07) or timing. The authors note almost no forgetting happened over such short gaps, which may be why. — [Wiseheart, D'Souza & Chae 2017, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553926/)
- **Meta-analyses, general motor and task learning (not music).** Massed practice depresses both performance and learning when measured by retention ([Lee & Genovese 1988](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.1988.10609373)). Across 63 studies and 112 effect sizes, spaced practice beat massed practice by a mean weighted effect of 0.46, moderated by the type of task and the interval, and larger in less rigorous studies ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.5.795)).
- **Controlled, general motor (golf putting).** Distributed practice beat massed practice during practice and at retention after 1, 7 and 28 days. — [Dail & Christina 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2004.10609146)
- **Meta-analysis, motor sequence learning, 34 articles, 1,296 subjects.** The large "gain after sleep" shrinks to nothing once averaging, fatigue built up in training, time of testing and training length are accounted for: "no evidence that sleep enhances learning", though sleep may stabilise memory. — [Pan & Rickard 2015, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000009)
- **Controlled, general motor (finger sequences).** Overnight speed gains of 17.5 to 28.9%; the transitions that were hardest at the end of training improved most overnight (17.8% against 1.4% for the easiest). — [Kuriyama, Stickgold & Walker 2004, Learning & Memory](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.76304)
- **Controlled, general motor (5-key sequence), 27 analysed.** With 10 seconds of typing alternating with 10 seconds of rest, all early improvement appeared across the rests (2.69 key presses per second gained across rests, a slight loss within practice periods), and 95% of the first day's gain was complete by trial 11 of 36. — [Bönstrup et al. 2019, Current Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6482074); replicated online, cumulative N = 951 — [Bönstrup et al. 2020, npj Science of Learning](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-020-0066-9)
- **Controlled, general motor, disputing the last point.** With N = 159, post-rest improvement was explained by fatigue-like "reactive inhibition" that builds during performance and fades in breaks, with no offline learning needed ([Gupta & Rickard 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-022-00140-z)). In five experiments, people who trained in 10-second bouts with 10-second rests produced more correct key presses during training than people who trained without breaks, but the advantage "vanished within seconds after training"; the gains appeared even with random, non-repeating sequences ([Das et al. 2025, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2509233122)).
- **Expert opinion.** Three correct repetitions, a 10-second break, three more, starting over after an error; revisit a new passage two or three times in a day; once it is comfortable, leave a day or two between visits; leave about 6 hours between two very similar new passages. — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf)

#### Slow practice against practice at tempo

- **Survey, 256 adult instrumentalists (not an experiment).** 96.5% use slow practice (99.45% of classical players). On 7-point frequency scales, slow practice (M = 6.07) was used more than chunking (5.28) or rhythm variation (4.28); gradually increasing the tempo (5.72) more than alternating slow and fast (4.90) or random tempi (2.65). The authors say the psychological mechanisms are little understood and that causal tests are still needed. — [Allingham & Wöllner 2022, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356211073481)
- **Qualitative analysis of the same survey.** Respondents named pitfalls: choosing too slow a tempo, using only slow practice, "getting stuck in slow ways of playing". The authors note research showing that tempo changes how the motor system is organised, so slow practice "might not help the performer to play at a faster tempo", and that chunking at tempo and rhythm variation are the usual ways round this. — [Allingham 2022, doctoral dissertation, University of Hamburg](https://ediss.sub.uni-hamburg.de/handle/ediss/9763)
- **Controlled, 27 advanced instrumentalists (not piano-specific).** One-bar passages learned to a criterion were then played at other tempi and in other contexts: tempo accuracy and pitch accuracy suffered when the performance tempo differed from the tempo at which the passage was learned. — [Duke & Pierce 1991, JRME](https://doi.org/10.2307/3344689)
- **Controlled, 60 high-school wind players.** Six practice trials of an étude with a steady tempo increase, at performance tempo throughout, or alternating slower and faster: the tempo pattern made no significant difference to gains. Hearing a model first improved rhythm and tempo gains. — [Henley 1999, dissertation (published in JRME 2001)](https://doi.org/10.31390/gradschool_disstheses.6992)
- **Observational, piano, 5 experts.** The patterns of finger coordination, the independence of the striking finger and rhythmic accuracy were the same at a normal tempo and at the fastest possible tempo, a stability the authors think is specific to skilled pianists. — [Furuya & Soechting 2012, Journal of Neurophysiology](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00378.2012)
- **Observational, piano, professionals against amateurs.** As tempo rose, professionals increased elbow rotation more and finger muscle co-activation less than amateurs; individual top speed was explained by elbow velocity, not finger velocity. So less skilled players do change how they move as tempo rises. — [Furuya et al. 2011, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2011.00050)

#### Rhythm variations

- **Controlled, piano, 27 pianists, single study.** Practising a sequence in various rhythms reduced activity in the hand muscles (including muscles not needed for the task) and changed how muscles were coordinated; practising in one rhythm, or resting, did not. Top speed improved equally with or without rhythm variation. — [Furuya & Yokota 2018, Journal of Neurophysiology](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00922.2017)
- **Expert opinion.** Rhythm practice (dotted rhythms, shifting accents, expanding groups) sharpens reflexes and exposes weak spots, but overused it makes playing mechanical and dotted rhythms can create tension; pick the pattern for a specific problem and keep checking that it works. — [Practising the Piano, 2025](https://practisingthepiano.com/rhythm-practice-done-right/)

#### Chunking

- **Observational, piano, conservatory students.** In two-octave scales, timing was least consistent at the octave boundary and listeners were worst at hearing deviations there: the octave behaves as one motor "chunk". — [van Vugt, Jabusch & Altenmüller 2012, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00495)
- **Observational, 22 pianists at four levels.** Amount of practice did not predict performance quality; pianists who were practising in longer segments by the middle stage of learning gave better performances. — [Williamon & Valentine 2000, British Journal of Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712600161871)
- **Expert opinion.** "At-tempo chunking": play one beat plus one note at tempo cleanly through the whole passage, then two beats plus a note, then three. — [Gebrian, practice challenge 2024](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/September-Practice-Challenge-2024.pdf). "Little bits fast": a few notes at speed in one gesture, repeated, extended and started from different points, alternated with slow playing. — [Fitch, Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/enjoying-ultra-slow-practice/)

#### Mental practice and listening to a model

- **Controlled, piano, N = 40 music majors.** Physical practice and alternating physical and mental practice both shortened performance time more than mental practice alone, and did not differ from each other; all three beat no practice. — [Coffman 1990, JRME](https://doi.org/10.2307/3345182)
- **Controlled, expert pianists.** Mental practice improved performance of hard sequences, but less than physical practice. — [Bernardi et al. 2013, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00451)
- **Controlled, 16 pianists.** Mental practice combined with physical practice gave results indistinguishable from physical practice alone. — [Bernardi et al. 2013, Music Perception](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2012.30.3.275)
- **Controlled, classical guitar.** A week of combined mental and physical practice beat physical practice alone on memory for the piece, more clearly 10 days later. — [Iorio et al. 2022, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735621995234)
- **Controlled, keyboard, 32 non-pianist musicians.** Listening to ten repetitions of the target melody before practising led to larger gains during practice and larger gains overnight. — [Cash, Allen, Simmons & Duke 2014, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429413520409)

#### How many correct repetitions, and what the best practisers did

- **Observational, piano, 17 advanced pianists.** Each practised a hard three-bar passage from a Shostakovich concerto for as long as they chose, and was tested the next day. Rank at the test was unrelated to practice time, total trials or complete trials. It was related to the percentage of all trials that were correct (r = −.51), the percentage of complete trials that were correct (r = −.71) and the number of incorrect trials (r = .48). — [Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- **The same study's strategies (as quoted in a secondary summary).** Three strategies set the top three pianists apart: locating and correcting the exact source of each error, varying the tempo of trials systematically (slowing down to get it right, then speeding up), and repeating the target until the error was corrected and the passage stable. The others included playing hands together early, stopping in anticipation of errors and addressing errors at once. Self-chosen practice time ran from about 8.5 to 57 minutes. — [Kageyama, summary of Duke et al. 2009](https://bulletproofmusician.com/8-things-top-practicers-do-differently/)
- **Meta-analysis, general (not music).** Overlearning (continuing after the first correct performance) has a moderate effect on retention, depending on how much, the task and the delay. — [Driskell, Willis & Copper 1992](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.5.615)
- **Controlled, verbal learning, 218 students.** Overlearners recalled far more after one week, but the advantage "decreased dramatically" over 1 to 9 weeks; the authors call overlearning inefficient for long-term retention. — [Rohrer et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1083)
- **Controlled, visual (not motor) learning.** About 20 minutes of continued training after performance had levelled off protected the skill from being disrupted by a second task, and instead disrupted learning of the second task; the effect may last only a few hours. — [Shibata et al. 2017, Nature Neuroscience](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5323354/)
- **Expert opinion, with numbers.** Play a fixed spot correctly at least five times in a row; if it took ten tries to get right, add at least five correct repetitions (ten is better). — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf). A different author claims about a hundred repetitions of a short passage are needed for overnight improvement, that returns diminish in under 10 minutes per passage, and that the last run before stopping should be the most correct one, at moderate or slow speed. These are his own estimates; I saw no studies cited for them. — [Chang, Fundamentals of Piano Practice, II.15](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_procedures/II.15.html)

### Inferences

Design principles that follow, with the strength of what is behind them:

- **Judge progress by the first run of the next day, not the last run of today.** Schedules that look best in the session are often worse later, and learners cannot tell the difference. *Strength: controlled, piano (one study, n = 25) and other instruments, backed by general reviews.* The app can use the first, "cold" run of an exercise each day as its test for moving on or raising the tempo.
- **Keep technique work short and daily; let sleep come between sessions on the same exercise.** *Strength: controlled keyboard studies (small) plus general meta-analyses; that sleep adds learning rather than protecting it is disputed.* A 20-minute daily habit is already the distributed schedule the evidence favours.
- **Mix exercises within a session once a pattern is known; keep a new pattern in a short block first.** Rotate after every run or every few minutes rather than finishing one exercise before the next. *Strength: controlled, mixed across instruments; meta-analyses show a medium effect that is larger for adults and weaker outside the laboratory.* It will feel harder and look worse in the session, which the app should say.
- **Do not teach two very similar new patterns in one session, and do not put a similar key-press task into the rest after a new one.** *Strength: controlled, keyboard (N = 60 and N = 118), single lab.* A dissimilar activity, or anything that is not a motor task, is safe in the gap.
- **Use short runs with short rests, and a longer pause early in a new exercise.** Rests of 10 to 30 seconds raise performance in the session and limit fatigue; a 5-minute pause after the first three minutes helped next-day results in one lab. *Strength: controlled, keyboard and general motor; whether the short rests add lasting learning is disputed (2022 and 2025 studies say not).* They cost little and have a safety case of their own (see section 4).
- **End an exercise on stability, with a time cap.** Stop after a few clean runs in a row (pedagogy sources say three to five) or after a few minutes, whichever comes first, and do not keep drilling once it is stable. *Strength: observational (n = 17) plus expert opinion; the numbers are convention, not findings.* A hard cap that cuts practice off before the pattern is secure may itself hurt (one small study), so the cap should be generous for a new pattern.
- **After an error, make the next run easier and aimed at the spot: slower, or just the bars around it.** Wrong runs are what predicted poor retention, not the amount of practice. *Strength: observational, piano, n = 17.*
- **Play the exercise to the learner before the first run.** *Strength: controlled, keyboard (n = 32) and wind players (N = 60).*
- **For a beginner, use few tempi in a predictable order; add variety (tempo, rhythm, articulation, key) once the pattern is fluent.** *Strength: controlled, novices at a keyboard, immediate test only; the benefit of variety for more skilled players is suggested, not shown.*
- **Rhythm variations are a way to vary a fluent pattern and reduce effort, not a route to speed.** *Strength: controlled, 27 trained pianists, single study.*
- **Mental run-throughs can stand in for some repetitions but not all.** *Strength: controlled, piano (N = 40 and smaller).*

### Gaps

- I found no controlled experiment comparing slow practice with practice at tempo at the piano, and none testing rhythm variations or chunking against plain repetition for learning (only for muscle activity). The slow-practice literature I could verify is a survey and its qualitative follow-up.
- No study tests how many correct repetitions are worth doing at the piano. "Three", "five in a row" and "a hundred" all come from pedagogy.
- Interleaving at the piano rests on one study (n = 25); the other music studies are wind instruments, with mixed results.
- I could not retrieve the abstract of Ammar et al. 2023 (the publisher's page would not load), so its numbers are missing. Highben & Palmer 2004 on auditory and motor mental practice, and Maynard 2006 on repetition in artist teachers' practice, could not be verified. The abstract of Pascual-Leone et al. 1995 (five-finger exercise, 2 hours a day for 5 days, enlarging cortical motor maps) is truncated before its mental-practice results: [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1995.74.3.1037).
- The open-access copies of Stambaugh's papers (digitalcommons.ric.edu) are outside this session's allowed network, so I could not check the effect size that looks wrong in the 2011 abstract.
- Whether rests add learning or only performance is unresolved; the most recent evidence (2025) is against lasting benefit.

## 2. What does research say about feedback and attention?

### Takeaway

Informative feedback after a run helps, and there is no good evidence that giving it after every run does harm; feedback shown during playing is the risky kind, because it can distract and create dependence, although findings are mixed and none are from piano. Attending to the sound or the keys rather than the fingers helped in three small piano studies, but a 2024 re-analysis of the wider literature puts the average effect near zero, and the claimed learning benefit of letting learners choose looks similarly inflated.

### Cited Findings

#### Focus of attention

- **Controlled, piano, 16 music majors, within subjects.** A brief keyboard passage was played while focusing on the fingers, the keys, the hammers or the sound. Performance was most accurate and transferred best with focus on the effects of the movement, and the more distant the focus, the more accurate the control. — [Duke, Cash & Allen 2011, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429410396093)
- **Controlled, amateur pianists.** After a week's practice of a set piece, playing with an external focus gave fewer wrong notes and fewer corrections than playing with an internal focus, regardless of how experienced the player was. Sample size not given in the abstract. — [Jentzsch & Braun 2023, Psychology of Music](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356221101431)
- **Controlled, skilled musicians on several instruments, two experiments.** Focusing on playing for the audience and on the expressive sound improved rated musical expression against both focusing on finger precision and no instruction; in the second experiment technical precision was also higher than with the internal focus. — [Mornell & Wulf 2019, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429418801573)
- **Controlled, 56 second-year band students.** "Think about your fingers", "think about your sound" and no instruction gave no difference in evenness on the day or 24 hours later; looked at one by one, more woodwind and valved-brass players did best with the fingers focus. The author advises using both with beginners. — [Stambaugh 2019, JRME](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429419835841)
- **Controlled, violin bowing, 16 experienced and 16 novice players.** Focusing on the feel of the bow on the string improved performance most, compared with focusing on the sound or on the arm. — [Allingham, Burger & Wöllner 2021, Journal of New Music Research](https://doi.org/10.1080/09298215.2021.1978506)
- **Meta-analysis, general motor skills, in favour.** 73 studies (1,824 participants) for performance and 40 studies (1,274) for learning: external focus better for performance (g = 0.26), retention (g = 0.58) and transfer (g = 0.58), regardless of age or skill. — [Chua et al. 2021, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000335)
- **Re-analysis of the same data, against.** After correcting for publication bias, the effects are negligible: g = 0.01 for performance, 0.15 for retention, 0.09 for transfer; the data favour no effect, with large unexplained variation between studies. — [McKay et al. 2024, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.304)
- **Expert opinion.** Focus on the sound and the expressive intent, or on what the instrument must do, rather than on the body; "a focus closer to the body is better at lower skill levels". — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf)

#### How often, and what kind of, feedback

- **Controlled, general motor, three experiments (the classic finding).** As the title states, giving knowledge of results on fewer trials enhanced learning; the abstract I could read gives no numbers. — [Winstein & Schmidt 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.677)
- **Meta-analysis, general motor, 61 papers, N = 2,228.** No significant effect of reduced feedback frequency at any point, during practice or at retention; the studies were "severely underpowered" and "robust evidence regarding feedback frequency and motor learning is lacking". — [McKay et al. 2022, Psychology of Sport and Exercise](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/v2cp7)
- **Controlled, general motor, N = 228.** Feedback giving the size of the error, or a graded score, produced learning; pass/fail feedback produced no improvement over the pre-test. Letting learners choose when to receive feedback made no difference to learning, nor to how autonomous or competent they felt. — [St. Germain et al. 2023, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02170-5)
- **Controlled, keyboard, 10 adults new to music.** Over four days of piano practice the fingers came to move more independently; visual feedback on rhythmic accuracy shown after each practice run increased independence further in a few finger pairs. — [Furuya, Nakamura & Nagata 2014, Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.06.031)
- **Controlled, piano keystrokes.** Repeating a force-control task with error feedback improved untrained people but not expert pianists, who needed a different kind of training. — [Hirano, Sakurada & Furuya 2020, Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abd2558)
- **Controlled, general motor (throwing).** Feedback on the three best of every six trials gave better retention than feedback on the three worst. — [Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007](https://doi.org/10.5641/193250307x13082490460346). A meta-analysis of such "enhanced expectancy" studies found g = 0.54 but warns it may be inflated by small studies. — [Bacelar et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984x.2022.2042839)

#### Feedback shown during playing

- **Controlled, general motor, two experiments.** Showing a movement on screen continuously while it was made improved some aspects during practice, but in retention tests the next day it had interfered with learning an accurate movement pattern. Originally Human Factors, 1997. — [Schmidt & Wulf 1997 (reprint)](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315243092-20)
- **Review, general motor.** For simple tasks, concurrent feedback helps during practice and the gains are lost at retention (dependence on the display); the more complex the task, the more a learner can profit from concurrent visual feedback early on; feedback should become less frequent as skill grows; feedback that says how to correct an error is better than feedback that only reports it. — [Sigrist et al. 2013, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0333-8)
- **Controlled, conservatory percussion students.** Neither of two real-time visual displays reduced timing or dynamics errors; the authors describe real-time visual feedback as "potentially disruptive to timing processes" through extra cognitive load, though a higher-level display improved imitation of expressive style. — [Brandmeyer et al. 2011, Psychological Research](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-010-0291-6)
- **Controlled, 12 beginning violinists (children and adults).** Real-time visual and aural feedback on intonation helped some players and distracted others, whose intonation got worse; it took time to learn to use. — [Pardue & McPherson 2019, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00627)
- **Randomised trial, 50 violin novices.** Real-time visual feedback on bowing improved the bowing during practice "at the expense of" sound quality, but at a later test without feedback that group had the better sound quality. — [Blanco, Tassani & Ramírez 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.648479)
- **Controlled, piano novices, two user studies.** Notes projected on the keys as a piano roll gave faster learning and lower cognitive load than sheet music. This is help with finding notes; nothing was tested without the projection. — [Rogers et al. 2014, P.I.A.N.O.](https://doi.org/10.1145/2669485.2669514)

#### Self-chosen practice and task difficulty

- **Meta-analysis, general motor, 52 effects, N = 2,061.** Self-controlled practice shows g = 0.44 at face value, but only published studies show a benefit; corrected for selective reporting the estimate is g = 0.107, "not currently distinguishable from zero". — [McKay et al. 2022, Meta-Psychology](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/8d3nb)
- **Case study, one young clarinettist.** On a piece she chose herself she practised longer, persevered, and used more advanced strategies (silent fingering, thinking, singing) than on assigned pieces. — [Renwick & McPherson 2002, British Journal of Music Education](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0265051702000256)
- **Controlled, piano.** Raising the difficulty of a piano learning task automatically when measured mental workload fell gave better accuracy and speed than a control condition. — [Yuksel et al. 2016, "Learn Piano with BACh"](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858388)
- **Theory.** Learning is best at a "challenge point" where task difficulty suits the learner's skill ([Guadagnoli & Lee 2004](https://doi.org/10.3200/jmbr.36.2.212-224)). For a class of learning algorithms on two-choice tasks the optimal training accuracy is about 85% ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4)); this was derived for classification, not movement.

### Inferences

- **Give feedback after every run, and make it informative: what was uneven, early, late or wrong, and by how much, rather than pass or fail.** *Strength: controlled, general motor (N = 228) and two small keyboard studies; the old advice to ration feedback is not supported by the 2022 meta-analysis.*
- **Marks during playing should be optional, and an exercise should include runs without them.** The risk is distraction and reliance on the display; the benefit is early guidance in a complex task. *Strength: controlled, but mixed and from other instruments and general motor tasks; no piano study.* Prima's existing choice of marks while playing or afterwards fits this; "afterwards" is the safer default for an exercise the learner already knows.
- **Word instructions and feedback in terms of sound and keys ("even", "quiet", "every note the same length"), not finger mechanics.** *Strength: controlled, piano, three small studies in favour; disputed by a bias-corrected meta-analysis, and null in beginner wind players.* It costs nothing, so it is still the sensible default; a beginner may sometimes need a nearer cue (the key, the feel of the key bed).
- **Let the learner choose for motivation, but let the app set the schedule.** Choice has not been shown to improve motor learning, and learners prefer blocked repetition, which is the schedule that works less well. *Strength: meta-analysis and a preregistered experiment (general motor); controlled and observational in music.*
- **Point out the best run as well as the faults.** *Strength: controlled, general motor, probably inflated by small studies.*
- **Adjust difficulty to results.** *Strength: one controlled piano study plus theory; the "85%" figure does not transfer to motor skills.*

### Gaps

- I found no study of whether marks shown during piano playing help or harm what is retained. The nearest evidence is from violin, percussion and general motor tasks.
- Sample sizes for Jentzsch & Braun 2023 and Mornell & Wulf 2019 are not in their abstracts, and the publisher's pages would not load.
- No study tests feedback schedules for adult piano beginners specifically.
- The piano attention studies measure performance in one session or shortly afterwards; I found none that tests retention weeks later.

## 3. What is the evidence for and against mechanical finger exercises such as Hanon?

### Takeaway

No controlled study I could find tests whether Hanon-type exercises improve playing or transfer to pieces. The laboratory evidence is indirect but consistent: pianists differ from non-pianists in how independently and quickly they control their fingers, not in finger strength or in the hand's anatomy, and skilled players use less finger force and more of the arm. Most pedagogues I could verify reject strength-building and finger-lifting drills while accepting scales and arpeggios; whether isolated independence or hand-strengthening exercises have any place is still disputed.

### Cited Findings

#### Finger independence, strength and speed

- **Observational, pianists against non-musicians.** Pianists tapped faster, especially with the ring and little fingers, and more regularly, but pinch and grasp force did not differ: "piano training... changed the ability to move individual fingers rapidly, but not their flexor strength". — [Aoki, Furuya & Kinoshita 2005, Motor Control](https://doi.org/10.1123/mcj.9.1.23)
- **Observational, 10 pianists and 10 non-musicians.** Pianists had faster finger movements and more independent control. There was no group difference in the passive mechanical coupling between fingers, range of motion or maximum force. — [Kimoto, Oku & Furuya 2019, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6704118/)
- **The same paper, 14 pianists.** Among pianists, top finger speed was related to finger flexion strength and to biomechanical features of the hand, not to neuromuscular independence, and not to starting age or lifetime practice. The authors suggest strength training and stretching might help, note the findings are correlational, and warn that excessive training can cause dystonia. — [Kimoto, Oku & Furuya 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6704118/)
- **Observational, 24 pianists.** The fastest rate of repeated thumb-and-little-finger keystrokes (about 6 to 8.6 per second) was predicted by finger tapping rate and elbow extensor strength. Grip strength was related to slower playing at the softest level. Starting age and practice before age 20 predicted nothing; the authors write that "the repetition of mere practice does not provide extremely fast piano keystrokes". — [Furuya et al. 2015, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4621510/)
- **Controlled (before and after), 10 adults new to music.** Four days of piano practice reduced unwanted co-movement between fingers, most at the ring and little fingers and most where independence was worst at the start. This came from ordinary practice, not isolation drills. — [Furuya, Nakamura & Nagata 2014, Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.06.031)
- **Controlled, healthy adults (not pianists).** Mechanical coupling between the fingers is "a major factor" limiting independence, most for the index, middle and ring fingers; neural control mainly limits the ring and little fingers in large movements. — [Lang & Schieber 2004, Journal of Neurophysiology](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00480.2004)
- **Biomechanical analysis.** Anatomical restrictions in the hand can limit the finger movements a musician can make voluntarily. — [Leijnse et al. 1992, Journal of Biomechanics](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290%2892%2990281-5)
- **Observational, professional against amateur pianists.** In some sequences amateurs "tended to work against the innate connectivity between digits", while professionals let non-striking fingers move slightly, "a more economical strategy"; in other sequences professionals were the more independent. — [Winges & Furuya 2015, Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.10.041)

#### How skilled players differ in force and movement

- **Observational pilot, amateurs against experts.** To play finger exercises at the same tempo and loudness, amateurs applied significantly more force to the keys and held it longer. — [Parlitz, Peschel & Altenmüller 1998, Journal of Biomechanics](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9290%2898%2900113-4)
- **Observational, 7 experts and 7 novices.** Experts used less co-contraction of finger flexors and extensors and less force at the knuckle, moving the key with the shoulder and forearm; novices pushed down from the hand. — [Furuya & Kinoshita 2008, Experimental Brain Research](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-007-1184-9)
- **Observational, professionals against amateurs.** Professionals used less activity in the forearm finger muscles and more forearm rotation in fast alternating keystrokes. — [Furuya et al. 2011, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2011.00050)
- **Controlled, expert pianists.** Finger speed that had stopped improving after weeks of practice improved after a robotic glove moved the fingers through fast, complex patterns; fast-but-simple or slow-but-complex movements did nothing. The gain was specific to the trained skill. — [Furuya et al. 2025, Science Robotics](https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.adn3802)

#### Transfer, and what repetitive exercises do to the forearm

- **Controlled, piano.** Skilled adults transferred learning to new melodies when the musical structure stayed the same, whatever the fingers had to do. The least experienced child pianists transferred only to sequences with the same fingers and the same notes. — [Palmer & Meyer 2000, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00216)
- **Observational, 19 expert pianists followed for about 27 months.** Evenness of scale playing tracked total practice time (43% of variance); it was not related to what the pianists reported about how they practised scales. — [Jabusch et al. 2009, Human Movement Science](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2008.08.001)
- **Controlled, 49 trained pianists, using Hanon as the fatiguing task.** The first two bars of Hanon's Exercise No. 7 were looped loudly (at least 78 dB) at crotchet = 112 until perceived exertion reached 7 out of 10 twice running, or 12 minutes. Thirty players stopped after 3.5 minutes on average (210 ± 100 s); nineteen lasted about 11.6 minutes. Forearm muscles showed fatigue, extensors more than flexors, and loudness control deteriorated. The authors note that playing to exhaustion is not typical practice. — [Goubault et al. 2021, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8047012/)
- **Controlled, 10 adult men without keyboard training.** Five-finger patterns at a fast tempo (about 200 ms between notes) roughly doubled forearm muscle activity compared with a self-chosen tempo (about 750 ms); patterns using non-adjacent fingers needed more activity than adjacent-finger patterns or single fingers. The experimenters gave 20 to 30 seconds of rest between trials to limit fatigue. — [Chong, Kim & Yoo 2015, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4554952/)

#### What the pedagogues say (expert opinion throughout)

- **Hanon's own claims.** The 60 exercises (1873) are meant to build speed, precision, agility and strength in all the fingers and flexibility of the wrists; once mastered, the whole book is to be played through daily. — [Wikipedia, The Virtuoso Pianist in 60 Exercises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Virtuoso_Pianist_in_60_Exercises)
- **Chang: against Hanon, for targeted work.** Ten objections, among them: no evidence is offered; both hands play the same notes so the weaker hand does not develop; no rest is built in and relaxation is never mentioned; the advice to lift the fingers high is wrong; repeating what is already mastered wastes time. "Speed is skill, not strength." He accepts scales and arpeggios for loosening up, and still describes a light finger-independence exercise, with warnings ("do not press down firmly as this is a form of stress"). "Students must never practice with fatigued hands." — [Chang, Fundamentals of Piano Practice, III.7](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html)
- **Taubman approach: against.** Exercises by Hanon, Czerny, Pischna and Dohnányi involve finger isolation, often with curled fingers, stretching and pushing, which "can lead to fatigue, tension, and pain"; endless repetition and practising in rhythms "have not shown themselves to be sufficiently effective". Solve the technical problem first, practise the solution slowly with exaggerated movements, then faster; "repeat only until the hoped-for result is obtained, and then move on". — [Golandsky, guest post](https://crosseyedpianist.com/tag/edna-golandsky/)
- **A 2026 peer-reviewed reading of Taubman.** Taubman's "curling" means actively flexing the end joints of the fingers, which is likely to increase co-contraction and stiffness across wrist and fingers. Her claim that it can lead to dystonia has "not been scientifically validated at the time of publication", and no study has examined how curling as she defines it affects forces or injury; the same paper notes that some co-contraction is necessary in skilled playing. — [Sabo, Comeau, Dvorkin & Russell 2026, Journal of Piano Research](https://doi.org/10.70760/KFXI1598)
- **Whiteside: against finger technique altogether.** Fingers have limited strength, so drilling finger strength wastes time; the upper arm and torso should drive playing; she was dismissive of Hanon and Czerny (that last point is marked "citation needed" in the source). — [Wikipedia, Abby Whiteside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Whiteside)
- **Sándor.** His book stresses gravity as a source of energy, criticises "excessively tense muscular activities", and corrects the idea that pianists play only with their fingers. — [Wikipedia, György Sándor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gy%C3%B6rgy_S%C3%A1ndor)
- **Huang: a qualified defence.** Hanon, Schmitt and Czerny are useful to beginners as variety but guarantee nothing; scales and arpeggios are the backbone of technique; mindless repetition of finger patterns is dulling; choose exercises related to the repertoire; raise the metronome only when playing is comfortable; pain means stop and find another way; avoid grip-strengthening tools. — [Huang, The Piano Education Page](https://www.pianoeducation.org/pnotmi2.html)
- **A teacher writing in Pianist magazine: against Hanon, for scales.** Any playing builds finger strength and independence; daily scales, chords and arpeggios and repertoire do it better; the idea that piano needs forearm strength is "one of the most dangerous myths"; rote exercises encourage playing without pauses. — [Rizzo 2019, Pianist](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/this-is-why-hanon-exercises-are-a-waste-of-time-and-possibly-dangerous/)
- **Gieseking and Leimer.** From his studies with Leimer, Gieseking "usually studied new pieces away from the piano". — [Wikipedia, Walter Gieseking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gieseking)

### Inferences

- **Where the consensus lies.** (1) Dexterity is a matter of control and coordination, not finger strength: *observational laboratory studies, consistent across several samples.* (2) Skilled playing uses less finger force and less co-contraction, so exercises should reward quiet, even, relaxed playing, not loudness or endurance: *observational.* (3) Mindless repetition is not effective practice: *observational (section 1) and near-unanimous expert opinion.* (4) Scales, arpeggios and broken chords are worth knowing because they are what pieces are made of: *expert opinion, with transfer evidence pointing the same way.*
- **What is disputed.** Whether isolated finger-independence drills have any place (Chang keeps a gentle one; Taubman and Whiteside reject them); whether hand strength matters at all (two of Furuya's studies link speed to finger and elbow strength among trained pianists, and suggest strength work might help); whether Hanon is useful for beginners (Huang) or harmful (Taubman); and whether Taubman's injury claims are right (not tested).
- **Design principle: no strength, endurance, finger-lifting or held-finger exercises.** Build independence by having the learner play varied patterns lightly and evenly. *Strength: observational laboratory evidence plus majority expert opinion; no trial.*
- **Design principle: make exercises out of patterns that occur in the learner's pieces, in the keys of those pieces.** At this level transfer is narrow: what carries over is what shares both fingers and notes. *Strength: controlled, piano (children and skilled adults), single study; plus expert opinion.*
- **Design principle: never loop a pattern loudly and fast without pause.** That is the protocol a laboratory used to fatigue trained pianists within minutes. Prefer moderate or soft dynamics, runs of seconds rather than minutes, and a rest between runs. *Strength: controlled, piano (49 trained pianists) and non-pianists (n = 10).*
- **Design principle: each hand gets its own runs.** Chang's objection that playing the same notes in both hands hides the weaker hand is reasonable, and the app can score hands separately. *Strength: expert opinion only.*

### Gaps

- No controlled trial of Hanon or any other exercise book against repertoire-based technique work, at any level. Transfer of exercises to pieces is untested.
- I could not verify from their own texts the positions of Matthay, Sándor (beyond the summary above), Fink, Neuhaus, Bernstein, Fraser, or Leimer and Gieseking: the search allowance had run out and the pages I could reach (encyclopaedia entries, a publisher's listing) give titles and dates but not the arguments. What follows is my recollection of these books, **unverified in this session**, and should be checked before it is relied on: Matthay taught forearm rotation, the use of arm weight and stopping effort the moment the sound is made, and opposed stiff finger drill; Sándor reduces technique to a few basic motions and treats fatigue and pain as signs of faulty movement, not of weak muscles; Neuhaus derives technique from the musical image and prefers to build it through music, while still describing basic elements such as scales, arpeggios, double notes and chords; Fink sets out fundamental movements of the whole arm; Bernstein stresses concentration and choreographed movement; Fraser, unusually among modern writers, argues for building the structural strength of the hand's arch with specific exercises; Leimer and Gieseking stress visualising the score, intense listening and short, concentrated practice periods.
- Wikipedia gives the titles and dates of Matthay's books (for example *The Act of Touch*, 1903; *The Fore-Arm Rotation Principle*, 1912) but not their content: [Tobias Matthay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobias_Matthay). Its entries on [Taubman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Taubman) and [Neuhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Neuhaus) likewise say little about method.
- Hanon's instruction to lift the fingers high is reported here through Chang's critique; I did not read Hanon's preface.
- Furuya's samples are almost all conservatory-trained Japanese pianists; none of the laboratory work is on adult beginners.

## 4. What are the injury risks?

### Takeaway

Playing-related pain is common among serious pianists (reported by roughly a quarter to nine-tenths, depending on the definition), but the studies are cross-sectional surveys of students and professionals, and no risk factor is firmly established. The factors that recur are long practice, long stretches without a break (more than 45 to 60 minutes), forceful chords and octaves, small hands, playing through pain and sudden increases in playing. Advice on warm-up and breaks is expert consensus, not tested prevention, and I found nothing on adult beginners in particular.

### Cited Findings

#### How common

- **Systematic review, pianists.** Prevalence of playing-related musculoskeletal disorders in pianists ranged from 26% to 93% across studies; no agreement on risk factors; evidence judged insufficient. — [Bragge, Bialocerkowski & McMeeken 2006, Occupational Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqi177)
- **Systematic review, keyboard players, 12 studies.** Musculoskeletal pain in 25.8% to 77.0%; wrists and hands 13.8 to 65.8%, neck 9.8 to 64.2%, shoulders 9.8 to 59.8%. The only consistent risk factor was being female (odds ratios 1.05 to 1.90). Also reported: age over 18, more than 20 hours a week, more than 60 minutes without a rest break, no regular sport, and playing despite pain. — [Amaral Corrêa et al. 2018, PM&R](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmrj.2018.04.001)
- **Survey, 203 Japanese female pianists, teachers and piano students.** 77% had had a playing-related disorder; 44% of those were serious enough to need treatment. Associated factors: more than 4 hours of practice a day, playing chords forcefully, eagerness about practice and nervous traits. Hand size was not a significant factor. — [Furuya et al. 2006, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2006.3023)
- **Survey, 195 Italian conservatory piano students.** 38.4% affected. In single-factor analyses the affected group differed in age, hours per week, playing more than 60 minutes without a break, lack of sport and accepting "no pain, no gain"; in the combined analysis age, lack of sport and "no pain, no gain" remained. Upper-limb problems correlated with hand size. — [Bruno, Lorusso & L'Abbate 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-007-0279-8)
- **Survey, 192 piano students in Malaysia.** 35.8% reported a disorder. Not taking breaks during practice, not doing physical cool-down exercises and diet remained associated in the combined analysis; practice hours were associated in the single-factor test. — [Ling, Loo & Hamedon 2018, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2018.2013)
- **Survey, 363 Chinese conservatory piano students.** 82.6% had had at least one disorder; wrist 50.4%, shoulder 41.3%, finger 36.4%. Those who warmed up and those who took breaks reported fewer and less severe problems. — [Zhao, Wang & Zhang 2024, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11513592/)
- **Survey, 182 students in Irish music schools.** Lifetime prevalence of an injury that stopped playing for 48 hours or more: 25.8%; the wrist was the commonest site (36.6%). — [Shields & Dockrell 2000, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2000.4030)
- **Online survey, 455 keyboard players.** Age and gender were significant risk factors for hand, finger and wrist problems; daily playing time and type of musician were not. — [Pak & Chesky 2001, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2001.1004)
- **Survey including amateurs, as reported in a review.** Among 341 Spanish pianists, professional and amateur, aged 8 to 70, 65.1% had or had had a repetitive strain injury. — [Russell 2006, Recherche en éducation musicale](https://www.mus.ulaval.ca/sites/mus.ulaval.ca/files/2020-11/REEM_24_Biomechanical.pdf)
- **Survey, 357 amateur musicians in Dutch student orchestras (median 5 hours of practice a week).** 67.8% had a playing-related disorder in the past year, with limited effect on function. Weekly practice time was not associated. Piano was grouped with harp and percussion (16 people, 56.2%). — [Kok et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5812604/)
- **Systematic reviews, musicians in general.** Best estimates of prevalence 39% and 47% in adult classical musicians when mild complaints are excluded ([Zaza 1998, CMAJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1229223/)). Twelve-month prevalence 41 to 93% in professionals ([Kok et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-015-1090-6)).

#### Risk factors

- **Case series, 200 pianists with hand pain attributed to overuse (mean age 26).** Of the 70 who were practising a particular technique when it began, 74% were practising octaves (30) or chords (22), which need the thumb and little finger spread wide. — [Sakai 2002, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2002.4028)
- **Case-control, 220 pianists with overuse disorders against 62 without.** The patients had smaller hands; the author suggests small-handed pianists over-spread and over-extend the thumb to reach. — [Sakai & Shimawaki 2010, Journal of Hand Surgery (European)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1753193409352405)
- **Survey, 66 pianists.** 28 had overuse syndromes of the elbow, wrist or hand. Smaller hands meant more problems; starting age, practice duration and intensity, warming up and stretching afterwards made no difference. — [De Smet, Ghyselen & Lysens 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0753-9053%2898%2980030-6)
- **Study of 35 college piano majors.** Among playing-related and hand measurements, the span between the right third and fourth fingers stood out as related to pain; the authors call for replication. — [Yoshimura et al. 2006, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2006.3024)
- **Case-control, 281 classical musicians.** "Warming up before and taking breaks during practice sessions protected" against a first playing-related disorder; women and string players were at higher risk. — [Zaza & Farewell 1997, American Journal of Industrial Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9219660/)
- **Survey, 850 music students at 56 European institutions.** 48% reported a playing-related disorder in 12 months; feeling more exerted after 45 minutes of practice without a break was one of the associated factors. — [Cruder et al. 2020, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0242660)
- **Prospective study, 442 music students free of problems at the start.** 49% developed a playing-related disorder within 12 months. Onset was associated with a change in physical activity level (adjusted odds ratio about 2.3), higher fatigue (about 1.08) and socially prescribed perfectionism (about 1.10 at six months). — [Cruder et al. 2023, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49965-7)
- **Systematic reviews of risk factors, musicians in general.** Consistent associations with previous injury, performance anxiety and stress, but study quality is low and causes cannot be established ([Baadjou et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqw052)). Long hours, over-practising and not taking breaks are among the factors reported ([Wu 2007, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2007.2011)). The overall evidence remains low despite many studies ([Rotter et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-019-01467-8)).
- **Clinical review.** "Sustained high levels of playing or sudden increases in playing load" are listed as a modifiable cause of overload. — [Chan & Ackermann 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00706)
- **Laboratory evidence on load.** Amateurs press harder and longer than experts for the same sound ([Parlitz et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9290%2898%2900113-4)); fast playing roughly doubles forearm muscle activity in untrained adults ([Chong et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4554952/)); a loud, fast, continuous Hanon pattern brought most trained pianists to high exertion within minutes, with the extensors fatiguing most ([Goubault et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8047012/)).

#### Focal dystonia

- **Review.** Musician's dystonia, a loss of voluntary control of highly practised movements, develops in about 1% of professional musicians; those affected show more anxiety and perfectionism; a genetic contribution is likely. — [Altenmüller & Jabusch 2010, MPPA](https://doi.org/10.21091/mppa.2010.1002)
- **Case-control, 110 musicians with hand dystonia, 99 healthy professionals, 61 healthy relatives.** Patients had started their instrument later (mean age 10.0 against 7.7, p < .001), 71% were male, 37% had a family history of movement disorders (11% of healthy musicians); 30% reported increased practice in the six months before onset. Annual practice time was similar to healthy musicians'. Cross-sectional, so not proof of cause. — [Schmidt et al. 2013, JAMA Neurology](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2013.3931)
- **Systematic review, 16 articles, 1,144 affected musicians.** Dystonia "generally arises after at least ten years of intensive playing (corresponding to roughly 10 000 hours of practice)"; average age at onset 28 to 44; the limb with the greatest fine-motor demand is the one affected. — [Rozanski et al. 2015, Deutsches Ärzteblatt International](https://doi.org/10.3238/arztebl.2015.0871)
- **Review.** It is a painless loss of dexterity specific to one skill, with no predictably effective treatment. — [Sadnicka et al. 2018, Nature Reviews Neurology](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrneurol.2017.146)

#### Warm-up, session length and breaks

- **Systematic review, sport.** 31 trials of upper-body warm-up: "no studies of upper body warm-up effects on injury prevention were discovered". — [McCrary, Ackermann & Halaki 2015, British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2014-094228)
- **Controlled, 55 violinists.** Fifteen minutes of cardiovascular, core-muscle or musical (technical exercise) warm-up changed neither muscle activity nor rated performance; the only effect was lower perceived exertion. — [McCrary et al. 2016, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000765)
- **Randomised trial, 170 conservatory students.** An 11-class prevention course on posture and psychosocial factors was no better than promoting general physical activity at reducing disability; many dropped out. — [Baadjou et al. 2018, Occupational Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqy105)
- **Expert opinion, clinical.** "A 5-min rest break should be taken for every 25 min of playing"; after 45 to 60 minutes, 10 to 15 minutes' rest; more often for harder repertoire. The authors state that such management "has been based on expert opinion". On returning after injury: start with as little as 5 minutes of playing followed by 5 minutes' rest. — [Chan & Ackermann 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00706)
- **Expert opinion.** "Many teachers advocate a five-minute break for every thirty-minute practice", but no specific evidence supports that interval; micro-breaks of a few seconds within playing are also recommended; avoid sudden increases in practice time. — [Zhao, Wang & Zhang 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11513592/)
- **Expert opinion.** At least a 5-minute break every 25 to 30 minutes; no more than five hours a day. — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf)
- **Expert opinion, BAPAM (reviewed 2019).** Warm up with gentle whole-body and wrist and hand movements before playing; do not stretch before playing, only gently afterwards; take regular breaks; cool down by playing "something soft, slow, and simple"; "STOP if you feel pain". No intervals or durations are given. — [BAPAM, Don't Cramp Your Style](https://www.bapam.org.uk/dont-cramp-your-style/)
- **Expert opinion, NASM and PAMA (2014).** "Sufficient physical and musical warm-up time is important"; "regular breaks during practice and rehearsal are vital"; set a reasonable limit on daily practice; "avoid sudden increases in practice times". No numbers. — [NASM-PAMA student information sheet](https://www.liberty.edu/music/wp-content/uploads/sites/26/2019/05/Neuromuscular_Health_June_2014.pdf)

### Inferences

- **A 20-minute daily session is well inside every threshold in this literature** (trouble is associated with more than 4 hours a day, more than 20 hours a week, or more than 45 to 60 minutes without a break). The realistic risks for this learner come from how exercises are played, not how long: force, repeated wide stretches, tension, and a sudden jump in load. *Strength: observational, from students and professionals; my extrapolation to an adult amateur.*
- **Design principle: keep stretches and force out of technique exercises at this level.** No repeated octaves or wide chords as drills, nothing marked loud for long, and nothing that holds the hand at full span. If a piece needs them, a few quiet repetitions are enough. *Strength: observational (case series and case-control, hundreds of pianists), consistent on octaves, chords and small hands.*
- **Design principle: stop on pain, and never reward pushing through.** The app should say so, and should not offer streak or endurance rewards that pay for playing while sore. *Strength: observational (acceptance of "no pain, no gain" was an independent risk factor in one survey) plus unanimous expert opinion.*
- **Design principle: raise load gradually.** If the app ever lengthens the technique block or adds exercises, it should do so in small steps and not all at once after a lay-off. *Strength: expert opinion; one prospective study links change in activity to onset.*
- **Design principle: build pauses into the exercise itself** (seconds between runs), which is what both the micro-break advice and the fatigue studies point to. A separate break is not needed within 20 minutes. *Strength: expert opinion plus laboratory fatigue data.*
- **Warm-up: start the block with something slow, soft and easy.** There is no evidence that warming up prevents injury, and one survey and one controlled study found no effect, but it lowers perceived effort and costs a minute. *Strength: expert opinion; evidence of benefit absent.*
- **Dystonia is not a realistic risk at this dose.** It follows many years of intensive practice in professionals. The association with starting later is from professionals who started at 10 instead of 7 and says nothing about adult amateurs. *Strength: observational; my inference.*

### Gaps

- I found no study of injury or pain in adult beginners or adult returning learners at the piano. The only data touching amateurs are the Dutch student orchestras (few pianists) and one Spanish survey known to me only through a review.
- Newmark & Lederman 1987, on amateurs who suddenly increased their playing at a music course, is cited by reviews for the "sudden increase" risk, but I could not read it or confirm its numbers. Allsop & Ackland 2010, on pianists' techniques and practice strategies, could not be retrieved either.
- No trial tests any break schedule. The "5 minutes in every 25 or 30" rule is expert convention.
- No study measures what short technique exercises do to an untrained adult's forearm over weeks; the fatigue studies are single sessions.
- Nothing I found compares digital and acoustic pianos for injury risk. One survey found no link with the model of piano played ([Bruno et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-007-0279-8)).

## 5. What is known about adults learning piano motor skills?

### Takeaway

Adults, including people over 60 who have never played, do learn piano motor skills and gain finger control from it, but fine and complex movements improve more slowly with age, what is learned in a session is less reliably kept overnight, and new learning is more easily disrupted by what follows it. The schedule features that help younger adults (random order, spacing, sleep) appear to help adults at least as much; nearly all the ageing evidence is from people over 60 doing laboratory key-press tasks, not from middle-aged piano learners.

### Cited Findings

- **Randomised trial, 156 healthy adults new to music, older age.** A year of piano lessons improved fine motor skills more than a year of music listening, with related changes in the motor cortex between 6 and 12 months. — [Worschech et al. 2023, European Journal of Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.16031)
- **Randomised trial, 155 adults aged 60 to 80.** Sixteen weeks of group piano training (two 90-minute sessions a week) improved working memory and processing speed against no training, and raised musical and general self-efficacy. — [Bugos & Wang 2022, Journals of Gerontology B](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gbac021)
- **Randomised trial, 31 adults aged 60 to 85 new to music.** Six months of individual piano instruction improved Trail Making and Digit Symbol performance against controls. — [Bugos et al. 2007, Aging & Mental Health](https://doi.org/10.1080/13607860601086504)
- **Controlled, 15 older novices, 10 weeks of small-group piano.** Visuo-motor speed improved; motivation was best when participants were happy with the repertoire, especially familiar music. — [MacRitchie et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02868)
- **Review, general motor learning and age.** "Although motor performance tends to decline in old age, learning capabilities remain intact"; gains in fine motor tasks are smaller in older adults, and age differences are larger in complex tasks. — [Voelcker-Rehage 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11556-008-0030-9)
- **Controlled, general motor (sequence task), 12 older and 14 young adults.** Older adults learned as well or better within the session but, unlike the young, showed no improvement at the retest 24 hours later. — [Brown, Robertson & Press 2009, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0006683)
- **Controlled, general motor.** Older adults learned a sequence as well as young adults at first but did not improve after sleep. — [Spencer, Gouw & Ivry 2007, Learning & Memory](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.569407)
- **Controlled, general motor, 16 older and 15 young adults.** Tested 24 hours after training, including a night's sleep, older adults kept their performance and improved by 17.4% by the end of the retest, the same as the young (17.3%); after 12 waking hours they got worse. The improvement appeared later in the retest than in the young. — [Tucker, McKinley & Stickgold 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1532-5415.2011.03324.x)
- **Controlled, general motor.** Older adults showed no sleep-related gain on a task needing separate finger movements, but did on a version done with the whole hand; the authors blame declining finger dexterity. — [Gudberg, Wulff & Johansen-Berg 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2014.12.014)
- **Controlled, general motor, children, young and older adults.** Practising a second task after the first disrupted memory of the first only in older adults, who also showed no gains between sessions. — [Roig et al. 2014, Neurobiology of Aging](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2014.02.022)
- **Controlled, general motor.** Random practice gave better retention than blocked practice in older as well as young adults. — [Lin et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020196). Meta-analyses find the benefit of random practice large in older adults and medium in adults ([Czyż et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65753-3)), and larger in adults than in the young ([Brady 2004](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.99.1.116-126)).
- **Observational, ages 4 to 85.** One measure of implicit sequence learning drops sharply at about age 12, and declines again after 60. — [Janacsek, Fiser & Nemeth 2012, Developmental Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2012.01150.x)
- **Controlled, adult musicians matched for experience.** Those who began before age 7 learned a timed motor sequence better than those who began later, most clearly in synchronisation. — [Watanabe, Savion-Lemieux & Penhune 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-006-0619-z)
- **Observational, trained pianists.** Among pianists, neither starting age nor accumulated practice predicted top finger speed. — [Furuya et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4621510/); [Kimoto et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6704118/)
- **Controlled (before and after), 10 adults new to music.** Finger independence improved measurably in four days of piano practice. — [Furuya, Nakamura & Nagata 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.06.031)
- **Controlled, keyboard.** Non-musician adults made larger percentage gains across a rest and overnight than music majors did. — [Simmons et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735617735373)
- **Observational, pianists.** Older expert pianists kept their music-related skills despite general slowing, in proportion to their deliberate practice in later adulthood; older amateurs showed the usual decline. — [Krampe & Ericsson 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.125.4.331)
- **Meta-analysis.** Older age is one of the factors that changes how much performance appears to improve after sleep. — [Pan & Rickard 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000009)

### Inferences

- **An adult learner should expect real but slower gains in finger control, and the app should measure them over weeks, not sessions.** *Strength: randomised trials in older adults (general outcome) plus general motor reviews.*
- **Design principle: fewer new patterns per session, and none learned back to back.** Older learners are more easily disrupted by what follows new learning. *Strength: controlled, general motor, adults over 60, single studies; consistent with the keyboard interference studies in section 1.*
- **Design principle: test on the next day and repeat over several days before treating a pattern as learned.** One good session is weaker evidence of learning in an older adult than in a young one. *Strength: controlled, general motor.*
- **Design principle: keep random or mixed order.** The benefit is, if anything, larger in adults. *Strength: meta-analysis, general motor.*
- **Design principle: on a retest, give a short run-in before judging.** In one study older adults' overnight improvement showed only after the first few trials. *Strength: one controlled study, general motor.*
- **Familiar, chosen music keeps adults practising.** *Strength: one small study with qualitative data.*
- These studies concern people over 60. If the learner is younger, the age effects are probably smaller, but the direction of each principle is the same and none costs anything. *My inference.*

### Gaps

- I found no study of practice schedules for adult piano beginners (blocked against mixed, spacing, session length), and no comparison of how fast adults and children acquire piano technique.
- Evidence on middle-aged adults (roughly 30 to 60) is nearly absent from what I could check; the ageing literature compares people in their twenties with people over 60.
- The piano trials in older adults report cognitive and general motor outcomes, not which way of practising worked best.
- Whether older adults' weaker overnight consolidation applies to musical material, where the sound gives extra feedback, has not been tested as far as I could find.

## 6. How much technique work do pedagogy sources recommend, and how should tempo be raised?

### Takeaway

No source I could verify gives an evidence-based share of practice time for technique, and none gives a tested rule for raising tempo. Recommendations run from Hanon's whole book every day to no separate exercises at all, with the modern middle being a few minutes of scales and arpeggios, varied daily and tied to the repertoire. For tempo, the one experiment found that the pattern of tempo increase made no difference; what the observational evidence and most pedagogues agree on is to tie tempo to accuracy, slow down after errors, change tempo deliberately, and include playing at full speed in short stretches.

### Cited Findings

#### How much technique work

- **Expert opinion (historical).** Hanon: once learned, play the entire book through every day. — [Wikipedia, The Virtuoso Pianist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Virtuoso_Pianist_in_60_Exercises)
- **Expert opinion, against.** "Time can be better spent practicing 'real' music than 'exercise' music"; someone with two hours who spends one on Hanon "would waste half of his piano lifetime"; targeted exercises "should not be practiced every day like Hanon"; scales and arpeggios are acceptable for loosening up. — [Chang, III.7](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_topics/III.7.html)
- **Expert opinion.** Use technical exercises sparingly, chosen for the repertoire in hand; they are "only a means to an end". No time is given. — [Huang](https://www.pianoeducation.org/pnotmi2.html)
- **Expert opinion.** Practise scales, chords and arpeggios every day; drop Hanon. No time is given. — [Rizzo 2019](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/this-is-why-hanon-exercises-are-a-waste-of-time-and-possibly-dangerous/)
- **Expert opinion (Taubman approach).** No exercise routine: practise the solution to a specific problem "for as long as he or she can concentrate", and "repeat only until the hoped-for result is obtained, and then move on". — [Golandsky](https://crosseyedpianist.com/tag/edna-golandsky/)
- **Expert opinion.** Vary the technique routine daily by drawing the key, the pattern and the tempo at random. — [Gebrian, handout](https://mollygebrian.squarespace.com/s/interleaved-practice-handout.pdf)
- **Observational, 257 young musicians, 94 with 42-week practice diaries.** Achievement was strongly related to the amount of formal practice; high achievers were more consistent from week to week and "tended to concentrate technical practice in the mornings". — [Sloboda et al. 1996, British Journal of Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1996.tb02591.x)
- **Observational, 19 expert pianists.** Evenness of scales followed total practice time, not the reported content of scale practice. — [Jabusch et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2008.08.001)
- **Meta-analyses.** Accumulated practice correlates with musical achievement (corrected r = 0.61, 13 studies, N = 788: [Platz et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00646)); deliberate practice explains about 21% of the variance in music performance ([Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)).
- **Expert opinion on session length.** Keep sessions to what concentration allows: roughly 10 to 20 minutes for younger students, 45 to 60 for older ones; the article reports Auer's remark that practising with the mind achieves in an hour and a half what the fingers need all day for. — [Kageyama, Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/how-many-hours-a-day-should-you-practice/)
- **Expert opinion on time per item.** Switch what you are working on every 2 to 5 minutes ([Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf)); stop a passage when returns diminish, usually "in less than 10 minutes" ([Chang, II.15](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_procedures/II.15.html)).

#### How tempo should be raised

- **Controlled, 60 high-school wind players.** Steady increase, performance tempo throughout, and alternating slower and faster tempi produced no significant difference in gains over six trials. — [Henley 1999](https://doi.org/10.31390/gradschool_disstheses.6992)
- **Observational, piano, n = 17.** The best practisers varied tempo systematically from trial to trial, slowing to correct an error and speeding up once it was secure; how often they played wrongly predicted the next day's result. — [Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429408328851); [summary of strategies](https://bulletproofmusician.com/8-things-top-practicers-do-differently/)
- **Controlled, 27 advanced instrumentalists.** A passage learned at one tempo was played less accurately at a different one. — [Duke & Pierce 1991](https://doi.org/10.2307/3344689)
- **Controlled, 48 novices at a keyboard.** Practising at two tempi in a fixed order gave smoother movement on new tempi than six tempi or a shuffled order (immediate test). — [Caramiaux et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5832267/)
- **Survey, N = 256.** Gradually increasing the tempo is the most used strategy, ahead of alternating slow and fast; random tempi are rarely used. Respondents warned against a tempo that is too slow and against only practising slowly. — [Allingham & Wöllner 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356211073481)
- **Expert opinion, with numbers.** Play it correctly at least five times in a row; three correct repetitions, a 10-second break, three more, starting again after an error. To get faster: different rhythms, "click up" with the metronome, interleaved clicking up, at-tempo chunking (one beat plus a note at full tempo, then two beats plus a note), and "click-up chunking" (one beat at a time with a rest after each, from slow to goal tempo, then two beats). — [Gebrian 2024, Appendix A](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/Gebrian-Appendix-A.pdf); [practice challenge 2024](https://www.mollygebrian.com/s/September-Practice-Challenge-2024.pdf)
- **Expert opinion.** Practise at a speed at which you can play accurately, not as fast as possible; go briefly faster to find what must change, then return to the fastest accurate speed; raise speed "in manageable jumps"; "do not stay at any one speed for too long"; if there is no progress in a few minutes, something is wrong. — [Chang, II.13](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_procedures/II.13.html)
- **Expert opinion, against simple ramping.** Speeding up a slow motion is "like forcing a horse [to] run as fast as a gallop by simply speeding up the walk": habits harmless at slow speed become obstacles at speed. — [Chang, II.16](https://fundamentals-of-piano-practice.readthedocs.io/chapter1/ch1_procedures/II.16.html)
- **Expert opinion.** Slow practice belongs to the first stage of learning; keep the movements quick even when the notes are far apart; then use "little bits fast" and alternate slow and fast. Practising just under tempo tends to drift back up. Two or three slow repetitions of a section are enough. — [Fitch](https://practisingthepiano.com/enjoying-ultra-slow-practice/)
- **Expert opinion.** Raise the metronome only once the playing is comfortable; there is no standard speed. — [Huang](https://www.pianoeducation.org/pnotmi2.html)
- **Expert opinion.** Slow practice risks reinforcing movements that must be unlearned at speed; it may be most useful after a passage has reached its goal tempo. The article cites no studies. — [Kageyama, "Is slow practice really necessary?"](https://bulletproofmusician.com/is-slow-practice-really-necessary/)
- **Laboratory evidence that tempo changes the work.** Less skilled players change their movement as tempo rises ([Furuya et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2011.00050)), and forearm muscle activity roughly doubles at a fast tempo in untrained adults ([Chong et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4554952/)).

### Inferences

- **There is no evidence for any particular rule**: not for a number of clean repetitions, a size of metronome step, or a share of the target tempo. The numbers in circulation (three, five in a row, one notch) are teaching conventions. The app is free to choose its own, and should treat them as adjustable.
- **Design principle: tempo follows accuracy in both directions.** Raise it after a short run of clean, even plays (three to five is the conventional range) and lower it immediately after a flawed one. *Strength: observational, piano (n = 17), plus near-unanimous expert opinion; the only experiment on tempo patterns found no difference between patterns.*
- **Design principle: do not only ramp.** Mix in short bursts at or near the target tempo (a beat or two, then longer) from early on, because what is learned at one tempo does not fully carry to another and movements change with speed. *Strength: controlled (other instruments) and laboratory observation, plus expert opinion.*
- **Design principle: for a new pattern keep to two or three tempi in a steady order; widen the range later.** *Strength: controlled, novices, immediate test only.*
- **Design principle: speed is the last thing to add and the first to remove.** Faster playing roughly doubles muscular load in untrained adults, so tempo increases should be small for a learner who is still pressing hard or playing unevenly, and loudness should not rise with tempo. *Strength: laboratory evidence, small samples; my inference for exercise design.*
- **Design principle: a short, varied technique block, a few minutes of a 20-minute session.** The sources that give times suggest minutes per item (2 to 5, never more than about 10), favour scales, arpeggios and patterns from the pieces in hand over a fixed drill book, and favour changing key, pattern and tempo from day to day. A block of about 3 to 5 minutes, two or three exercises, placed at the start where it can double as the slow, soft warm-up, is consistent with all of them. *Strength: expert opinion; the proportion is my inference, not a finding.*

### Gaps

- I could not reach guidance from ABRSM, Trinity or the Royal Conservatory on how much technical work to do in a session; the search allowance had run out and I did not have their page addresses. I could not check Jørgensen's chapter on practice strategies in Williamon's *Musical Excellence* beyond its abstract ([chapter record](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198525356.003.0005)), nor Klickstein's *The Musician's Way*.
- No source I verified states tempo targets as a percentage of the final tempo.
- No experiment compares criteria for raising tempo (for example three clean runs against five, or small steps against large) on any instrument, as far as I could find. Henley's study is the only controlled comparison of tempo patterns and used six trials on one étude.
- No study addresses how much technique work suits a 20-minute session, or an adult at grades 1 to 5.
