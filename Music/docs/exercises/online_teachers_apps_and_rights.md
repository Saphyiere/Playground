# Online piano teachers' technique exercises, practice-app scoring, and the rules on using YouTube tutorials (for Prima)

Research date: 10 November 2026. How to read the source labels: **(own write-up)** means the teacher's or channel's own website post, usually the written companion to a video; **(third-party write-up)** means someone else describing a teacher's video or course; **(snippet)** means I saw the claim only in a search-engine summary of the page and could not open the page itself. I watched no videos. Page contents were read through a fetch tool that summarises pages and returns only short quotations, and notation shown as images could not be read, so the pattern descriptions below are prose-level, in my own words. Nothing here is legal advice; it reports what the terms, statutes and guidance say.

## 1. Which technique exercises and daily routines do widely followed English-language piano teachers on YouTube recommend for beginners and intermediates?

### Takeaway
The teachers' own written posts describe a small, repeatable vocabulary that a generator can rebuild without copying anyone's notation: five-finger patterns with articulation, dynamic and rhythm variants; scales on a metronome "notes-per-beat ladder"; arpeggios (one hand, hand-over-hand, cross-hand); a triad shape walked up the white keys; chord qualities cycled through all 12 roots; short progression routines; left-hand accompaniment figures; two-hand coordination drills; held-note finger-independence drills; and a few speed drills. Most teachers say "start slowly" rather than give a number; the concrete figures come from Robert Estrin (metronome 60, one then two then four notes per beat, four clean repetitions before moving on), Graham Fitch (fixed pulse of about 60 while the scale grows from one to four octaves), Pianote (for example E major at 90 with two notes per click, tempo raised 3 to 5 bpm at a time) and Piano With Jonny (140 to 160 BPM as a target for seventh-chord patterns, under five minutes a day, technique no more than about 15% of practice).

### Cited Findings

#### Pianote (Lisa Witt and colleagues; "The Note" blog, own write-ups of the channel's lessons)
- **Beginner routine built on five-finger positions (27 Dec 2021).** Step 1 is a posture and breathing check. Step 2 is a five-note scale hands together in C position (right thumb on middle C, fingers 1 to 5 going up; left little finger on the C below, 5 to 1 going up), then the same shape moved to G, A and F positions, finishing back on C. Step 3 is a right-hand four-note arpeggio (C E G C) over left-hand fifths that move through C–G, G–D, A–E and F–C, then left-hand four-note arpeggios with the right hand holding a fifth. Step 4 is a three-note melody over the same left-hand shapes. Things to watch: shoulder tension, the little finger drifting away from the keys, control before speed. No BPM is given, only "slowly". — [Pianote, Piano Practice Routine for Beginners](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine-for-beginners/)
- **"4 Piano Exercises For Beginners" (10 May 2019).** (1) Five-finger scale up and down, hands separately then together, aiming for even pressure; variants are one hand legato against the other staccato (then swapped) and one hand in quarter notes against the other in eighth notes (two against one, then swapped). (2) Contrary-motion C major scale, both thumbs starting on middle C, one octave out and back with identical fingering in both hands, then with a swell (soft, louder at the halfway point, soft again). (3) A 1-3-5 triad hand shape ("claw") placed on C and moved up one white key at a time and back; beginners may play only the outer two notes; the purpose given is dexterity and chord muscle memory. (4) The same walk with each triad broken into single notes, for speed under control. No tempo figures. — [Pianote, 4 Piano Exercises For Beginners](https://www.pianote.com/blog/beginner-exercises/)
- **"4 Things Beginner Piano Players Should Practice" (24 Dec 2019).** Finding every group of two and three black keys; the C five-finger scale each hand then together, very slowly; the 1-3-5 triad shape moved up key by key (C, D minor, E minor and so on), right hand then left, letting arm weight rather than pressing bring the keys down; and a "name a note (or chord), find it fast" drill. — [Pianote, 4 Things Beginner Piano Players Should Practice](https://www.pianote.com/blog/4-things-a-beginner-should-do/)
- **Hand-independence exercises for beginners (16 Oct 2020).** (1) Rhythm on one pitch: the right hand repeats G on every eighth-note count while the left plays G only on beat 1, the "and" of 2, beat 3 and the "and" of 4. (2) Two lines moving differently in C: the left hand rocks between C, G and the C above while the right hand, an octave higher, walks up and down the five-finger pattern, one right-hand note per left-hand note. (3) A broken D major triad with a different order in each hand (right: bottom, middle, top, middle; left: bottom, top, middle, top), then the orders swapped. Advice: slow and patient, and rest between sessions because the skill consolidates overnight; no BPM. — [Pianote, Hand Independence Exercises For Beginners](https://www.pianote.com/blog/beginner-hand-independence-exercises/)
- **"3 Easy Piano Warm-Ups (For Busy Adults)" (Lisa Witt, 28 Jul 2026).** A "morning coffee" sequence (harmonic intervals, a C scale with fifths underneath, broken arpeggios, a Hanon pattern alternating staccato and legato, and a hand-independence warm-up); a chord-coordination routine on a four-chord loop the page writes as "Dm1 – Bb2 – F – C1" (the numbers apparently marking first and second inversions, chosen so the hand barely moves), built in six layers (right-hand solid chords; add left-hand roots; left-hand root–fifth–root; left hand moving while the right holds; right-hand chords broken into single notes; both together); and a "sight-reading sprint" in A minor (the scale in each hand, then A minor–G–F–G held four beats each, then one chord note per beat). The only pace advice is to play "slowly, with intention". — [Pianote, 3 Easy Piano Warm-Ups](https://www.pianote.com/blog/easy-piano-warm-ups/)
- **Practice-routine template (Lisa Witt, 9 Sep 2025).** Warm up for 3 to 5 minutes on the first four chords of the song being learned, broken as bottom–middle–top–middle with pedal, in place of traditional exercises such as Hanon; then the scale that matches the song, with a backing track rather than only a metronome, hands separately before together; then about 5 minutes per chunk of 1 to 4 bars. Suggested load for beginners: 10 to 30 minutes a session, 3 to 5 sessions a week. — [Pianote, How To Build The Perfect Piano Practice Routine](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine/)
- **One-key routine (D minor; Lisa Witt, page updated 13 Jan 2023).** One-octave natural minor then harmonic minor; one hand staccato while the other is legato, then swapped; the four chords i, iv, VI, V (D minor, G minor, B-flat, A) as right-hand chords over left-hand fifths, then in both hands; inversions practised inside the progression rather than in isolation; arpeggios inside the progression; then right-hand improvisation over rocking left-hand fifths. No BPM ("Speed comes with time"). — [Pianote, A Complete Practice Routine in D Minor](https://www.pianote.com/blog/d-minor-practice-routine/)
- **Three "classical" exercises at three levels (Lisa Witt, updated 8 Apr 2024).** Scales: beginner C major and A minor; intermediate E major and C-sharp minor (E major at 90 bpm, two notes per click); advanced A-flat major and F minor; hands separately then together; motto "Consistency > Speed". Arpeggios: one octave (beginner; right, left, then both, five times each), two octaves (intermediate), three octaves (advanced); flexible wrist, the elbow leading the hand. Hanon: one pattern per level from *The Virtuoso Pianist* (1873), pencilling in finger numbers; the intermediate choice targets the ring and little fingers. — [Pianote, 3 Classical Piano Exercises](https://www.pianote.com/blog/classical-piano-exercises/)
- **Fast scales by "burst" practice (Jordan Rudess, 8 Dec 2023).** Treat the first notes of an ascending C major scale as pickups into a target note that lands on the click: aim at E, then at F (the first thumb crossing), then G, adding one note at a time; keep the fingers light and let the whole arm travel in one continuous motion rather than concentrating on each thumb tuck; hear the scale as one unit. No tempo figures. — [Pianote, How to Play Scales FAST](https://www.pianote.com/blog/how-to-play-scales-fast/)
- **Three beginner left-hand patterns (Jordan Leibel, updated 13 Jan 2023), all over I–V–vi–IV.** (1) Alternate the two outer notes of each chord in quarter notes under right-hand eighth notes, choosing inversions that move smoothly. (2) "Half arpeggio": a half-note root, then four eighth notes climbing the chord to the octave. (3) Continuous eighth-note arpeggio up and back down (root, third, fifth, octave, fifth, third, root) with the fifth filling the last eighth of the bar. No fingering or tempo. — [Pianote, 3 Left Hand Piano Patterns For Beginner Players](https://www.pianote.com/blog/left-hand-piano-patterns/)
- **Arpeggio patterns (Lisa Witt, 15 Mar 2024, and 26 Nov 2021).** A one-octave A minor arpeggio fingered left 5-3-2-1 and right 1-2-3-5; a beginner "crossover" (broken triad, cross a hand over, land on the root an octave up); root–fifth–root shapes; a fast arpeggio in one hand against slow chord tones in the other; the advice to release each note and let the arm flow rather than fix on the thumb tuck. The 2021 post gives three hand-over-hand arpeggio studies split between the hands (downloadable in F major), advising finger 2 or 3 rather than 5 on the top crossing note, a metronome for evenness, and pedal held through. — [Pianote, Beginner's Guide to Piano Arpeggios and Patterns](https://www.pianote.com/blog/what-is-an-arpeggio-piano/); [Pianote, 3 Beautiful Arpeggio Exercises](https://www.pianote.com/blog/3-beautiful-arpeggio-exercises/)
- **Technique overview (Charmaine Li, 12 Apr 2024).** Scales in all twelve keys (or one or two a session) in staccato, legato and tenuto, paired with a chord progression or backing track; arpeggios guided by forearm rotation from the elbow; diatonic chords of C played solid and broken, plus inversions; Hanon as "unique patterns"; a pedal-change drill (lift with each chord); metronome rule of starting well under tempo and raising it 3 to 5 bpm at a time. — [Pianote, The Ultimate Piano Technique Resource](https://www.pianote.com/blog/ultimate-piano-technique/)
- **Baseline fingerings Pianote teaches.** One-octave C major: right hand 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5 up, left hand 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1 up; root-position triads fingered 1-3-5 (right) and 5-3-1 (left), built as outer "shell" first then the third; a loud-in-one-hand, soft-in-the-other five-finger drill; and the statement that 15 minutes of daily practice is enough to see progress. — [Pianote, How to Play Piano – The Ultimate Guide](https://www.pianote.com/blog/how-to-play-piano/)

#### Piano With Jonny (Jonny May; "Quick Tip" posts written by site staff, own write-ups)
- **Five warm-ups in a five-note C position.** "Neighbors" (step away from a chord tone and back), "Pivot" (intervals widening from the thumb: second, third, fourth, fifth; harder variant pivoting on the little finger), "Trio" (three-note figures in contrary motion, starting from the thumbs), "Skipper" (thirds, thumbs outward, then reversed) and a pedalled "Film Score" pattern. Suggested variations: extreme dynamics, different dynamics per hand, staccato, varied slurs, parallel octaves, C minor, other keys, hands four octaves apart. Guidance: if you cannot stay relaxed the tempo is too fast; spend no more than about 15% of practice time on technique; a 50 BPM gain over a month at ten minutes a day beats 25 BPM gained in an hour. — [Piano With Jonny, 5 Essential Piano Exercises](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-essential-piano-exercises/)
- **Chord-quality cycles through all 12 roots.** Beginner: on each root play major, sus4, sus2, minor, diminished, augmented triads in that order (72 chords), watching the single note that moves between qualities; about 5 minutes a day, adding a line a week if that is too long. Intermediate: major 7th, dominant 7th, minor 7th, half-diminished, diminished 7th on all 12 roots (60 chords), naming each aloud. The page's month-by-month tempo table is an image I could not read, and the order of roots is not stated in the text. — [Piano With Jonny, The Top Exercise to Practice Piano Chords](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/the-top-exercise-to-practice-piano-chords/)
- **Daily five (technique, theory, tunes, improvisation, ear).** The technique item is ascending diatonic seventh chords: blocked, then an eighth-note pattern, then a triplet pattern; target 140 to 160 BPM, starting slower; under five minutes a day; a new key each month. — [Piano With Jonny, 5 Things to Practice on Piano Every Day](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-things-to-practice-on-piano-every-day/)
- **One speed exercise ("Jonny's Magic Exercise").** Right hand in triplets using the finger groups 1-2-3, 2-3-4, 3-4-5, 4-5-4 from a five-note position starting on F, the whole hand shifting up one white key each bar to near the top of the keyboard, then descending with 5-4-3, 4-3-2, 3-2-1, 2-1-2; the left hand alternates two three-note chords (an F sus2 shape and a first-inversion C triad) in a fixed three-hits-per-bar rhythm. Three steps: right hand, left-hand rhythm, together. Start slowly and build speed with no upper limit; no BPM. — [Piano With Jonny, 1 Piano Exercise for Speed, Strength & Control](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/1-piano-exercise-for-speed-strength-control/)
- **Stride left hand.** Entry level: bass note, then jump to the rest of the chord; easier still, jump to just the third and seventh, keeping successive after-chords close; learn slowly, then play with a backing track (the lesson is rated intermediate/advanced). The fuller exercise takes the diatonic chords of C in an "oom-pah": bass on beats 1 and 3 (in tenths, rolled if the hand cannot reach), a four-note chord on beats 2 and 4, each chord approached by a diminished seventh a half step below. — [Piano With Jonny, Learn How to Play Stride Piano](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/learn-how-to-play-stride-piano/); [Piano With Jonny, The #1 Stride Piano Exercise](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/the-1-stride-piano-exercise/)

#### Robert Estrin (Living Pianos; own articles and episode notes)
- **Scales and arpeggios on a metronome ladder (29 Aug 2012; restated Jan 2026).** Metronome at 60. One note per beat with raised, rounded fingers; when the scale has gone comfortably four times in a row, two notes per beat; after four clean repetitions, four notes per beat, now staying close to the keys and lighter. If four per beat fails, lower the metronome and climb back one notch at a time. Use four octaves, prepare the thumb early (tucked under the hand going up in the right hand, down in the left), keep the arms quiet and let the fingers work. The 2026 article frames this as 5 to 10 minutes a day on one scale a week, starting with C major, with arpeggios done the same way, and tells the player to listen for evenness and clean, non-overlapping notes. — [Living Pianos, How to Practice Scales and Arpeggios](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/); [Living Pianos, 10 Minutes That Will Transform Your Piano Playing](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/10-minutes-that-will-transform-your-piano-playing/)
- **Arpeggios in "chords" (Dec 2018).** Besides the ladder (about five minutes at one note per beat before speeding up), block the notes that lie under the hand together and then play the thumb note, landing on the thumb securely; two notes to the beat is the working speed for this. — [Living Pianos, 3 Ways to Practice Arpeggios](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/3-ways-to-practice-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)
- **Hanon as a ten-minute daily warm-up (Nov 2017).** Same ladder at 60 (one, two, then four notes per beat), the whole exercise about four times at a fairly strong volume, fingers raised and rounded, no arm motion, one new exercise a week; after about ten exercises move on to scales; "Faster = Lighter". — [Living Pianos, Learning Hanon Part 1](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/learning-hanon-part-1-the-first-exercises-the-virtuoso-pianist/)
- **Broken triads through four qualities (Oct 2017).** On each root play major, minor, diminished, augmented in a seamless line, then move up chromatically; the triad is split between the hands and all five fingers are used; a few minutes; no tempo given. — [Living Pianos, The Best Piano Exercises (Part 1) – Broken Triads](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/the-best-piano-exercises-part-1-broken-triads/)
- **Octaves from the wrist (Oct 2017).** A C major scale in octaves at 60, one note per beat, all up-and-down motion from the wrist with the arm only steering, an arch between thumb and little finger, other fingers out of the way; then two notes per beat, adding a note per beat until the limit; stay close to the keys and lighten as speed rises; daily. — [Living Pianos, The Best Piano Exercises (Part 4) – Octaves](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/the-best-piano-exercises-part-4-octaves/)
- **His "four that work" (18 Sep 2025, episode notes only).** Scales, arpeggios, wrist technique and octaves, aimed at intermediate and advanced players; the notes give purposes but no patterns. — [Living Pianos, 4 Piano Exercises That Actually Work](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/4-piano-exercises-that-actually-work/)
- **Longer-term targets (snippet).** A search summary of his Virtual Sheet Music page gives scales at 72 with four notes per beat and arpeggios at 60 with four notes per beat as a starting target; the page refused my direct request (HTTP 403). — [Virtual Sheet Music, How Much Should You Practice Scales?](https://www.virtualsheetmusic.com/experts/robert/piano-scales/)

#### Graham Fitch (Practising the Piano blog and Online Academy; own write-ups)
- **A scale process on a fixed pulse (27 Sep 2013).** Keep one pulse (about 60, or whatever is comfortable): one octave in quarter notes, slow, firm and forte; two octaves in eighths, lighter; three octaves in triplets with a very light finger touch; four octaves in sixteenths; optionally once as fast as possible. Hands separately and together. He credits the idea to the teacher Nehama Patkin. — [Practising the Piano, A Beautiful Process for Scales](https://practisingthepiano.com/a-beautiful-process-for-scales/)
- **Arpeggio practice plan (2 May 2014), for players who already know their arpeggios.** A chain of every arpeggio that contains a chosen note, changing one note at a time without stopping, with a new starting note each day and varied dynamics, speed, rhythm and compass (two to four octaves); a "Russian" pattern combining similar and contrary motion, begun very slowly; and rhythm practice in which he advises against dotted rhythms (they make the arm jerky), preferring four notes slow and firm followed by four fast and light. — [Practising the Piano, Arpeggio Practice Plan](https://practisingthepiano.com/arpeggio_practice_plan/)
- **Hanon as a "blank canvas" for movement (8 Jul 2021).** He uses Hanon No. 1 to train thumb flexibility (hands separately, pairing the thumb with finger 2, then 3, adding 4 and 5 if comfortable, without the elbow bobbing) and sideways wrist adjustments that keep the forearm behind the playing finger; he rejects "finger strengthening" and lifting each finger high in isolation, and suggests watching the hand. No tempo advice. — [Practising the Piano, Jailbreaking Hanon](https://practisingthepiano.com/jailbreaking-hanon-piano-exercises/)
- **On exercises in general (11 Jul 2019).** What matters is how exercises are done: with a specific goal and attention to sound and sensation; "Two or three repetitions with the full involvement of the mind and the ear will usually suffice". He does not assign fixed-position finger-lifting drills and warns that isolating fingers from hand and arm produces tension. — [Practising the Piano, On Technical Exercises](https://practisingthepiano.com/on-technical-exercises/)
- **Rhythms and accents (snippet).** His Online Academy workshop covers rhythms in groups of twos and threes, syncopated rhythms and shifting accents for scales and passagework, and when to avoid them. — [Informance Online Academy, Practising in Rhythms and Accents](https://online-academy.informance.biz/store/product/154-practising-in-rhythms-and-accents)

#### Hoffman Academy (own blog)
- **Beginner sequence.** Whole-body and wrist stretches and a finger "wiggle"; pentascales (five-finger patterns) by step and by skip, singing finger numbers, then hands together in similar and in contrary motion; "finger skips"; Hanon only later as a "super challenge" (introduced in their Unit 16); curved fingers "like holding an orange", relaxed shoulders, elbows and wrists, short focused bursts. — [Hoffman Academy, Piano Exercises for Beginners](https://hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-exercises-for-beginners)
- **"Finger Power".** After major and minor pentascales come finger-skip patterns: for the 1–2 skip the right thumb starts on middle C, finger 2 skips to E, fingers 3, 4, 5 take F, G, A, the fingers step back down, and the pattern restarts one note higher each time; versions exist for skips between 1–2, 2–3, 3–4 and 4–5, hands separately then together. Next come Hanon, one-octave scales, then arpeggios, chords, progressions and inversions. Use a metronome, start slowly; an away-from-the-piano drill is tapping random finger orders (for example 1 5 3 2 4) on a table. — [Hoffman Academy, Finger Power](https://www.hoffmanacademy.com/blog/finger-power/)
- **Scales.** Order of learning: pentascales, one octave, then two octaves; C, G, D, A and E major share one fingering; hands separately for the first few years; say finger numbers at the crossings; a new scale each week covers all 24 in about six months; they prefer melodic minor played the same up and down. No tempo figures. — [Hoffman Academy, All Piano Scales Guide](https://www.hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-scales)

#### PianoTV (Allysia; own blog posts with printable sheets)
- **Five hand-independence exercises for near-beginners (8 Oct 2016).** A two-hand C major scale with one hand loud and the other soft, then reversed; the same with one hand legato and the other staccato; a canon (a familiar round with the left hand entering about a bar later); a five-finger pattern with steady notes in one hand against dotted and eighth-note rhythms in the other; and that last one again with staccato in one hand against short slurs in the other. She points advanced players to Bach's two-part inventions (roughly grade 7 to 8) as the "gold standard". No tempo. — [PianoTV, 5 Piano Exercises for Hand Independence](https://www.pianotv.net/2016/10/piano-exercises-hand-independence/)
- **Five speed exercises (21 Oct 2017).** A one-chord arpeggio with the left hand crossing over the right and back; a longer cross-hand arpeggio over several octaves with pedal; a shortened Czerny Op. 299 study (two-octave scales in the right hand over simple left-hand chords, with a two-octave leap inside a sixteenth rest); and two Czerny passage patterns, fast five-finger figures in one hand over two alternating chords in the other, then reversed, looped until the fingers loosen and memorised to push speed. No BPM. — [PianoTV, 5 Piano Exercises for Speed](https://www.pianotv.net/2017/10/piano-exercises-for-speed/)
- **Five held-note finger-independence exercises for early intermediates (24 Apr 2018).** Hold one finger (thumb or little finger) while the others play sixteenth-note figures (two from Czerny, one being Op. 802 No. 10); alternate which outer finger holds (Henselt); change the held pair each bar (1 and 3, 2 and 4, 3 and 5); and hold three fingers while the inner ones move (Isidor Philipp). Purpose: "finger pedalling". Go slowly, one hand at a time; the held finger will want to lift. — [PianoTV, 5 Exercises for Finger Independence](https://www.pianotv.net/2018/04/5-exercises-for-finger-independence-early-intermediate/)

#### Denis Zhdanov (course page, own description)
- His paid course teaches technique and musicality through 21 Hanon numbers (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 13, 19, 20, 23, 32, 37, 38, 40, 41, 44, 45, 48, 50, 51, 58). Lesson titles show what each is used for: an "in and out" motion, phrasing and forearm rotation on No. 1; Nos. 5 and 6 as preparation for broken octaves; soft playing; crescendo with weaker fingers on a short arpeggio (No. 20); extreme dynamic changes (No. 23); thumb training and position changes (Nos. 32, 37); scales (No. 38) and chromatic scales (No. 40); long arpeggios (No. 41); repeated notes (No. 44); thirds (No. 50); octaves (Nos. 51, 58). His stated view: "we should never separate technical and musical aspects". — [Arteprism courses, Basics of Piano Technique & Musicality via Hanon Exercises](https://courses.arteprism.org/p/a-comprehensive-guide-for-piano-technique-musicality-via-hanon-exercises)

#### Josh Wright (third-party write-up of his ProPractice course, plus his store page)
- A reviewer describes three technique tiers: mid-to-late beginner work on five-finger patterns using Schmitt's Preparatory Exercises, Hanon and Czerny (all free on IMSLP); intermediate work on scales, arpeggios and triads with one video per key plus the chromatic scale, each key video showing a different way to practise (twelve in all), with "Pro Tips" on playing faster, fluidity, awkward and black-key arpeggios, clarity, lining the hands up with the metronome and a "cycling" method; and advanced technique through Chopin études. He stresses clean releases and a relaxed technique. — [Girl in Blue Music, Piano Course Review: ProPractice](https://girlinbluemusic.com/piano-course-review-propractice-by-dr-josh-wright/)
- His own store page confirms the series covers scales, arpeggios, triads and seventh chords in 12 keys plus chromatic scales, with "12 different practice techniques", but does not name them. — [Josh Wright Piano, Complete ProPractice Technique Series](https://www.joshwrightpiano.com/store/p80/COMPLETE_ProPractice_Technique_Series.html)

#### tonebase Piano (own blog)
- **A teacher's lesson order that doubles as a routine (Nicole DiPaolo, 24 Jul 2023).** A "wiggle" warm-up for thumb and little finger; chromatic scales in parallel or contrary motion; major or minor scales rotating round the circle of fifths; cadence progressions (I–IV–I–V–I) in all keys, split into four groups (sharp majors, flat majors, sharp minors, flat minors) and rotated so all are covered in a month; arpeggios; repertoire; sight-reading. Breaks at least every 45 minutes. Required technique is only scales (all three minor forms), arpeggios and those cadences; otherwise she makes short studies out of the hard passages in the student's pieces. — [tonebase, How to Practice Piano – Complete Guide](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/piano-practice)
- **Scale guides tied to repertoire (2 Jul 2023).** The C major guide gives standard fingerings and then graded reductions of passages from a Clementi sonatina and a Mozart sonata (melody alone, then with bass, then as written), with singing and scale-degree work; no metronome marks. — [tonebase, C Major Scale: A Piano Practice Guide](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/c-major-scale-piano)

#### Nahre Sol (own shop pages; no written account of her video advice found)

The pages state no practice or tempo advice. — [Nahre Sol shop, 5 LH Warm-Ups Exercises in All Transpositions](https://www.nahresol.com/shop/p/5-lh-warm-ups-exercises-in-all-transpositions-beg-int); [Nahre Sol shop, Intermediate/Advanced Piano Exercises](https://www.nahresol.com/shop/p/modified-chopin-exercises-volume-one)

#### Jazer Lee (third-party write-up only) and a magazine warm-up set
- Pianist magazine's warm-up article (Ellie Palmer, 9 Sep 2021) includes "ten exercises based on the C major scale, adapted from a Jazer Lee lesson", starting with similar and contrary motion and moving to progressively harder syncopation between the hands. The same article gives two silent stretching drills (hold a diminished-seventh hand shape without sounding it and lift one finger at a time; a left-hand version walking each finger a semitone up and down), and Hanon Nos. 1 and 2 hands separately then together with loose wrists, the wrist dropping at the top of each phrase; it says No. 2 takes about two minutes. — [Pianist Magazine, 5 effective warm-up exercises](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/5-effective-warm-up-exercises-to-include-in-your-piano-practice)

### Inferences
- The exercises that can be rebuilt as generated patterns from prose alone (no teacher's notation needed) are: five-finger pattern in any position with per-hand articulation, dynamics and 2:1 rhythm switches; contrary-motion scale with a swell; triad shape walked up a scale, solid then broken; chord-quality cycle on each of 12 roots; diatonic seventh chords blocked, in eighths, in triplets; scale ladder (1, 2, 4 notes per beat or 1 to 4 octaves on a fixed pulse); arpeggio ladder and "block then thumb" arpeggio; hand-over-hand and cross-hand arpeggios; four-chord progression routines in a key with left-hand figures (fifths, root–fifth–root, half arpeggio, full arpeggio, stride); two-line coordination drills; held-note drills; pickup-to-target "burst" scales; octave scale from the wrist.
- Where teachers give numbers they cluster at a slow anchor (60 per beat) with speed coming from subdividing, not from raising the metronome, and at very short daily doses (2 to 10 minutes). That suggests exercises of roughly 30 seconds to 2 minutes each, grouped into a 5 to 10 minute block.
- What teachers ask the player to listen and watch for is consistent enough to turn into feedback text: evenness of volume and timing, hands exactly together, one hand clearly louder than the other when asked, clean releases with no overlapping notes, landing on the beat after a crossing, lighter touch as speed rises, relaxed shoulders, a little finger that stays near the keys.

### Gaps

A Scribd-hosted copy of his "Piano Pack no. 2" appeared in a search summary as containing exercises for the fourth and fifth fingers and Alberti-bass patterns, with a practice checklist; I did not open it and cannot vouch for it ([Scribd listing](https://www.scribd.com/document/704092221/Bill-Hilton-s-Piano-Pack-2)).

- **Nahre Sol:** only shop descriptions; nothing written about her practice videos.

- **Denis Zhdanov:** the course page lists lesson titles but not how each exercise is practised; I found no free written account of his warm-up videos.

## 2. Which exercises come up again and again across teachers, and where do teachers disagree (for example on Hanon)?

### Takeaway
Ten families recur across at least three independent sources: five-finger patterns, metronome-laddered scales, arpeggios, moving a triad shape and cycling chord types through keys, cadences and four-chord progressions in every key, left-hand accompaniment figures, two-hand independence drills on a scale, held-note finger independence, speed drills, and (less often, and with less written detail) octaves and leaps. The live disagreements are about Hanon (daily warm-up, vehicle for movement, or not used at all), about lifting the fingers, about how much to think about the thumb, about metronome versus backing track, and about how much time technique deserves.

### Cited Findings

#### Recurring families
- **Five-finger (pentascale) patterns as the first exercise:** Pianote's beginner routine and "4 exercises" ([routine](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine-for-beginners/), [4 exercises](https://www.pianote.com/blog/beginner-exercises/)); Hoffman Academy calls pentascales "one of the first and best" exercises ([Hoffman](https://hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-exercises-for-beginners)); Piano With Jonny's five warm-ups need only five notes ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-essential-piano-exercises/)); Josh Wright's mid-beginner tier is five-finger patterns from Schmitt, Hanon and Czerny ([review](https://girlinbluemusic.com/piano-course-review-propractice-by-dr-josh-wright/)).
- **Scales with a slow anchor and subdivision:** Estrin's 60 with one, two, four notes per beat ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Fitch's fixed pulse with one to four octaves ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/a-beautiful-process-for-scales/)); Pianote's level-by-level keys with two notes per click at 90 ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/classical-piano-exercises/)); tonebase's rotation round the circle of fifths ([tonebase](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/piano-practice)); Hoffman's one new scale a week ([Hoffman](https://www.hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-scales)).
- **Arpeggios:** Estrin (ladder and block-then-thumb) ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/3-ways-to-practice-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Fitch (chains, similar-plus-contrary, grouped rhythms) ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/arpeggio_practice_plan/)); Pianote (crossover and hand-over-hand) ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/what-is-an-arpeggio-piano/)); PianoTV (cross-hand) ([PianoTV](https://www.pianotv.net/2017/10/piano-exercises-for-speed/)).
- **Chord shapes and chord types through the keys:** Pianote's triad shape walked up the white keys ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/beginner-exercises/)); Piano With Jonny's six triad types and five seventh-chord types on all 12 roots ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/the-top-exercise-to-practice-piano-chords/)); Estrin's major–minor–diminished–augmented broken triads moving chromatically ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/the-best-piano-exercises-part-1-broken-triads/)).
- **Cadences and progressions in every key:** tonebase's I–IV–I–V–I in all keys in four rotating groups ([tonebase](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/piano-practice)); Pianote's four-chord routines in one key with inversions practised inside the progression ([D minor routine](https://www.pianote.com/blog/d-minor-practice-routine/), [warm-ups](https://www.pianote.com/blog/easy-piano-warm-ups/)); Piano With Jonny's diatonic sevenths in a new key each month ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-things-to-practice-on-piano-every-day/)); Nahre Sol's ii–V–I left-hand warm-ups in 12 keys ([Nahre Sol shop](https://www.nahresol.com/shop/p/5-lh-warm-ups-exercises-in-all-transpositions-beg-int)).
- **Left-hand accompaniment figures:** fifths, root–fifth–root, half and full arpeggios over I–V–vi–IV ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/left-hand-piano-patterns/)); stride from "root then chord" up to tenths ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/learn-how-to-play-stride-piano/)); a classical broken-chord (Alberti) accompaniment appears in tonebase's Clementi reductions ([tonebase](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/c-major-scale-piano)).
- **Hand independence on a scale or five-finger pattern (loud against soft, legato against staccato, even against uneven rhythm, similar against contrary):** PianoTV ([PianoTV](https://www.pianotv.net/2016/10/piano-exercises-hand-independence/)); Pianote ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/beginner-hand-independence-exercises/)); the Jazer Lee set as adapted by Pianist magazine ([Pianist](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/5-effective-warm-up-exercises-to-include-in-your-piano-practice)).
- **Held-note finger independence:** PianoTV's Czerny, Henselt and Philipp set ([PianoTV](https://www.pianotv.net/2018/04/5-exercises-for-finger-independence-early-intermediate/)); Pianist magazine's silent held-chord lifts ([Pianist](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/5-effective-warm-up-exercises-to-include-in-your-piano-practice)).
- **Speed drills:** pickup-to-target bursts ([Pianote/Rudess](https://www.pianote.com/blog/how-to-play-scales-fast/)); a shifting triplet pattern ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/1-piano-exercise-for-speed-strength-control/)); looped Czerny five-finger passages ([PianoTV](https://www.pianotv.net/2017/10/piano-exercises-for-speed/)); four slow then four fast notes ([Fitch](https://practisingthepiano.com/arpeggio_practice_plan/)).
- **Octaves and leaps (less written detail):** Estrin's wrist-octave scale ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/the-best-piano-exercises-part-4-octaves/)); Zhdanov's use of Hanon Nos. 5, 6, 51 and 58 for broken octaves and octaves ([course page](https://courses.arteprism.org/p/a-comprehensive-guide-for-piano-technique-musicality-via-hanon-exercises)); leaps inside stride ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/the-1-stride-piano-exercise/)) and inside a Czerny study ([PianoTV](https://www.pianotv.net/2017/10/piano-exercises-for-speed/)).
- **Thumb-under work:** Estrin says prepare the thumb early, tucked under the hand ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Fitch trains thumb flexibility on Hanon No. 1 and warns against dropping the arm onto the thumb ([Jailbreaking Hanon](https://practisingthepiano.com/jailbreaking-hanon-piano-exercises/), [scales process](https://practisingthepiano.com/a-beautiful-process-for-scales/)); Hoffman has students say the finger number at each crossing ([Hoffman](https://www.hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-scales)).

#### Disagreements
- **Hanon, for:** Estrin recommends about ten minutes daily, one exercise a week, with raised fingers at slow speeds ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/learning-hanon-part-1-the-first-exercises-the-virtuoso-pianist/)). Pianote includes Hanon in a warm-up and as one of three classical exercises ([warm-ups](https://www.pianote.com/blog/easy-piano-warm-ups/), [classical exercises](https://www.pianote.com/blog/classical-piano-exercises/)). Hoffman Academy brings it in after pentascales as a later challenge ([Hoffman](https://hoffmanacademy.com/blog/piano-exercises-for-beginners)). Josh Wright's beginner tier uses Schmitt, Hanon and Czerny ([review](https://girlinbluemusic.com/piano-course-review-propractice-by-dr-josh-wright/)).
- **Hanon, repurposed:** Fitch says the patterns are harmless and useful only as frameworks for wrist, thumb and rotation movements, not for "finger strengthening" ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/jailbreaking-hanon-piano-exercises/)). Zhdanov teaches Hanon numbers as material for phrasing, dynamics and rotation and says technique and music should never be separated ([course page](https://courses.arteprism.org/p/a-comprehensive-guide-for-piano-technique-musicality-via-hanon-exercises)).
- **Hanon, against:** tonebase's Nicole DiPaolo does not use Hanon (she notes Hanon was an organist), regards Czerny as harmless but less rewarding than Mozart, Haydn and early Beethoven, and prefers studies made from the student's own hard passages ([tonebase](https://www.tonebase.co/piano-blog-posts/piano-practice)). Pianote's 2025 routine warms up on the song's own chords instead ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine/)). tonebase's own community post says many pianists have a "love/hate relationship" with Hanon ([tonebase community](https://piano-community.tonebase.co/t/x2haq9x)).
- **Finger lifting:** Estrin asks for "raised, rounded fingers" at slow speeds ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Fitch warns against lifting each finger high and in isolation ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/jailbreaking-hanon-piano-exercises/)).
- **The thumb in fast scales:** Estrin wants it prepared under the hand ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Jordan Rudess, on Pianote, says to stop focusing on each tuck and let the arm carry the hand ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/how-to-play-scales-fast/)).
- **Arm and wrist:** Estrin says scales and arpeggios come from the fingers with a quiet arm ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/10-minutes-that-will-transform-your-piano-playing/)); Pianote says to guide arpeggios with forearm rotation from the elbow ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/ultimate-piano-technique/)); Fitch's scale process relies on a gliding arm ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/a-beautiful-process-for-scales/)).
- **Metronome or backing track:** Estrin says always use a metronome ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/videos/how-to-practice-scales-and-arpeggios-on-the-piano/)); Pianote suggests backing tracks instead of only a metronome ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine/)), as does Piano With Jonny ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-essential-piano-exercises/)).
- **Dotted rhythms:** Fitch advises against them for arpeggios ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/arpeggio_practice_plan/)).
- **How much technique:** Piano With Jonny caps it at about 15% of practice ([PWJ](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/5-essential-piano-exercises/)); Estrin suggests 5 to 10 minutes a day ([Living Pianos](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/10-minutes-that-will-transform-your-piano-playing/)); Fitch says two or three attentive repetitions usually suffice ([Practising the Piano](https://practisingthepiano.com/on-technical-exercises/)); Pianote's routine gives the warm-up 3 to 5 minutes ([Pianote](https://www.pianote.com/blog/piano-practice-routine/)).

### Inferences
- For an adult around grades 1 to 5 who already has scales, arpeggios and finger-independence drills, the families not yet covered are chords (shape walks, quality cycles, inversions inside progressions, cadences in every key), left-hand accompaniment figures, two-hand independence variants layered onto existing scales, speed "bursts", octaves and leaps.
- The Hanon dispute is mostly about manner, not notes: every critic above objects to mechanical, high-fingered repetition, and every supporter prescribes slow tempo, short duration and attention. An app can sidestep it by generating short patterns and attaching a specific thing to listen for.
- Because the teachers disagree on finger height and thumb use, feedback text on those points should be offered as options ("some teachers suggest") rather than rules; evenness, synchrony, balance between the hands and clean releases are uncontroversial and are things MIDI can measure.

### Gaps
- PianoTV's view of Hanon ("personally not a huge fan", preferring Czerny) came only from a search summary of pianotv.net; the likely page ([PianoTV Q&A, Feb 2017](https://www.pianotv.net/2017/02/qa-pianotv-polyrhythm-flying-pinkies/)) returned empty when fetched, so the wording is unverified.
- I found no written statement of Josh Wright's, Jazer Lee's, Bill Hilton's or Annique Göttler's position on Hanon.
- Written detail on octaves, leaps and Alberti bass from these teachers is thin; the families are confirmed as recurring, but tempo and length advice for them is mostly missing.

## 3. What technique content do practice apps that listen through MIDI or the microphone include, and how do they score it?

### Takeaway
The apps score two things, the right note and its timing, and mostly stop there; only Piano Marvel publishes numeric bands (80, 90, 96 and 100%) and a test rule (the SASR: 20 seconds to look, 80% to advance, three strikes to end, score 100 to 1900), while Melodics documents its mechanics (per-note timing precision plus streaks, stars up to "Platinum", Auto BPM in 10 BPM steps, Wait Mode) without numbers. No vendor I could read publishes a timing window in milliseconds. Dedicated technique content is thin in the song-led apps and richest in Piano Marvel, Melodics' drills, and a handful of small scale apps.

### Cited Findings

#### Piano Marvel
- **What is scored.** Play Mode "primarily assesses note accuracy and rhythm accuracy"; dynamics and articulation are not assessed; the score covers the whole performance. Bands: 80% bronze, 90% silver, 96% gold, 100% "super gold". — [Piano Marvel, Play Mode](https://pianomarvel.com/feature/play-mode)
- **Timing and length count.** The support article on notes marked wrong says "Even small timing differences can affect scoring", lists playing early, late, rushing, dragging, cutting notes short, holding too long and ignoring rests as causes, and notes that the pedal is not detected over MIDI; it describes Practice mode as slowing things down and Learn mode as waiting for correct notes. No tolerance figure is given. — [Piano Marvel support, Scoring Issues](https://support.pianomarvel.com/portal/en/kb/articles/scoring)
- **Practice Mode.** The player sets the pace and the app follows; it is unavailable in boot camps and sight-reading tests; the recommended sequence is "Video, Practice, Play". — [Piano Marvel, Practice Mode](https://pianomarvel.com/feature/practice-mode)
- **Structure and rewards.** Correct notes turn green and wrong notes red; bronze, silver and gold trophies go into two separate trophy cases, one for the Method and one for technique exercises. — [Piano Marvel, About us](https://pianomarvel.com/about-us). A 2018 teacher review says the library includes Method and Technique levels 1 to 6 and that completing a level earns a trophy. — [TopMusic review, 3 Apr 2018](https://topmusic.co/piano-marvel-the-creative-online-piano-lesson-system-product-review/)
- **Technique content.** The App Store listing says Premium adds courses for scales, arpeggios and chords, plus boot camps, sight-reading, ear training and harmonization, and that assessment works over MIDI or by microphone on an acoustic piano. — [App Store, Piano Marvel](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/piano-marvel-lessons-music/id1016751878)
- **Sight-reading test (SASR).** 18 levels of 5 sublevels (90 in all); scores run 100 to 1900, each 100 being one level; the test picks excerpts from earlier results; the page gives an average score of 404. — [Piano Marvel, SASR](https://pianomarvel.com/feature/sasr). The player has 20 seconds to look over each excerpt before a count-in; 80% or more brings a harder excerpt; under 80% earns a strike; three strikes end the test and produce the score. — [Piano Marvel, The Ultimate Sight Reading Test](https://pianomarvel.com/article/the-ultimate-sight-reading-test)

#### Melodics (keys)
- **Scoring now (article dated 14 May 2026).** "Every note is measured for how closely on time it was – not just whether it counted as a hit"; consecutive correct notes build a streak and a miss resets it; one, two, three or Platinum stars, Platinum being an all-green run; "There's no ceiling for how high your score can go", scores can exceed 100, and longer lessons score higher. — [Melodics support, Lesson scoring](https://support.melodics.com/en/articles/15092017-lesson-scoring)
- **Practice tools (8 Dec 2025).** A BPM slider down to a stop or above tempo; Auto BPM, which will "ramp up the speed by 10 BPM if you're nailing it"; looping; Wait Mode, which pauses until the correct note is played. — [Melodics support, Practice Mode](https://support.melodics.com/en/articles/6777027-practice-mode)
- **Progress.** XP feeds a "Stardom" rank described as a dedication rating, not a skill rating; each instrument has 15 "Stages" that act as tests; a fresh set of lessons is suggested each Monday. — [Melodics support, Understanding your progress](https://support.melodics.com/en/articles/15091765-understanding-your-progress-in-melodics)
- **Technique content.** Besides lessons and courses, keys "exercises" are "repeatable drills that focus on developing muscle memory", with scales and intervals named; the default daily goal is 5 minutes and consecutive days build a streak. — [Melodics, How it works](https://melodics.com/how-it-works)

#### Playground Sessions
- "Play a note right, it turns green; play it wrong, it turns red; play it at the wrong time, it turns pink." Tools: slower tempo, looping, one hand at a time, metronome, backing tracks. — [Playground Sessions, product page](https://start.playgroundsessions.com/real-piano-learning)
- A review (updated 11 July 2022) describes a Bootcamp in Rookie, Intermediate and Advanced tiers, the five-finger position introduced in the second lesson, a percentage and points after each lesson, levels reached by points, 15 stars earning song credits, and the need for a MIDI keyboard. — [Top Ten Reviews, Playground Sessions](https://www.toptenreviews.com/playground-sessions-review)

#### Simply Piano
- **Feedback colours (14 Sep 2026).** Blue: correct with no hint; yellow: correct after the app showed a hint on its on-screen keyboard; red: wrong note, shown in moving stages only; grey keys show what the app is hearing. — [Simply Piano help, The basics](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/7943490-learning-with-simply-piano-the-basics)
- **Stars (31 Aug 2026).** "Star levels" are practice sections using library songs; up to 3 stars per song, and a stated number of stars (shown as a fraction such as 0/6) is needed to move on. How a star is earned is not documented. — [Simply Piano help, Understanding Star Levels](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/7943591-understanding-star-levels)
- **Technique content.** The "Soloist" path is described as "playing melodies, expanding your note range, and polishing your technique" and the "Chords" path as accompaniment and chord fundamentals. — [Simply Piano help, Soloist and Chords](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/2791299-soloist-and-chords-choosing-a-course-path). Weekly "5-minute workouts" (flash cards, song-library training, on-the-go stages) exist but, as of 6 Aug 2026, "some players may no longer see" them because the app is "refreshing how practice works". — [Simply Piano help, 5-minute workouts](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/7939170-what-are-5-minute-workouts)
- Tempo options appear in the moving stages of its song library, and live "Feedback" along a moving cursor is iOS-only. — [Simply Piano help, Understanding PLAY](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/7943680-understanding-play)

#### flowkey
- A walkthrough (24 Sep 2025) describes Wait Mode (the app waits for the correct note), 50% and 75% speeds, practising one hand at a time, listening through the microphone, and a scale lesson that shows a video of the hands and then has the player play with the next key highlighted, a tick for each correct note and a pause on a wrong one. It mentions courses on scales and arpeggios, playing with both hands and reading. No numeric score is described. — [Pianist Magazine, Learn all the songs that you love with flowkey](https://pianistmagazine.com/blogs/learn-all-the-songs-that-you-love-with-flowkey)

#### Skoove
- An independent review (updated 15 Sep 2026) says every lesson runs "Listen, Learn, Play"; the app "grades pitch and timing, and stops there", so note length, phrasing, dynamics and pedalling are not judged; input is USB or Bluetooth MIDI or the microphone; and reviewers found no technical framework for hand independence, chord inversions or fingering. — [Pianoers, Skoove review](https://pianoers.com/skoove-review/)

#### Yousician (snippets: its help pages refused my direct requests with HTTP 403)
- Each song part earns from one silver to three gold stars; gold needs everything right with good timing; on piano, correct, well-timed notes raise a points multiplier up to 5x; the app shows the latest and the best score. — [Yousician support, Song scoring and leaderboards](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/208014795-Song-scoring-and-leaderboards)
- The piano syllabus has 9 levels in its Classical and Pop themes and 5 in a Knowledge theme, where mini-games cover vocabulary, scales and chord training. — [Yousician support, How the piano syllabus works](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/204697992-How-the-piano-syllabus-works)

#### Synthesia
- Melody Practice "pauses the piece whenever the user misses a note"; the program rates the performance afterwards and gives a score that can be posted to an online scoreboard; it takes MIDI or computer-keyboard input. — [Wikipedia, Synthesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthesia_(video_game)). Its own guide shows left-hand notes in blue, right-hand in green and optional finger numbers, and lists no guide on scoring. — [Synthesia guide, Playing your first song](https://synthesia.app/support/guide/firstSong); [Synthesia guides](https://synthesia.app/support/guide)

#### Apps devoted to scales and technique
- **ABRSM Piano Scales Trainer** (£4.99): notation with suggested fingering, example performances, a metronome, a backing track for each scale, a test mode that picks scales for the grade, a recorder with self-rating, and a practice meter; nothing indicates it listens and marks. — [Pianist Magazine, ABRSM's digital support for pianists, 10 July 2020](https://www.pianistmagazine.com/blogs/abrsms-digital-support-for-pianists). A search summary of its store listing says it covers the Grade 1 to 5 scale requirements of the syllabus from 2021 (snippet). — [App Store, ABRSM Piano Scales Trainer](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1511139245)
- **Scale Study: tempo practice** (a small independent app, first released in February 2026 according to a search summary; full detection needs a MIDI keyboard): "listens to your playing, confirms when a scale is correct, and records the fastest tempo you can play it cleanly", with a calendar, filters by scale, key and hand, and an optional prompt asking whether the fingering was correct. — [App Store, Scale Study](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6758663846)
- **MasterPiano: Sight Reading** (MIDI or microphone): a wait mode, and a play-along mode with metronome that scores accuracy, rhythm and tempo and draws a bar-by-bar map of weak passages; levels follow the ABRSM sight-reading grades and version 2.0 mentions scales, arpeggios and chords. — [App Store, MasterPiano](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6758087301)

### Inferences
- The scoring ideas worth weighing for Prima's technique exercises, each traceable above: percentage bands with a pass line at 80% (Piano Marvel); a three-strike adaptive ladder (SASR); a "fastest clean tempo" record per scale, key and hand (Scale Study); automatic tempo ramp in fixed steps after a clean pass (Melodics' 10 BPM); a wait mode for learning the notes; a third colour for "right note, wrong time" (Playground Sessions); marking a note differently when it was only right after a hint (Simply Piano); a per-bar map (MasterPiano).
- The commercial apps largely ignore what teachers say to listen for (evenness, balance between the hands, clean releases, articulation). A MIDI-connected app can measure all of these from note-on and note-off times and velocities, so technique exercises are where a personal app can do something the large ones do not.
- Since nobody publishes timing windows, Prima's own windows have to be set by experiment.

### Gaps

## 4. What do YouTube's Terms of Service and API policies say about downloading or scraping videos, captions and transcripts, including for personal use? What does the YouTube Data API allow for captions?

### Takeaway
YouTube's terms allow viewing for personal, non-commercial use and embedding through its player, and forbid downloading, reproducing or accessing the service by automated means without written permission; the personal-use wording covers watching, not copying. The API rules forbid scraping, storing video, and using anything but the official API to get its data, and the official captions endpoint hands over caption text only to someone with edit rights on the video. So scraping tutorials or their transcripts is not permitted by the terms, whether or not the app is published.

- `captions.list` returns the list of caption tracks for a video, costs 50 quota units, needs authorization with the `youtube.force-ssl` or `youtubepartner` scope, and "the API response does not contain the actual captions". — [YouTube Data API, captions.list](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/captions/list)

#### The law around the terms (personal use included)
- United States: fair use is decided "on a case-by-case basis" on four factors, and "there is no formula" such as a set amount that may be copied. — [U.S. Copyright Office, Fair Use Index](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)
- United States: separately from infringement, "No person shall circumvent a technological measure that effectively controls access to a work protected under this title"; the section says it does not affect defences such as fair use. — [17 U.S.C. § 1201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201)
- Whether stream-ripping tools circumvent such a measure on YouTube is still in court: a district judge dismissed Yout's case against the RIAA at the end of 2022, and the appeal to the Second Circuit was still pending on 3 April 2026. — [TorrentFreak, 3 Apr 2026](https://torrentfreak.com/yout-com-hopes-supreme-courts-cox-ruling-helps-its-case-riaa-disagrees/)
- Terms of service are contracts and their reach is contested: in Meta v. Bright Data (N.D. Cal., 23 Jan 2024) the court held that scraping public pages while logged out did not breach Meta's terms because the scraper was not "using" the service under them; commentators stress the ruling turned on Meta's wording. — [Farella Braun + Martel, Meta Platforms v. Bright Data](https://www.fbm.com/data-analytics/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/)
- European Union: the private-copying exception covers only copies made from lawful sources (ACI Adam, C-435/12, 10 April 2014). — [Kluwer Copyright Blog, No more downloading from unlawful sources?](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/no-more-downloading-from-unlawful-sources/). The general text-and-data-mining exception applies to "lawfully accessible works" and only where the use "has not been expressly reserved by their rightholders in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means". — [Directive (EU) 2019/790, Article 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2019/790/article/4)
- United Kingdom: the private-copying exception was quashed in July 2015, the government stating that such copying "now constitute[s] acts of infringement". — [Pinsent Masons Out-Law, 22 July 2015](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/quashing-of-private-copying-exemption-a-temporary-stay-of-execution-for-music-industry-says-expert). Copying for computational analysis is allowed only to "a person who has lawful access to the work" and "for the sole purpose of research for a non-commercial purpose". — [Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s. 29A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/29A). Fair dealing for non-commercial research or private study covers limited extracts; "copying the whole work would not generally be considered fair dealing". — [GOV.UK, Exceptions to copyright](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exceptions-to-copyright). The government's report of 18 March 2026 says a broad mining exception with opt-out "is no longer the government's preferred way forward". — [GOV.UK, Report on copyright and artificial intelligence](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/report-and-impact-assessment-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/report-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence)

### Inferences
- **Not allowed under the terms, even for a personal, unpublished app:** saving videos or audio with a downloader; fetching watch pages, transcripts or caption files with a script or a transcript library (automated access, and outside the official API); and getting other people's caption text through the official API, which is impossible by design. The "personal, non-commercial" phrase in the terms is the scope of the viewing permission, not an exemption from the download and automation rules.
- **Allowed:** watching the tutorials and taking one's own notes; reading the teachers' own written pages (as these notes do); linking to a video or showing it in YouTube's embedded player from inside the app, following the embed rules; fetching titles and descriptions through the Data API with an API key, within the storage rules; using material a creator has licensed CC BY or given written permission for.
- **A practical, permitted way to draw on the tutorials:** treat each video as a lesson to learn an idea from, not as data to ingest. The owner (or Claude working from the teacher's public written post) writes a short specification in fresh words: pattern, hands, keys, tempo ladder, length, what to listen for. Prima's generator then produces its own notes from that specification, with its own name for the exercise and, if wanted, a "learn more" link to the video. No download, scrape, transcript or copied notation is involved.
- In the UK and the European Economic Area the terms defer to "applicable law", but the laws available there (lawful-source private copying in the EU, no private-copying exception in the UK, lawful-access mining exceptions) do not obviously turn scraping into a permitted act either. The project's own notes put the owner in a United States time zone, so the US terms, which lack that clause, are the ones that apply.

## 5. Under copyright law (United States primarily, with a note on the EU and UK), are exercise patterns, scales, chord drills and teaching methods protected, and what follows for an app that writes its own exercises?

### Takeaway
No: in the United States copyright never covers an idea, method or system, and the Copyright Office names scales, arpeggios and standard chord progressions as common property that nobody owns. What a teacher does own is their expression: the wording of the lesson, the video and its captions, and any exercise that is original music rather than a stock pattern. An app that composes its own exercises from the ideas taught is using what the law leaves free; one that reproduces a teacher's written-out original exercise, their text or their video is copying what the law protects. The EU and UK apply the same idea-versus-expression line.

### Cited Findings

#### Statute, regulation and Copyright Office guidance
- 17 U.S.C. § 102(b) excludes from copyright "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied." The Office adds that for a work describing a method, protection "will extend only to the original expression in that work and not to the underlying idea, methods, or systems described or explained", and gives the example of a book on a food-processing system: the copyright stops others copying the text and illustrations, but "does not give the author the right to prevent others from employing the system". — [U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 33, Works Not Protected by Copyright (rev. 03/2021)](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf); [17 U.S.C. § 102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)
- The Office's regulation lists as not copyrightable "Words and short phrases such as names, titles, and slogans" and "Ideas, plans, methods, systems, or devices, as distinguished from the particular manner in which they are expressed or described in a writing". — [37 C.F.R. § 202.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/202.1)
- Compendium § 802.5(A): "a musical work consisting entirely of common property material would not constitute original authorship", and the examples of common property musical material are "Diatonic or chromatic scales", "Arpeggios" and "Chord symbols based on standard chord progressions". — [Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices (3d ed.), ch. 800](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf)
- Compendium § 802.5(B): "short musical phrases are not copyrightable because they lack a sufficient amount of authorship"; a phrase of only a few notes such as "mi do re sol, sol, re mi do" is too short to register. — [Compendium ch. 800](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf)
- Compendium § 802.6(J), Method Books: they "typically contain common property elements such as scales, arpeggios, chord charts, and musical examples taken from preexisting sources. They also may contain original elements, such as instructional text, new music, and original musical exercises." A book of only common material can be registered, if at all, as a compilation, and "a compilation of all the diatonic major and minor scales would not constitute sufficient creative compilation authorship, because the selection and arrangement is dictated by the Western musical scale system." — [Compendium ch. 800](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf)
- Compendium § 802.6(I): an editor's added "fingering, accents, dynamics, and the like" is the kind of contribution claimed as "musical editing". § 802.5(C): "the automated transposition of a musical work from one key to another is not registrable", nor is music "created solely by a computer algorithm". § 808.8(C): captions that are "a verbatim transcription of the spoken words" add no copyright of their own. — [Compendium ch. 800](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf)
- The Compendium in force is the third edition as released on 28 January 2021. — [U.S. Copyright Office, Compendium](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/)

#### Cases
- Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin (9th Cir. en banc, 9 March 2020): the court approved a jury instruction that copyright does not protect common musical elements such as descending chromatic scales, arpeggios and short sequences of three notes, while protection for a selection and arrangement extends to "the particular way in which the artistic elements form a coherent pattern, synthesis, or design". — [Loeb & Loeb, Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2020/03/skidmore-v-led-zeppelin)
- Structured Asset Sales v. Sheeran (2d Cir., decided 1 November 2024): a four-chord progression and a syncopated harmonic rhythm are unprotectable alone, and protecting the pair would risk "a monopoly over a combination of two fundamental musical building blocks". — [Copyright Lately, Ed Sheeran wins copyright appeal](https://copyrightlately.com/ed-sheeran-wins-copyright-appeal-5-things-to-know/)
- Bikram's Yoga College v. Evolation Yoga (9th Cir., 8 Oct 2015): a fixed sequence of 26 poses "is an idea, process, or system designed to improve health" and so cannot be protected, whether as a compilation or as choreography; quoting Baker v. Selden, "The description of the art in a book, though entitled to the benefit of copyright, lays no foundation for an exclusive claim to the art itself." — [FindLaw, Bikram's Yoga College of India v. Evolation Yoga](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-9th-circuit/1715117.html)

#### Older exercise books
- Works first published or registered in 1930 entered the United States public domain on 1 January 2026, so anything published earlier is already there. — [Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain, Public Domain Day 2026](https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2026/). Hanon's *The Virtuoso Pianist* was published in 1873. — [Pianote, 3 Classical Piano Exercises](https://www.pianote.com/blog/classical-piano-exercises/). Josh Wright's beginner course relies on Schmitt, Hanon and Czerny as free on IMSLP. — [Girl in Blue Music review](https://girlinbluemusic.com/piano-course-review-propractice-by-dr-josh-wright/)

#### European Union and United Kingdom
- The international rule binding both: "Copyright protection shall extend to expressions and not to ideas, procedures, methods of operation or mathematical concepts as such." — [TRIPS Agreement, Article 9(2)](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04_e.htm)
- In the UK the House of Lords (Designers Guild v Russell Williams, 23 Nov 2000) held that copyright protects the expression of an idea and not the idea, which others remain "free to express" in works of their own, and that ideas which are "not original, or so commonplace as not to form a substantial part of the work" are unprotected. — [Wikipedia, Designers Guild v Russell Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Designer_Guild_Ltd_v_Russell_Williams_(Textiles)_Ltd)
- The UK's exceptions are specific and purpose-limited ("fair dealing" for non-commercial research and private study, for criticism, review and quotation, and for illustration in teaching), not an open-ended fair-use test. — [GOV.UK, Exceptions to copyright](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exceptions-to-copyright)

### Inferences
- **Free to use:** the patterns in questions 1 and 2 (five-finger patterns, scales, arpeggios, triad and seventh-chord cycles, inversions, cadences and common progressions in every key, stock accompaniment figures, the notion of playing one hand louder or more detached than the other, a tempo ladder, a daily routine's order). These are common property or methods; describing and implementing them in one's own way takes nothing protected.
- **Not free to copy:** a teacher's written explanation, PDF or sheet as laid out; an exercise that is real new music (for example a composed study with its own melody and harmony, such as the paid PDFs noted in question 1); a video, its audio or its captions. A teacher's chosen fingering and markings on someone else's notes are the sort of thing the Office treats as editorial authorship, so reproducing an edited page wholesale is different from choosing standard fingering oneself.
- **Grey zone and how to stay out of it:** the more an exercise is one teacher's distinctive composition, the more a note-for-note rebuild looks like copying expression rather than using an idea. Generating from a parameterised description (interval pattern, direction, rhythm ratio, key cycle) rather than from the teacher's bars keeps the app on the idea side; so does giving exercises Prima's own names, even though names and short phrases are not copyrightable.
- The terms question and the copyright question are separate: the ideas in a tutorial are free to use, but that does not make it permissible under YouTube's terms to download or scrape the tutorial to get at them.
- Pre-1930 exercise collections that the teachers themselves lean on (Hanon, Czerny, Schmitt) are a clean source of concrete patterns if specific historical exercises are wanted; a modern edition's added fingering and commentary may carry its own rights.

### Gaps
- I did not read the opinions in Skidmore or Sheeran themselves, only law-firm and specialist summaries; the reporter citations (952 F.3d 1051; 120 F.4th 1066; 803 F.3d 1032 for Bikram) and the report that the Supreme Court declined to hear Sheeran in 2025 come from search summaries and were not checked against primary sources.
- I found no case or Copyright Office statement dealing specifically with a piano technique exercise, so where "original musical exercise" ends and "common property pattern" begins is a matter of degree with no bright line in the sources.
- I did not retrieve the Compendium's chapter 300 sections that chapter 800 cross-refers to (§§ 313.4(C) and (D)), nor the EU InfoSoc Directive text itself; the EU position on ideas rests on TRIPS and commentary (a search summary noted that EU law states the idea/expression rule expressly only for software and databases).
- The UK point rests on an encyclopaedia summary of Designers Guild rather than the judgment.
- Public-domain status outside the United States (life plus 70 years in the EU and UK) was not checked work by work.
