# A concert pianist's ten daily exercises, and how they became families in Prima

A study of one lesson the owner likes, and the design that came out of it, done on 23 November 2026. The lesson is
[“I'm a concert pianist. I play these 10 exercises EVERY day.”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8QI_InPpq0s) by Peterson Piano
Academy (11 minutes, published 17 July 2026). The first half says what he plays, as it was seen; the second says what Prima
does with it. [exercises.md](../exercises.md) is the research this sits on: what the evidence says about technique practice,
and what may be taken from a video.

## In short

His ten are scales that part in the middle, arpeggios counted in fours, the thumb passing under, thirds, a wrist bounce on
repeated sixths, scales in octaves, trill bursts, rotation from a held note, one finger holding while four play, and two-note
slurs. What makes the lesson good is less the ten patterns, most of which are old stock, than how he works them: one pair of
fingers after another, alike and in mirror, loud, soft and detached, through every key. So they went into Prima as **ten
families, not ten fixed exercises**: each a main exercise with side ones made by a few choices (the interval, the pair of
fingers, which finger the thumb goes under, the touch), on top of the key, the hands and the tempo every exercise already has.
They sit in the sections Prima had, plus one new one, **Double notes**; and every exercise to play now also says what it
trains (finger strength, the thumb, the wrist and so on), which the list can be filtered by. The words and names are Prima's,
the notes are written by Prima's generators, and each links back to its place in the lesson. Two things could not be read
from the video and are the owner's to confirm by ear: the exact rhythm of the wrist bounce, and how long the trill's stop is.

## How it was read

In a browser, as a viewer does, on 23 November 2026: the page's description, YouTube's own transcript panel, and the picture,
paused and stepped by hand. No downloader, transcript library or script fetched anything, for the reasons in
[exercises.md](../exercises.md#scraping-is-barred-the-ideas-are-free).

- The description is one line and the academy's home page. There is **no written companion or PDF**; the academy's own
  materials are behind an account and were not opened.
- The picture has a strip along the bottom showing all 88 keys from above, **each key lit while it is down**. That is what the
  notes below come from: a lit key was placed by counting white keys from the left end and checked against the groups of
  black keys. His hands show only as fingertips at the strip's edge, so **fingering is from what he says**, not from what is
  seen, except where a lit key and a visible finger agree.
- **No sound could be heard.** Rhythms are from his counting aloud and from stepping the picture a twentieth of a second at a
  time. Where the keys light and go out faster than that, the rhythm is marked as not read.

## What he plays

Each is in my words. “Sure” means seen on the lit keys and said by him; “not read” means what it says.

**1. Scales that part in the middle** (0:28). Sure. Four octaves, the hands an octave apart, left from the C two below
middle C. Up two octaves together; there the hands part, the left going back down two octaves while the right goes on up two,
and come in again; on up two more octaves together; back to the middle; apart and in once more; and down to the start. He
calls it the Russian way (exam boards call the same shape a formula pattern). His point is to never play it the same way
twice: detached, loud, soft, growing and fading. Every key every day, the majors from C up to B and then the minors, the
harmonic minor above all.

**2. Arpeggios counted in fours** (1:51). Sure. Four octaves, hands an octave apart, standard fingering, every major and minor
key. A chord has three notes, so counted in threes the stress always falls on the same one, the right hand's thumb. Counted
in fours it falls on a different note, and so a different finger, each time. For finger strength and for the thumb passing
under.

**3. The thumb passing under** (2:53). Sure. Right hand from middle C: thumb, second finger, thumb on the next note, and back,
round and round as fast as it stays even. Then under the third finger, the fourth, and the fifth (seen: C to G and the thumb
on A). He keeps the wrist as still as he can so that the thumb does the work. Both hands.

**4. Thirds** (3:39). Sure. Two notes at once, a third apart, joined. Three neighbouring thirds up and back as a loop
(seen: C–E, D–F, E–G, D–F), so the right hand's fingers are 1 and 3, 2 and 4, 3 and 5, 2 and 4. Then the same a step higher,
through an octave. Both hands: the same notes an octave apart, or in mirror (seen: the left on E–G while the right is on
C–E), which he finds the easier. How many times he plays a loop before moving up is not clear: he talks over it.

**5. A wrist bounce on repeated sixths** (5:16). Notes sure, **rhythm not read**. For quick repeated notes, chords and
octaves: the wrist makes the movement and the fingers only hold their shape. Sixths in one hand, thumb and little finger
(seen: E with the C above it, then F–D, then G–E, a step up each time), then both hands. Then he puts more quick notes “in the
middle”: three, then more. The picture shows a long gap and then two or three strikes close together, which reads as a few
quick notes bouncing into a longer one, but the two keys do not always light together and there is no sound to check
against.

**6. Scales in octaves** (6:05). Sure. Every major scale in octaves, both hands, up and back, with the same wrist. For the
speed of octaves.

**7. Trill bursts** (6:29). Shape sure, **lengths not read**. A pair of fingers on two neighbouring notes: four notes back
and forth and a stop on the fifth, then the same from the next note up, the hand moving a step, through an octave. At the top
it turns round, starting each burst from the upper note, and comes down a step at a time. Pairs 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, 4–5. Hands
alone and together, where the left takes 5–4 against the right's 1–2 and the two must sound exactly together. As fast as it
stays even. His demonstration is slow and counted, so how quick the four are and how long the stop is can't be told; the stop
is long (the key stays lit about two seconds).

**8. Rotation from a held note** (7:45). Sure. Hold a key with the thumb; without working the next finger, turn the forearm
to play its key three times. Then hold that key and play the next three times, and so on up the hand's five notes, and back
down. Every pair of neighbouring fingers, both hands. For reaching a note from a held one, playing into the keys, and a
singing tone.

**9. One finger holds, four play** (8:53). Sure. A five-finger position. The thumb holds its key while the other four play
theirs up and back (seen: C lit throughout while D, E, F and G go up and back over it); then the second finger holds and the
other four play; and
so on through each finger. Right hand shown. He says some call these useless and he finds them among the most useful.

**10. Two-note slurs** (9:46). Sure. Pairs of joined notes with one pair of fingers: the wrist drops into the first and rises
out of the second, the first stronger. Each pair starts where the last ended (C–D, D–E, E–F …) up an octave; coming down the
pairs turn round (seen: A–G, then G–F). Pairs 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, 4–5. One hand or both (seen an octave apart; the left hand's
fingers then are not visible).

## What Prima already had

Three of the ten were there in some form: the thumb under three and under four fingers (two exercises); two-note slurs, but
in one place with the fingers changing; and scales in contrary motion, one octave. The Scales and Arpeggios drills play both
of his first two in similar motion. Thirds, the wrist bounce, octaves, trill bursts, rotation from a held note and the
held-finger exercise were not there. [exercises.md](../exercises.md) had left octaves and double notes out on purpose, and
called held-finger work the disputed kind; they are in now because the owner asked for this lesson's exercises, and their
words carry what that research found: quietly, briefly, an octave only if it's an easy reach, and stop at the first ache.

## The design

### A family is a main exercise and a few choices

A family is one entry in the catalogue. Its page has the key, the hands and the tempo as every exercise has, and under them
one row for each **variation**: a name and two to six options, one of them the usual. What's chosen is kept with the key and
hands, so the exercise opens as it was last played. The main exercise is every variation at its usual; a side exercise is
any other combination. Anything that takes an exercise and writes its music for a key and hands (the routines Claude builds)
gets the main exercise without knowing it's a family. Its own page plays what's chosen, round the play bar's Loop and
through the keys too.

| His | Prima's family | Section | Choices beyond key, hands and tempo | Trains |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Scales | Scale that parts in the middle | Scales | Size: one octave each way, or two (his four-octave form). Touch: joined, detached. Loudness: as it comes, soft, loud, growing and fading | Hands together, evenness |
| 2 Arpeggios | Arpeggio counted in fours | Broken chords | Count: in fours, in threes. Octaves: two, four | Finger strength, the thumb |
| 3 Thumb under | Thumb under | Thumb under | Under 2, 3, 4 or 5 fingers | The thumb |
| 4 Thirds | Thirds, three up and back | Double notes | Where: in one place, up the scale. Together: the same way, in mirror | Double notes, finger strength |
| 5 Wrist bounce | Bounced double notes | Double notes | Interval: thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths, octaves. Quick notes before the long one: one to four | Double notes, arm and wrist |
| 6 Octave scales | Scale in octaves | Double notes | One octave, two | Double notes, arm and wrist |
| 7 Trills | Trill bursts up the scale | Trills | Fingers: 1 and 2, 2 and 3, 3 and 4, 4 and 5 | Finger strength |
| 8 Rotation | Hold one, turn to the next | Arm weight and rotation | Reach: the next finger, a finger further | Rotation |
| 9 Independence | One finger holds, four play | Five-finger patterns | Holding: each in turn, or one finger | Finger independence |
| 10 Slurs | Two-note slurs up the scale | Touch, loud and soft | Fingers: 1 and 2, 2 and 3, 3 and 4, 4 and 5 | Touch, arm and wrist |

With keys and hands that is about 2,500 different pages of music from ten entries. The two thumb exercises became the one
family (it keeps the older one's place in the catalogue, so what's been played of it is still its own). The slurs in one
place and the three slow trills stay as they were: they are the first step before these.

### Sections, and a focus beside them

The sections were already half by material (Scales, Chords, Broken chords) and half by purpose (Thumb under, Trills, Touch,
Pedal). His exercises fit them, with one gap: nothing held thirds, sixths and octaves, so there is a new section, **Double
notes**, for the three families that are two keys at once in one hand.

What sections can't say is what an exercise is *for* when that cuts across them. An arpeggio counted in fours is a broken
chord, and what it trains is finger strength and the thumb; the owner's own word for a category was “finger strength”, which
is no section and never could be one, since five-finger patterns, trills, thirds and arpeggios all belong to it. So each
exercise to play carries a **focus**, one to three of twelve: finger strength, finger independence, evenness, the thumb, arm
and wrist, rotation, double notes, chords and landing, touch, loud and soft, hands together, pedal. The list can show one
focus at a time, across the sections, and the search finds them by name. A focus is kept beside the catalogue, by exercise,
so adding one doesn't mean editing sixty entries. “Finger strength” is the owner's word and stays the name; its line says
what the research found, that it's control that grows more than muscle.

### How the notes are written

Every family is a few lines over the same parts, which is what lets the next lesson's exercises be added the same way:

- **A cell**: what the hand does in one place (three thirds up and back; four trill notes and a stop; a held key and three
  turned ones).
- **A walk**: where the cell goes (it stays; up the scale a step a bar, for five steps or an octave, and back).
- **The hands**: one alone; together in parallel; together in mirror. A pair of fingers is the pair itself in a hand alone,
  and with both hands the left takes the opposite pair, as he does in the trills.
- **Overlays**: touch (detached), loudness (a marked level, or growing and fading), a lean on every nth note.
- **The staff**: a line that travels changes clef within two ledger lines and goes under an octave line at the extremes, so
  the four-octave forms are readable; a held key under moving ones is a second line on its staff.

Prima judges them as it judges any exercise: notes, timing and held keys always, and where the music marks them the touch,
loud and soft and the accents. Three of his points turn out to be things a keyboard can check and a teacher can only listen
for: the held key staying down in 8 and 9, the lean landing on the marked notes in 2, and the second note of a slur being
let go in 10. What it can't see is the same as ever: the wrist, the forearm's turn and the fingering are the player's to
feel.

### Where Prima's version differs from his, on purpose

- **Size.** His scales and arpeggios are four octaves; the usual here is the short form (one octave each way; two octaves),
  with his as the other option. Short, correct runs are what the evidence supports.
- **The thirds** go up the scale once and stop at the top, a bar on each step; “in one place” is there as the way in.
- **The bounce** is written in three: the quick notes in the first beat, the long one on the second, a beat's rest to move
  in. That is Prima's own rhythm, since his couldn't be read. It goes up through five places and back, not an octave. Fourths
  start from the fifth below the tonic, so that none of the five is the scale's one augmented fourth.
- **Trill bursts** are four sixteenths and a quarter, two bursts to a bar.
- **One finger holds** is a bar to each finger, the moving notes in eighths; one finger alone can be chosen, which he doesn't
  do but which is where the work is (the third and fourth).
- **Hold and turn** has a second reach, a finger further (thumb to third finger and so on), from his “all the combinations”.
- **Keys.** The bounce and the scale in octaves are in the twelve major keys; the others in every major and minor key. The
  thumb passes under only in the keys where it lands on white keys, and where it lands depends on how many fingers it passes
  under: the scale's fourth or fifth note under three or four (ten keys: C, A minor, G, D, E minor, D minor, A, G minor, E,
  C minor), its third or sixth under two or five (six: C, A minor, G, F, E minor, B minor). Changing how many moves the key
  to C if the one showing isn't among the new ones.
- **Round a loop and through the keys** each family goes straight on without its close, the bar of the held note it ends
  on, as every exercise does. Two differ: the scale in octaves has no bar that's only its close (its last octave is held to
  the end of its bar), so all of it goes round; and the bounce's last bar is on the step it started from, which round a loop
  is the next time round's first, so that bar goes with the close.

## For the owner to settle

1. **The bounce's rhythm.** If his is different from Prima's (quick notes, then a long one, then a rest), say what you hear
   at 5:30 to 6:00 and the cell changes; the rest of the family stays.
2. **The trill's lengths**, the same way, from 6:40.
3. **Octaves.** They are marked Later and worded with care. If your hand doesn't take an octave easily, leave them alone;
   the bounce's smaller intervals train the same wrist.
4. **The left hand's fingers in slurs with both hands** are Prima's choice (the opposite pair, as in the trills); he doesn't
   show them.

## Not done

Claude's routines can pick a family but not yet a variation of it, so a routine plays the main exercise. The three slow
trills and the two contrary-motion exercises could become families the same way. The focus list is a first cut, to be tuned
by use.
